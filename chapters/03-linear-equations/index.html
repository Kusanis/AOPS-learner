Chapter 3 · Algebra & Number

Linear Equations

Linear equations are the workhorses of algebra: no squares, no roots, just variables multiplied by constants. In this chapter you'll learn to recognise them, solve one equation or a pair of them, and, most importantly, turn a story told in words into equations you can solve.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • The degree of a term is the sum of its variables' exponents; a linear equation has degree 1.
  • One variable: do the same thing to both sides. Gather variable terms on one side, constants on the other, then divide by the coefficient.
  • Clear fractions first by multiplying through by a common denominator; if the variable was in a denominator, check for excluded values.
  • Two variables: substitution (solve one equation for a variable and plug in) or elimination (scale and add equations to cancel a variable).
  • Getting $0 = 0$ means the equations describe the same line (infinitely many solutions); getting $0 = c \ne 0$ means parallel lines (no solution).
  • Word problems: (1) name the unknowns, (2) write one equation per fact, (3) solve and check.
  • $\text{rate} \times \text{time} = \text{amount}$ covers both distance problems and work problems.

3.1 What is a Linear Equation?

Hook

A taxi charges a flat 3 dollars plus 2 dollars per mile. Ride 1 mile and you pay 5; ride 2 and you pay 7; ride 10 and you pay 23. Every extra mile adds exactly the same amount. Plot cost against distance and the points fall on a perfectly straight line. That steady, same-every-time growth is what "linear" means.

Concept

An equation is any statement with an equals sign. Unknown quantities are variables; fixed numbers are constants. A constant multiplying a variable term is that term's coefficient. In $5x - 2y = 9$, the variables are $x$ and $y$, the coefficients are $5$ and $-2$, and $9$ is the constant term.

The degree of a term is the sum of the exponents of its variables. So $7x$ has degree 1, $x^2$ has degree 2, and $3xy^2$ has degree $1 + 2 = 3$. A constant term has degree 0. The degree of an equation is the highest degree among its terms.

A linear equation has degree 1: every variable term is a constant times a single variable to the first power.

So $x + y + z = 4$ and $\tfrac12 x - \sqrt3\, y = \pi$ are linear (coefficients may be fractions or irrational numbers), while $xy = 6$, $x^2 + y = 1$, $\sqrt{x} + y = 2$ and $\dfrac{3}{x} = 5$ as written are not. Equations like $2^x = 8$, where the variable sits in an exponent, are not linear either.

Always simplify before judging. $(x+1)^2 = x^2 + 5$ looks quadratic, but expanding gives $x^2 + 2x + 1 = x^2 + 5$, and the $x^2$ terms cancel, leaving the linear equation $2x = 4$.

Name the parts

In the equation $4a - 7b + c = 12$, list the variables, the coefficients and the constant term.

  1. Variables: $a$, $b$, $c$.

    These are the unknown letters.

  2. Coefficients: $4$ (of $a$), $-7$ (of $b$), $1$ (of $c$).

    The sign belongs to the coefficient, and a bare variable has an invisible coefficient of $1$.

  3. Constant term: $12$.

    It is the only term with no variable.

Variables $a, b, c$; coefficients $4, -7, 1$; constant $12$.

Degrees of terms

Find the degree of each term: $9x$, $\ -2x^3$, $\ 5x^2y^3$, $\ 8$.

  1. $9x$: degree 1. $\ -2x^3$: degree 3.

    With one variable, the degree is just its exponent; the coefficient doesn't matter.

  2. $5x^2y^3$: degree $2 + 3 = 5$.

    With several variables, add their exponents.

  3. $8$: degree 0.

    A constant has no variables, like $8x^0$.

$1, 3, 5, 0$

Linear or not?

Which are linear? (a) $\tfrac{x}{3} + y = 1$ (b) $\sqrt2\,x - 5y = \pi$ (c) $2xy = 5$ (d) $\dfrac{3}{x} + y = 1$

  1. (a) is linear: it is $\tfrac13 x + 1y = 1$.

    Dividing by a constant is multiplying by the constant $\tfrac13$, so each term still has degree 1.

  2. (b) is linear.

    Coefficients and constants can be any numbers, irrational ones included. Only the variables' powers matter.

  3. (c) is not linear: $xy$ has degree 2.

    A product of two variables counts both exponents.

  4. (d) is not linear: $\dfrac{3}{x} = 3x^{-1}$.

    A variable in a denominator acts like a negative power, not a first power.

(a) and (b) are linear; (c) and (d) are not.

Spot the hidden powers

Which are linear? (a) $x\sqrt{y} = 4$ (b) $3x + 2^y = 7$ (c) $0.2x - y + 4z = w$

  1. (a) is not linear: $x\sqrt{y} = x\,y^{1/2}$ has degree $\tfrac32$.

    Roots are fractional powers, and linear equations can't have them.

  2. (b) is not linear.

    In $2^y$ the variable is in the exponent, so the term isn't a constant times $y$ at all.

  3. (c) is linear, with four variables.

    Every term is a constant times one variable to the first power; moving $w$ to the left gives $0.2x - y + 4z - w = 0$.

Only (c) is linear.

Simplify before you judge

Is $(x - 3)(x + 2) = x^2 + 4$ a linear equation? If so, solve it.

  1. Expand: $x^2 - x - 6 = x^2 + 4$.

    The degree can only be judged once the equation is multiplied out.

  2. Subtract $x^2$ from both sides: $-x - 6 = 4$.

    The squared terms cancel, so the equation really has degree 1.

  3. $-x = 10$, so $x = -10$. Check: $(-13)(-8) = 104 = 100 + 4$. ✓

    Solve the linear equation, then check in the original.

Yes, it is linear; $x = -10$.

Make it linear

For which value of $k$ is $(k - 2)x^2 + 3x + ky = 7$ a linear equation? Write the resulting equation.

  1. The only problem term is $(k-2)x^2$, so we need $k - 2 = 0$.

    The equation is linear exactly when the degree-2 term disappears, which happens when its coefficient is zero.

  2. $k = 2$ gives $0\cdot x^2 + 3x + 2y = 7$, that is, $3x + 2y = 7$.

    Substitute $k = 2$ everywhere, including the coefficient of $y$.

$k = 2$, giving $3x + 2y = 7$.

A term vanishes when its coefficient is zero; that can change an equation's degree.

3.2 One Equation, One Variable

Hook

Picture an old-fashioned balance scale. On the left: three mystery boxes and 5 one-gram weights. On the right: one mystery box and 11 weights. It balances. Take a box off each side and it still balances; take 5 weights off each side and it still balances. Now two boxes weigh 6 grams, so each box weighs 3. You've just solved $3x + 5 = x + 11$.

Concept

An equation is a balance. Whatever you do to one side, do to the other, and it stays true. To solve a linear equation in one variable:

1. Simplify each side (expand brackets, clear fractions).
2. Add or subtract to collect variable terms on one side and constants on the other.
3. Divide both sides by the coefficient of the variable.

$$ax + b = c \quad\Longrightarrow\quad ax = c - b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \frac{c - b}{a}\quad (a \ne 0)$$

Letters can stand for constants too. Solving "for $x$" means treating every other letter as a known number and isolating $x$.

If the variable appears in a denominator, multiply both sides by that denominator to clear it. The new equation may be linear even though the original wasn't. But any value that makes an original denominator zero is excluded, so always check your answer in the original equation.

Sometimes the variable cancels completely. If what's left is always true (like $6 = 6$), every number is a solution. If it's always false (like $6 = 5$), there is no solution.

Two moves

Solve $5x - 7 = 18$.

  1. Add $7$ to both sides: $5x = 25$.

    Undo the subtraction so the variable term stands alone.

  2. Divide both sides by $5$: $x = 5$.

    Undo the multiplication by the coefficient.

  3. Check: $5(5) - 7 = 18$. ✓

    Substituting back catches arithmetic slips.

$x = 5$

Variables on both sides

Solve $4(x - 3) = 2x + 6$.

  1. Expand: $4x - 12 = 2x + 6$.

    Simplify each side before moving terms.

  2. Subtract $2x$: $2x - 12 = 6$. Add $12$: $2x = 18$.

    Collect the $x$ terms on one side and the constants on the other.

  3. $x = 9$. Check: $4(6) = 24 = 18 + 6$. ✓

    Divide by the coefficient and verify.

$x = 9$

Clear the fractions

Solve $\dfrac{x}{3} + \dfrac{x}{4} = 14$.

  1. Multiply every term by $12$: $4x + 3x = 168$.

    $12$ is the least common multiple of the denominators, so every fraction becomes a whole number.

  2. $7x = 168$, so $x = 24$.

    Combine like terms and divide.

  3. Check: $8 + 6 = 14$. ✓

    $24/3 = 8$ and $24/4 = 6$.

$x = 24$

Solve a formula for a letter

The perimeter of a rectangle is $P = 2L + 2W$. Solve for $W$.

  1. Subtract $2L$: $P - 2L = 2W$.

    Treat $P$ and $L$ as known numbers and isolate the $W$ term.

  2. Divide by $2$: $W = \dfrac{P - 2L}{2}$.

    Divide by $W$'s coefficient, exactly as with numbers.

$W = \dfrac{P - 2L}{2}$

Compare with the book's Example 3-3.

Variable in the denominator

Solve $\dfrac{2x + 3}{x - 1} = 5$.

  1. Note $x \ne 1$, then multiply both sides by $x - 1$: $2x + 3 = 5x - 5$.

    Clearing the denominator turns the equation into a linear one. Record the excluded value first.

  2. $8 = 3x$, so $x = \dfrac{8}{3}$.

    Move $2x$ right and $-5$ left, then divide by $3$.

  3. Check: $\dfrac{16/3 + 9/3}{5/3} = \dfrac{25/3}{5/3} = 5$. ✓

    $x = \tfrac83$ is not the excluded value $1$, and it satisfies the original equation.

$x = \dfrac{8}{3}$

All numbers, or none

Solve (a) $3(x + 2) - x = 2(x + 3)$ and (b) $3(x + 2) - x = 2x + 5$.

  1. (a) Left side: $2x + 6$. Right side: $2x + 6$. So $2x + 6 = 2x + 6$.

    Simplify both sides first.

  2. Subtracting $2x$ leaves $6 = 6$, which is always true.

    The variable vanished and a true statement remained, so every real $x$ works. This is an identity.

  3. (b) $2x + 6 = 2x + 5$ leaves $6 = 5$, which is never true.

    No value of $x$ can make a false statement true, so there is no solution.

(a) every real number; (b) no solution.

The general case

Solve $ax + b = cx + d$ for $x$. When does it have exactly one solution?

  1. Subtract $cx$ and $b$: $ax - cx = d - b$.

    Collect the $x$ terms on the left and the constants on the right.

  2. Factor: $(a - c)x = d - b$.

    Factoring out $x$ shows its single combined coefficient.

  3. If $a \ne c$: $x = \dfrac{d - b}{a - c}$.

    Division is allowed only by a nonzero number. If $a = c$, we are in the previous example's situation: all numbers (if $b = d$) or none (if $b \ne d$).

$x = \dfrac{d-b}{a-c}$, exactly one solution when $a \ne c$.

Fractions on both sides

Solve $\dfrac{x + 2}{x - 3} = \dfrac{x - 1}{x - 4}$.

  1. Excluded values: $x \ne 3$, $x \ne 4$. Cross-multiply: $(x + 2)(x - 4) = (x - 1)(x - 3)$.

    Multiplying both sides by $(x-3)(x-4)$ clears both denominators at once.

  2. $x^2 - 2x - 8 = x^2 - 4x + 3$.

    Expand both products. The $x^2$ terms will cancel, so the equation is actually linear.

  3. $2x = 11$, so $x = \dfrac{11}{2}$.

    Subtract $x^2$, add $4x$ and $8$ to both sides.

  4. Check: $\dfrac{7.5}{2.5} = 3$ and $\dfrac{4.5}{1.5} = 3$. ✓

    The answer isn't an excluded value, and both sides agree.

$x = \dfrac{11}{2}$

An answer that isn't allowed

Solve $\dfrac{x}{x - 2} + 3 = \dfrac{2}{x - 2}$.

  1. Note $x \ne 2$. Multiply every term by $x - 2$: $x + 3(x - 2) = 2$.

    Remember to multiply the $3$ as well; it is a term of its own.

  2. $4x - 6 = 2$, so $x = 2$.

    Solve the linear equation as usual.

  3. But $x = 2$ makes the denominators zero, so it is not a solution. The equation has no solution.

    Multiplying by $x - 2$ is only valid when $x - 2 \ne 0$. The value it produced is extraneous: it solves the cleared equation but not the original.

No solution.

After clearing a variable denominator, always check the answer against the excluded values.

3.3 Two Equations, Two Variables

Hook

"Two numbers add to 10." That's not enough: 3 and 7 work, but so do 4 and 6, and 2.5 and 7.5. "Their difference is 4" pins them down: only 7 and 3 satisfy both. Each clue on its own allows infinitely many answers, a whole line of them. Two clues together usually leave just one, where the lines cross.

Concept

A solution of an equation in $x$ and $y$ is an ordered pair $(x, y)$. A linear equation like $x + y = 10$ has infinitely many, and they form a line when plotted. A system of two equations asks for the pairs that satisfy both: the intersection of two lines.

Substitution. Solve one equation for one variable ("$y = $ something without $y$"), then substitute into the other equation. You get a one-variable equation.

Elimination. Multiply the equations by constants so that one variable has opposite coefficients, then add. For $a_1x + b_1y = c_1$ and $a_2x + b_2y = c_2$, multiplying the first by $a_2$ and the second by $-a_1$ makes the $x$ terms cancel.

$$\begin{aligned} x + y &= 10 \\ x - y &= 4 \end{aligned} \quad\xrightarrow{\text{add}}\quad 2x = 14 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 7,\ y = 3.$$

Two lines can meet in one point, never meet (parallel), or be the same line. If elimination wipes out both variables and leaves a false statement like $0 = 3$, the lines are parallel and there is no solution. If it leaves $0 = 0$, the equations are multiples of each other and every point on the line is a solution.

"Linear" is about the unknowns you choose. If $\tfrac1x$ and $\tfrac1y$ appear (or $\sqrt x$ and $\sqrt y$), treat those as the unknowns, solve the linear system, then convert back.

Add to eliminate

Solve $x + y = 12$ and $x - y = 2$.

  1. Add the equations: $2x = 14$, so $x = 7$.

    The $y$ terms have opposite coefficients, so adding cancels them.

  2. Substitute: $7 + y = 12$, so $y = 5$.

    Either original equation gives $y$ once $x$ is known.

  3. Check: $7 - 5 = 2$. ✓

    Test the equation you didn't use.

$(x, y) = (7, 5)$

Substitute an expression

Solve $y = 2x - 1$ and $3x + y = 14$.

  1. Replace $y$ in the second equation: $3x + (2x - 1) = 14$.

    The first equation already says what $y$ equals, which is perfect for substitution.

  2. $5x = 15$, so $x = 3$, and $y = 2(3) - 1 = 5$.

    Solve the one-variable equation, then return to $y = 2x - 1$.

$(x, y) = (3, 5)$

Scale, then eliminate

Solve $3x + 2y = 16$ and $5x - 3y = -5$.

  1. Multiply the first by $3$ and the second by $2$: $9x + 6y = 48$ and $10x - 6y = -10$.

    Choose multipliers that give the $y$ terms opposite coefficients, $+6y$ and $-6y$.

  2. Add: $19x = 38$, so $x = 2$.

    The $y$ terms cancel.

  3. $3(2) + 2y = 16$ gives $y = 5$. Check: $5(2) - 3(5) = -5$. ✓

    Back-substitute, then check in the other equation.

$(x, y) = (2, 5)$

Compare with the book's Example 3-5.

Line the variables up first

Solve $4x = 3y + 1$ and $2y = x + 6$.

  1. Rewrite: $4x - 3y = 1$ and $-x + 2y = 6$.

    Putting $x$ terms, $y$ terms and constants in matching columns makes the structure clear.

  2. From the second: $x = 2y - 6$. Substitute: $4(2y - 6) - 3y = 1$.

    $x$ has coefficient $-1$ in the second equation, so solving for $x$ avoids fractions.

  3. $5y - 24 = 1$, so $y = 5$ and $x = 2(5) - 6 = 4$.

    Solve, then back-substitute.

  4. Check: $4(4) = 16 = 3(5) + 1$ and $2(5) = 10 = 4 + 6$. ✓

    Check in the original equations, not the rearranged ones.

$(x, y) = (4, 5)$

Same line or parallel?

Solve (a) $6x - 4y = 10$, $3x - 2y = 5$ and (b) $6x - 4y = 10$, $3x - 2y = 4$.

  1. (a) Double the second: $6x - 4y = 10$. Subtracting from the first gives $0 = 0$.

    The first equation is exactly twice the second, so both describe the same line.

  2. So every pair with $3x - 2y = 5$ works, for example $(1, -1)$ and $(3, 2)$.

    Infinitely many solutions: one for every point on the line.

  3. (b) Double the second: $6x - 4y = 8$. Subtracting gives $0 = 2$.

    The left sides match but the right sides don't, so the lines are parallel and never meet: no solution.

(a) infinitely many solutions; (b) no solution.

A system with fractions

Solve $\dfrac{x}{2} + \dfrac{y}{3} = 4$ and $\dfrac{x}{4} - \dfrac{y}{6} = 0$.

  1. Multiply the first by $6$ and the second by $12$: $3x + 2y = 24$ and $3x - 2y = 0$.

    Clear each equation's fractions with its own common denominator.

  2. Add: $6x = 24$, so $x = 4$; then $3(4) - 2y = 0$ gives $y = 6$.

    The $y$ coefficients are already opposites.

  3. Check: $2 + 2 = 4$ and $1 - 1 = 0$. ✓

    Verify in the original fractional equations.

$(x, y) = (4, 6)$

Choose k for no solution

For what value of $k$ does the system $2x + ky = 5$, $\ 4x + 6y = 7$ have no solution?

  1. For no solution, the left sides must be proportional but the right sides not.

    Parallel lines: elimination must cancel both variables and leave a false statement.

  2. The $x$ coefficients have ratio $4 : 2 = 2$, so we need $6 = 2k$, giving $k = 3$.

    Doubling the first equation must reproduce the second equation's left side.

  3. With $k = 3$: doubling gives $4x + 6y = 10$, but the second says $4x + 6y = 7$. Contradiction. ✓

    Since $10 \ne 7$, the lines are parallel and distinct.

$k = 3$

Linear in disguise

Solve $\dfrac{2}{x} + \dfrac{3}{y} = 2$ and $\dfrac{4}{x} - \dfrac{3}{y} = 1$.

  1. Let $u = \dfrac1x$ and $v = \dfrac1y$: $2u + 3v = 2$ and $4u - 3v = 1$.

    The equations are linear in the reciprocals, so rename them.

  2. Add: $6u = 3$, so $u = \dfrac12$; then $2\cdot\tfrac12 + 3v = 2$ gives $v = \dfrac13$.

    Ordinary elimination on the new unknowns.

  3. $x = \dfrac1u = 2$ and $y = \dfrac1v = 3$. Check: $1 + 1 = 2$ and $2 - 1 = 1$. ✓

    Convert back to the original variables.

$(x, y) = (2, 3)$

Pick the unknowns that make the system linear.

Compare with the book's Example 3-9.

Three unknowns, added cleverly

Solve $x + y = 11$, $\ y + z = 15$, $\ x + z = 12$.

  1. Add all three: $2x + 2y + 2z = 38$, so $x + y + z = 19$.

    Because of the symmetry, the sum of everything is easy to find.

  2. $z = 19 - (x + y) = 19 - 11 = 8$, $\ x = 19 - 15 = 4$, $\ y = 19 - 12 = 7$.

    Subtract each original equation from the total to isolate the missing variable.

  3. Check: $4 + 7 = 11$, $7 + 8 = 15$, $4 + 8 = 12$. ✓

    All three equations hold.

$(x, y, z) = (4, 7, 8)$

Adding all the equations can reveal a useful total.

3.4 Word Problems

Hook

You drive to a friend's house at 60 mph and, stuck in traffic, come back at 30 mph. Your average speed must be 45 mph, right? It isn't: it's 40. You spend twice as long on the slow leg, so the slow speed counts for more. Word problems reward people who translate carefully instead of guessing.

Concept

A word problem is a system of equations written in sentences. Solve it in three stages:

1. Define variables for the unknowns (write down what each letter means, with units).
2. Write equations: usually one per fact in the problem.
3. Solve the equations, then check the answer against the original words.

A few patterns come up again and again:

  • Digits. A two-digit number with tens digit $t$ and units digit $u$ has value $10t + u$; reversed, it is $10u + t$.
  • Motion. $\text{rate} \times \text{time} = \text{distance}$. Average speed is total distance ÷ total time, not the average of the speeds. With a current of speed $c$, a boat of speed $b$ goes $b + c$ downstream and $b - c$ upstream.
  • Work. If a job takes someone $T$ hours, they do $\tfrac1T$ of it per hour. Rates add when people work together: $(\text{rate})(\text{time}) = \text{fraction of the job done}$.

Ages now and later

Maria is three times as old as her brother. In 6 years she will be twice as old as he will be. How old is each now?

  1. Let $M$ and $B$ be their ages now. Then $M = 3B$ and $M + 6 = 2(B + 6)$.

    One equation per sentence. In 6 years both ages increase by 6.

  2. Substitute: $3B + 6 = 2B + 12$, so $B = 6$ and $M = 18$.

    The first equation gives $M$ directly, so substitution is quickest.

  3. Check: in 6 years, $24 = 2 \cdot 12$. ✓

    Re-read the problem with the answer in hand.

Maria is 18 and her brother is 6.

Consecutive integers

Three consecutive integers add to $87$. Find them.

  1. Call the middle one $n$; the three are $n - 1$, $n$, $n + 1$.

    Centering on the middle number makes the extras cancel.

  2. $(n - 1) + n + (n + 1) = 3n = 87$, so $n = 29$.

    The $-1$ and $+1$ cancel.

$28, 29, 30$

Counting coins

A jar holds 30 coins, all quarters and dimes, worth 5.10 dollars in total. How many of each?

  1. Let $q$ be the number of quarters and $d$ the number of dimes. Then $q + d = 30$.

    The first fact counts coins.

  2. In cents: $25q + 10d = 510$.

    The second fact counts value. Working in cents avoids decimals.

  3. Substitute $d = 30 - q$: $25q + 300 - 10q = 510$, so $15q = 210$ and $q = 14$, $d = 16$.

    Solve the system by substitution.

  4. Check: $350 + 160 = 510$ cents. ✓

    Confirm the total value.

14 quarters and 16 dimes.

Reversing the digits

The digits of a two-digit number add to 11. Reversing the digits makes the number 27 larger. Find the number.

  1. Let the tens digit be $t$ and the units digit $u$. Then $t + u = 11$.

    The number itself is $10t + u$, but the first fact is about the digits.

  2. $(10u + t) - (10t + u) = 27$ simplifies to $9u - 9t = 27$, so $u - t = 3$.

    Reversing swaps the place values.

  3. Adding $t + u = 11$ and $u - t = 3$: $2u = 14$, so $u = 7$, $t = 4$.

    Elimination by adding.

  4. Check: $74 - 47 = 27$. ✓

    Verify the reversal condition.

$47$

Trains approaching

Two trains start 360 miles apart and travel toward each other at 50 mph and 70 mph. How long until they meet?

  1. Let $t$ be the time in hours. The trains cover $50t$ and $70t$ miles.

    Rate times time gives each train's distance.

  2. They meet when $50t + 70t = 360$, so $120t = 360$ and $t = 3$.

    Together they close the whole gap. Equivalently, the gap shrinks at the combined rate of 120 mph.

3 hours

Average speed is not the average of speeds

A car climbs a mountain road at 30 mph and comes back down the same road at 60 mph. What is its average speed for the round trip?

  1. Let the road be $d$ miles long. The climb takes $\dfrac{d}{30}$ hours and the descent $\dfrac{d}{60}$ hours.

    The distance isn't given, so name it; we expect it to cancel.

  2. Total time: $\dfrac{d}{30} + \dfrac{d}{60} = \dfrac{2d + d}{60} = \dfrac{d}{20}$ hours.

    Add the times using the common denominator $60$.

  3. Average speed $= \dfrac{2d}{d/20} = 40$ mph.

    The round trip is $2d$ miles; dividing, $d$ cancels, so the answer doesn't depend on the road's length.

40 mph (not 45)

Average speed = total distance ÷ total time. The slower leg takes longer, so it weighs more.

With and against the current

A boat travels 36 miles downstream in 2 hours and returns upstream in 3 hours. Find the boat's speed in still water and the speed of the current.

  1. Let $b$ be the boat's still-water speed and $c$ the current's speed, in mph.

    Two unknowns, and the problem gives two trips: two equations.

  2. Downstream: $(b + c)(2) = 36$, so $b + c = 18$. Upstream: $(b - c)(3) = 36$, so $b - c = 12$.

    The current adds to the boat's speed going downstream and subtracts going upstream.

  3. Add: $2b = 30$, so $b = 15$ and $c = 3$.

    Elimination by adding.

Boat 15 mph, current 3 mph.

Two hoses together

Hose A fills a tank in 6 hours and hose B fills it in 4 hours. How long do they take together?

  1. A fills $\tfrac16$ of the tank per hour; B fills $\tfrac14$.

    If a job takes $T$ hours, the rate is $\tfrac1T$ of the job per hour.

  2. Together: $\tfrac16 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac{2}{12} + \tfrac{3}{12} = \tfrac{5}{12}$ of the tank per hour.

    Rates add when both work at the same time. (Times do not add.)

  3. $\tfrac{5}{12}t = 1$ gives $t = \tfrac{12}{5} = 2.4$ hours.

    Rate times time equals one whole tank.

$\tfrac{12}{5}$ hours, which is 2 hours 24 minutes.

A late start

Lena can paint a room in 6 hours; Omar can paint it in 9 hours. Lena paints alone for 2 hours, then Omar joins her. How long do they work together?

  1. In 2 hours Lena paints $2 \cdot \tfrac16 = \tfrac13$ of the room, leaving $\tfrac23$.

    Work done = rate × time, for the solo stretch.

  2. Together they paint $\tfrac16 + \tfrac19 = \tfrac{3}{18} + \tfrac{2}{18} = \tfrac{5}{18}$ per hour.

    Add their rates.

  3. $\tfrac{5}{18}t = \tfrac23$, so $t = \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac{18}{5} = \tfrac{12}{5}$ hours.

    Together they only need to finish the remaining $\tfrac23$, not the whole room.

$\tfrac{12}{5}$ hours (2 h 24 min), so 4.4 hours from the start.

Three workers in pairs

A and B together finish a job in 6 days, B and C in 10 days, and A and C in 7.5 days. How long would all three take working together?

  1. Let $a$, $b$, $c$ be their daily rates (jobs per day). Then $a + b = \tfrac16$, $\ b + c = \tfrac1{10}$, $\ a + c = \tfrac{1}{7.5} = \tfrac{2}{15}$.

    Use rates, not times, as the unknowns: rates add, so the equations are linear.

  2. Add all three: $2(a + b + c) = \tfrac{5}{30} + \tfrac{3}{30} + \tfrac{4}{30} = \tfrac{12}{30} = \tfrac25$.

    The same symmetric trick as the three-unknown system in §3.3.

  3. $a + b + c = \tfrac15$ job per day, so together they take $5$ days.

    Time = 1 job ÷ combined rate.

5 days

In work problems, make the rates the unknowns; then everything is linear.
