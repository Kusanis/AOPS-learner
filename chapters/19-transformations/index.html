Chapter 19 · Geometry

Shifts, Turns, Flips, Stretches, and Squeezes

A transformation is a rule that moves every point of the plane somewhere new. Slide a shape, spin it, flip it in a mirror, scale it, or stretch it like cellophane. In this chapter you'll describe each move with coordinates, learn what each one keeps the same, and use those unchanging features to solve problems and write proofs.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • Translation $a$ right and $b$ up: $(x, y) \to (x + a,\ y + b)$.
  • Rotations about the origin: $90^\circ$ counterclockwise $(x,y) \to (-y, x)$; $180^\circ$ $(x,y)\to(-x,-y)$; $90^\circ$ clockwise $(x,y) \to (y,-x)$. About another center, subtract the center, rotate, add it back.
  • Reflections: over the $x$-axis $(x,-y)$; $y$-axis $(-x,y)$; $y = x$ $(y,x)$; through a point $(p,q)$: $(2p - x,\ 2q - y)$. The mirror is the perpendicular bisector of each point and its image.
  • Stretch (distortion) by $k$ in one direction multiplies every area by $k$; it keeps lines and ratios along a line, but not angles.
  • Dilation with center $O$ and ratio $k$: $OA' = k \cdot OA$ on ray $OA$. Lengths scale by $k$, areas by $k^2$, angles stay the same.
  • Translations, rotations and reflections preserve distance, angle and area; reflections reverse orientation.
  • Two reflections make a rotation (crossing mirrors, twice the angle) or a translation (parallel mirrors, twice the gap).

19.1 Translation

Hook

Slide a cup across a table without twisting it. Every point of the cup, handle, rim and all, moves the same distance in the same direction. That single arrow, "this far, this way," describes the whole move.

Concept

A transformation moves each point $P$ to a point $P'$, its image; we say the transformation maps $P$ to $P'$. A translation slides every point by the same amount in the same direction, with no turning and no resizing, so a figure and its image are congruent and point the same way.

In coordinates, sliding $a$ units right and $b$ units up adds $a$ to every $x$-coordinate and $b$ to every $y$-coordinate (negative $a$ or $b$ means left or down):

$$(x, y) \longrightarrow (x + a,\ y + b)$$

Every segment from a point to its image is the same arrow, $\langle a, b\rangle$. Doing one translation after another just adds the arrows.

Writing a translation rule

Write the rule for the translation $4$ units right and $2$ units down, and find the image of $(-1, 5)$.

  1. Right $4$ adds $4$ to $x$; down $2$ subtracts $2$ from $y$: $(x, y) \to (x + 4,\ y - 2)$.

    Horizontal moves change only $x$; vertical moves change only $y$.

  2. $(-1, 5) \to (-1 + 4,\ 5 - 2) = (3, 3)$.

    Apply the rule.

$(x, y) \to (x + 4, y - 2)$; the image is $(3, 3)$.

Finding the translation

A translation maps $(-2, 7)$ to $(3, 1)$. What is its rule?

  1. Change in $x$: $3 - (-2) = 5$. Change in $y$: $1 - 7 = -6$.

    Subtract the original from the image.

  2. Rule: $(x, y) \to (x + 5,\ y - 6)$.

    One point and its image determine a translation completely.

$(x, y) \to (x + 5, y - 6)$

Translating a triangle

Triangle $ABC$ has $A(0,0)$, $B(4,0)$, $C(1,3)$. Find its image under $(x,y) \to (x - 2, y + 5)$ and check that side $AB$ keeps its length.

  1. $A' = (-2, 5)$, $B' = (2, 5)$, $C' = (-1, 8)$.

    Apply the rule to each vertex; the image triangle is the triangle on the images.

  2. $AB = 4$ and $A'B' = 2 - (-2) = 4$.

    Both segments are horizontal, so their lengths are differences of $x$-coordinates.

$A'(-2,5)$, $B'(2,5)$, $C'(-1,8)$; $A'B' = AB = 4$.

One slide after another

Apply $(x,y) \to (x+3, y-1)$ and then $(x,y) \to (x-5, y+4)$. What single translation does the same thing? Does the order matter?

  1. After the first: $(x+3, y-1)$. After the second: $(x + 3 - 5,\ y - 1 + 4) = (x - 2,\ y + 3)$.

    Feed the output of the first rule into the second.

  2. In the other order: $(x - 5 + 3,\ y + 4 - 1) = (x - 2, y + 3)$, the same.

    Adding numbers doesn't depend on order.

$(x, y) \to (x - 2, y + 3)$, in either order.

Sliding a parabola

The parabola $y = x^2$ is translated $3$ units right and $2$ units up. Find the equation of the image.

  1. A point $(X, Y)$ is on the image exactly when $(X - 3,\ Y - 2)$ is on the original.

    Undo the translation to get back to the original curve.

  2. So $Y - 2 = (X - 3)^2$, that is, $y = (x - 3)^2 + 2$.

    Substitute into $y = x^2$.

  3. Check: the vertex moved from $(0,0)$ to $(3, 2)$. ✓

    The lowest point should slide like every other point.

$y = (x - 3)^2 + 2$

To translate a graph $a$ right and $b$ up, replace $x$ with $x - a$ and $y$ with $y - b$.

Completing a parallelogram

$A(1, 2)$, $B(4, 3)$ and $C(6, 7)$ are three vertices of parallelogram $ABCD$. Find $D$.

  1. In parallelogram $ABCD$, side $AD$ is side $BC$ slid over: the translation taking $B$ to $A$ takes $C$ to $D$.

    Opposite sides are parallel and equal, so one is a translation of the other.

  2. $B \to A$ is $(x, y) \to (x - 3,\ y - 1)$, so $D = (6 - 3,\ 7 - 1) = (3, 6)$.

    Apply the same translation to $C$.

  3. Check: $\overrightarrow{AD} = \langle 2, 4\rangle = \overrightarrow{BC}$. ✓

    Both arrows match.

$D = (3, 6)$

Where to build the bridge

Town $A$ is at $(0, 4)$ and town $B$ is at $(8, -6)$. A river runs between the lines $y = 0$ and $y = -2$, and a bridge must cross it straight up and down. Where should the bridge go to make the trip from $A$ to $B$ as short as possible, and how long is the trip?

  1. The bridge length $2$ is fixed. Translate $B$ up by $2$ to $B'(8, -4)$: the walk from the bridge's bottom to $B$ is a translated copy of a walk from the bridge's top to $B'$.

    Translation preserves length, so we can "close up" the river.

  2. Now we need the shortest path from $A$ to $B'$ touching the bank $y = 0$: a straight line. The line from $(0,4)$ to $(8,-4)$ has slope $-1$ and crosses $y = 0$ at $x = 4$.

    The shortest path between two points is a segment.

  3. Build the bridge from $(4, 0)$ to $(4, -2)$. Trip length $= AB' + 2 = \sqrt{8^2 + 8^2} + 2 = 8\sqrt2 + 2$.

    Check: $A$ to $(4,0)$ is $4\sqrt2$, and $(4,-2)$ to $B$ is $4\sqrt2$.

Bridge at $x = 4$; trip length $8\sqrt2 + 2 \approx 13.3$.

Translating part of a path can turn a bent path into a straight one.

19.2 Rotation

Hook

Pin a sheet of paper to a board with one thumbtack and spin it. The tack doesn't move at all, and every other point travels around a circle centered at the tack, all turning through the same angle.

Concept

A rotation has a fixed center $O$, an angle $\theta$ and a direction (counterclockwise unless stated otherwise). It maps each point $P$ to the point $P'$ with $OP' = OP$ and $\angle POP' = \theta$. Rotations keep lengths and angles, so figures map to congruent figures.

$$90^\circ \text{ ccw: } (x,y) \to (-y,\ x) \qquad 90^\circ \text{ cw: } (x,y) \to (y,\ -x)$$ $$180^\circ: (x,y) \to (-x,\ -y)$$

These rules are for the origin as center. For any other center $(p, q)$: subtract the center, rotate, then add the center back.

A figure has rotational symmetry if some rotation (other than a full turn) maps it onto itself. A regular $n$-gon is mapped to itself by rotations about its center through multiples of $\tfrac{360^\circ}{n}$.

A quarter turn

Rotate $(3, 1)$ by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise about the origin.

  1. Use $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$: $(3, 1) \to (-1, 3)$.

    A quarter turn counterclockwise sends the positive $x$-axis to the positive $y$-axis and the positive $y$-axis to the negative $x$-axis.

  2. Check: both points are $\sqrt{10}$ from the origin, and $(3,1)\cdot(-1,3) = -3 + 3 = 0$, so the angle is $90^\circ$.

    Same distance from the center and a right angle at the center.

$(-1, 3)$

A half turn

Rotate $(4, -2)$ by $180^\circ$ about the origin.

  1. $(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$ gives $(-4, 2)$.

    A half turn sends each point straight through the center to the other side.

$(-4, 2)$

Clockwise

Rotate $(2, 5)$ by $90^\circ$ clockwise about the origin.

  1. $90^\circ$ clockwise is the same as $270^\circ$ counterclockwise: apply $(x,y)\to(-y,x)$ three times, or use $(x, y) \to (y, -x)$ directly.

    Three quarter turns one way equal one quarter turn the other way.

  2. $(2, 5) \to (5, -2)$.

    Apply the rule.

$(5, -2)$

A center that isn't the origin

Rotate $P(5, 2)$ by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise about $C(2, 1)$.

  1. Position of $P$ relative to $C$: $(5 - 2,\ 2 - 1) = (3, 1)$.

    Move the problem so the center is at the origin.

  2. Rotate: $(3, 1) \to (-1, 3)$.

    Now the origin rule applies.

  3. Add $C$ back: $(-1 + 2,\ 3 + 1) = (1, 4)$.

    Move everything back.

$(1, 4)$

Symmetry of an octagon

How many rotations about its center with angle from $0^\circ$ up to (but not including) $360^\circ$ map a regular octagon onto itself? What is the smallest positive one?

  1. The rotation must carry one vertex to some vertex; there are $8$ choices, and each choice fixes the rotation.

    Once one vertex lands, the rest follow in order.

  2. The center angles between neighbouring vertices are $\tfrac{360^\circ}{8} = 45^\circ$, so the angles are $0^\circ, 45^\circ, 90^\circ, \dots, 315^\circ$.

    Lines from the center to the vertices cut $360^\circ$ into $8$ equal parts.

$8$ rotations; the smallest positive is $45^\circ$.

Building an equilateral triangle

$A = (0, 0)$ and $B = (4, 0)$. Find a point $C$ above the $x$-axis so that triangle $ABC$ is equilateral.

  1. In an equilateral triangle, $AC = AB$ and $\angle BAC = 60^\circ$. So $C$ is the image of $B$ under a $60^\circ$ counterclockwise rotation about $A$.

    That is exactly the definition of a rotation.

  2. $B$ is $4$ units from $A$ at angle $0^\circ$, so $C$ is $4$ units from $A$ at angle $60^\circ$: $C = (4\cos 60^\circ,\ 4\sin 60^\circ) = (2,\ 2\sqrt3)$.

    Equivalently, the $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle with hypotenuse $4$ has legs $2$ and $2\sqrt3$.

$C = (2, 2\sqrt3)$

Finding the center of rotation

A rotation maps $A(2,0)$ to $A'(5,3)$ and $B(4,0)$ to $B'(5,5)$. Find its center and angle.

  1. The center $O$ has $OA = OA'$, so it lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AA'$. Midpoint $(3.5, 1.5)$, direction $\langle 3, 3\rangle$: the bisector is $x + y = 5$.

    Points equidistant from two points form the perpendicular bisector.

  2. Likewise for $BB'$: midpoint $(4.5, 2.5)$, direction $\langle 1, 5\rangle$: the bisector is $x + 5y = 4.5 + 12.5 = 17$.

    The line through the midpoint perpendicular to $\langle 1,5\rangle$.

  3. Solve: $x = 5 - y$, so $5 + 4y = 17$, $y = 3$, $x = 2$. Center $O(2, 3)$.

    The center lies on both bisectors.

  4. Relative to $O$: $A \to (0, -3)$ and $A' \to (3, 0)$. Since $(x,y)\to(-y,x)$ sends $(0,-3)$ to $(3,0)$, the angle is $90^\circ$ counterclockwise. Check $B$: $(2,-3) \to (3, 2)$, giving $(5,5)$. ✓

    Confirm with the second point.

Center $(2, 3)$, angle $90^\circ$ counterclockwise.

The center of a rotation lies on the perpendicular bisector of every segment from a point to its image.

19.3 Reflection

Hook

Fold a sheet of paper with a fresh ink blot on one half and press. The blot prints a perfect mirror image on the other half: every dot lands just as far from the crease, straight across it.

Concept

A reflection in a line $\ell$ (the mirror) maps each point $P$ to the point $P'$ so that $\ell$ is the perpendicular bisector of $PP'$: $P'$ is as far behind the mirror as $P$ is in front, straight across. Points on $\ell$ stay put. Reflections keep lengths and angles but reverse orientation: a clockwise-labeled triangle becomes counterclockwise.

$$x\text{-axis: } (x, y)\to(x, -y) \qquad y\text{-axis: } (x,y)\to(-x, y)$$ $$y = x: (x,y)\to(y, x)$$

A reflection through a point $O$ sends $P$ to the point $P'$ with $O$ the midpoint of $PP'$. Through the point $(p, q)$, the rule is $(x, y) \to$ $(2p - x,\ 2q - y)$. It is the same as a $180^\circ$ rotation about $O$.

If a reflection maps a figure onto itself, the mirror is a line of symmetry; if a point reflection does, $O$ is a point of symmetry.

Over the axes

Reflect $(3, -2)$ over the $x$-axis and over the $y$-axis.

  1. Over the $x$-axis: $(3, 2)$.

    The mirror is horizontal, so only the up-down coordinate flips sign.

  2. Over the $y$-axis: $(-3, -2)$.

    A vertical mirror flips the left-right coordinate.

$(3, 2)$ and $(-3, -2)$

Over the line $y = x$

Reflect $(-1, 4)$ over the line $y = x$.

  1. $(x, y) \to (y, x)$: $(-1, 4) \to (4, -1)$.

    The midpoint $(1.5, 1.5)$ is on $y = x$, and the segment has slope $-1$, perpendicular to the mirror.

$(4, -1)$

Reflecting through a point

Reflect $P(5, 1)$ through the point $O(2, 3)$.

  1. From $P$ to $O$ is $\langle -3, 2\rangle$. Go the same again from $O$: $P' = (2 - 3,\ 3 + 2) = (-1, 5)$.

    $O$ must be the midpoint of $PP'$.

  2. Check with the formula: $(2\cdot2 - 5,\ 2\cdot3 - 1) = (-1, 5)$. ✓

    $P' = 2O - P$ says the same thing.

$(-1, 5)$

A vertical mirror off the axis

Reflect $(7, 2)$ over the line $x = 3$. Then give a rule for this reflection.

  1. $(7, 2)$ is $4$ units right of the mirror, so its image is $4$ units left: $(-1, 2)$.

    Equal distances on opposite sides, straight across.

  2. In general, the mirror $x = 3$ is the midpoint of $x$ and $x'$: $\tfrac{x + x'}{2} = 3$, so $(x, y) \to (6 - x,\ y)$.

    The $y$-coordinate doesn't change for a vertical mirror.

$(-1, 2)$; rule $(x, y) \to (6 - x, y)$.

Counting lines of symmetry

How many lines of symmetry do these have: a regular pentagon, a non-square rectangle, a square, and the capital letter H?

  1. Regular pentagon: $5$, one through each vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side.

    A regular $n$-gon has $n$ lines of symmetry.

  2. Rectangle: $2$ (the lines joining midpoints of opposite sides). The diagonals are not mirror lines unless it is a square.

    Folding along a diagonal of a long rectangle doesn't match the corners.

  3. Square: $4$ (two midlines and two diagonals). Letter H: $2$ (vertical and horizontal).

    Test each candidate fold.

$5$, $2$, $4$, $2$

A slanted mirror

Reflect $P(4, 1)$ over the line $y = x + 1$.

  1. The mirror has slope $1$, so $PP'$ has slope $-1$: $P' = (4 - t,\ 1 + t)$ for some $t$.

    The segment to the image is perpendicular to the mirror.

  2. The midpoint $\left(4 - \tfrac t2,\ 1 + \tfrac t2\right)$ lies on $y = x + 1$: $1 + \tfrac t2 = 5 - \tfrac t2$, so $t = 4$.

    The mirror bisects $PP'$.

  3. $P' = (0, 5)$. Check: midpoint $(2, 3)$ is on the line. ✓

    Always verify both conditions.

$(0, 5)$

Reflecting a line

Find the equation of the reflection of the line $y = 2x + 1$ over the line $y = x$.

  1. A point $(X, Y)$ is on the image exactly when its mirror image $(Y, X)$ is on the original: $X = 2Y + 1$.

    Reflection over $y = x$ undoes itself, so swap the coordinates.

  2. Solve for $Y$: $y = \dfrac{x - 1}{2}$.

    Rewrite in slope-intercept form.

$y = \dfrac{x-1}{2}$

The shortest bounce

A point must travel from $A(1, 3)$ to the $x$-axis and then to $B(7, 5)$. Find the shortest possible total distance and the point where it touches the axis.

  1. Reflect $A$ over the axis to $A'(1, -3)$. For any $P$ on the axis, $PA = PA'$, so $PA + PB = PA' + PB$.

    The axis is the perpendicular bisector of $AA'$.

  2. $PA' + PB$ is smallest when $P$ is on segment $A'B$: the minimum is $A'B = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10$.

    A straight segment is the shortest path from $A'$ to $B$.

  3. Segment $A'B$ rises $8$ over a run of $6$; it climbs the $3$ units to the axis after a run of $\tfrac{3}{8}\cdot 6 = \tfrac94$. So $P = \left(\tfrac{13}{4}, 0\right)$.

    Check: $PA = 3.75$, $PB = 6.25$, total $10$. ✓

Shortest distance $10$, touching at $\left(\tfrac{13}{4}, 0\right)$.

Reflecting one endpoint turns a bounce path into a straight line (light reflects the same way).

19.4 Distortion

Hook

Draw a circle on a sheet of stretchy plastic and pull the sides. The circle becomes an oval, a right angle in a drawn triangle may no longer be right, yet straight lines stay straight and the picture keeps the same up-and-down size.

Concept

A distortion (stretch or squeeze) multiplies distances in one direction by a factor $k$ while leaving the perpendicular direction alone. Stretching horizontally and vertically:

$$\text{horizontal: } (x, y) \to (kx,\ y) \qquad\qquad \text{vertical: } (x, y) \to (x,\ ky)$$

With $k > 1$ it is a stretch; with $0 < k < 1$ a squeeze. Lines go to lines, but angles are not preserved, so a figure is generally not similar to its image. An isosceles triangle can be stretched into an equilateral one, and a circle stretches into an ellipse.

A stretch by factor $k$ multiplies every area by $k$.

Why? Slice a figure into thin strips along the stretch direction. Each strip gets $k$ times as long and keeps its width, so its area is multiplied by $k$.

Stretching a square

The square with corners $(0,0)$, $(2,0)$, $(2,2)$, $(0,2)$ is stretched horizontally by factor $3$. Describe the image and its area.

  1. $(x, y) \to (3x, y)$ gives corners $(0,0)$, $(6,0)$, $(6,2)$, $(0,2)$: a $6 \times 2$ rectangle.

    Only the $x$-coordinates triple.

  2. Area $12 = 3 \times 4$.

    The area is multiplied by the stretch factor.

A $6 \times 2$ rectangle, area $12$.

A squeeze

Where does $(4, 6)$ go under a vertical squeeze by factor $\tfrac12$?

  1. $(x, y) \to \left(x, \tfrac12 y\right)$: $(4, 6) \to (4, 3)$.

    Vertical distances from the $x$-axis are halved.

$(4, 3)$

A circle becomes an ellipse

The circle of radius $2$ centered at the origin is stretched horizontally by factor $3$. Find the width, height and area of the resulting ellipse.

  1. The circle spans $-2$ to $2$ both ways. After the stretch it spans $-6$ to $6$ horizontally and still $-2$ to $2$ vertically: width $12$, height $4$.

    Only horizontal distances triple.

  2. Area $= 3 \cdot \pi \cdot 2^2 = 12\pi$.

    Area is multiplied by the stretch factor.

Width $12$, height $4$, area $12\pi$.

Isosceles to equilateral

The triangle with vertices $(-1, 0)$, $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ is stretched vertically by factor $k$. For what $k$ is the image equilateral?

  1. The base $(-1,0)$ to $(1,0)$ doesn't move: its length is $2$. The apex goes to $(0, k)$.

    Points on the $x$-axis are fixed by a vertical stretch.

  2. An equilateral triangle with side $2$ has height $\sqrt3$, so $k = \sqrt3$.

    Height of an equilateral triangle is $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ times the side.

$k = \sqrt3$

Which angles change?

The right isosceles triangle $(0,0)$, $(2,0)$, $(0,2)$ is stretched horizontally by factor $2$. Find the angles of the image.

  1. Image: $(0,0)$, $(4,0)$, $(0,2)$.

    Double every $x$-coordinate.

  2. The angle at the origin is still $90^\circ$: its sides lie along the axes, and the axes stay perpendicular.

    Horizontal lines stay horizontal and vertical lines stay vertical.

  3. At $(4,0)$: $\tan\theta = \tfrac24$, so $\theta \approx 26.6^\circ$; the third angle is $\approx 63.4^\circ$. The $45^\circ$ angles are gone.

    The legs are now $4$ and $2$ instead of $2$ and $2$.

$90^\circ$, $\approx 26.6^\circ$, $\approx 63.4^\circ$.

Distortions keep lines straight but change most angles.

Ratios of areas survive

A triangle of area $5$ sits inside a rectangle of area $20$. The picture is stretched by factor $4$. What fraction of the new rectangle does the new triangle cover?

  1. New areas: triangle $20$, rectangle $80$.

    Every area is multiplied by $4$.

  2. Fraction $= \tfrac{20}{80} = \tfrac14$, the same as $\tfrac{5}{20}$ before.

    The common factor $k$ cancels.

$\tfrac14$

The area of an ellipse

Find the area enclosed by the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{25} + \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1$.

  1. $(X, Y)$ is on the ellipse exactly when $\left(\tfrac X5, \tfrac Y3\right)$ is on the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$.

    Rewrite the equation as $\left(\tfrac x5\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac y3\right)^2 = 1$.

  2. So the ellipse is the unit circle stretched horizontally by $5$ and vertically by $3$.

    Undoing the stretches gives back the circle.

  3. Area $= \pi \cdot 1^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 = 15\pi$.

    Each stretch multiplies the area by its factor.

$15\pi$

An ellipse with half-axes $a$ and $b$ has area $\pi ab$.

19.5 Dilation

Hook

Shine a flashlight at a cardboard cut-out in front of a wall. The shadow is the same shape, only bigger, and every point of the shadow sits on the light ray through the matching point of the cut-out. Move the cut-out closer to the light and the shadow grows.

Concept

A dilation with center $O$ and ratio (scale factor) $k > 0$ maps each point $A$ to the point $A'$ on ray $OA$ with

$$OA' = k \cdot OA.$$

With center at the origin, $(x, y) \to (kx, ky)$. With center $(p, q)$:

$$(x, y) \to \big(p + k(x - p),\ q + k(y - q)\big)$$

A figure and its image are similar and point the same way: every length is multiplied by $k$, every angle is unchanged, every segment is parallel to its image, and every area is multiplied by $k^2$. Conversely, two similar figures pointing the same way (but not congruent) are always related by a dilation; the lines through corresponding points all meet at its center.

Dilating from the origin

Find the image of $(3, -2)$ under the dilation with center the origin and ratio $4$.

  1. $(x, y) \to (4x, 4y)$: $(12, -8)$.

    The point moves along the ray from the origin to $4$ times its distance.

$(12, -8)$

Scaling a square

A square with side $3$ is dilated by ratio $2.5$. Find the new side and area.

  1. Side $3 \times 2.5 = 7.5$.

    Lengths scale by $k$.

  2. Area $7.5^2 = 56.25 = 9 \times 2.5^2$.

    Areas scale by $k^2$.

Side $7.5$, area $56.25$.

A center away from the origin

Dilate $P(3, 3)$ with center $O(1, 2)$ and ratio $3$.

  1. $\overrightarrow{OP} = \langle 2, 1\rangle$; tripled: $\langle 6, 3\rangle$.

    $P'$ is on ray $OP$ at $3$ times the distance.

  2. $P' = (1 + 6,\ 2 + 3) = (7, 5)$.

    Start from the center and follow the scaled arrow.

$(7, 5)$

Find the center and ratio

Triangle $A(1,1)$, $B(3,1)$, $C(1,4)$ is dilated to $A'(-1,-1)$, $B'(5,-1)$, $C'(-1,8)$. Find the ratio and center.

  1. $AB = 2$ and $A'B' = 6$, so $k = 3$.

    Every length is multiplied by $k$.

  2. $A' = O + 3(A - O)$ gives $2O = 3A - A' = (4, 4)$, so $O = (2, 2)$.

    Solve the dilation formula for the center.

  3. Check $B$: $(2,2) + 3\langle 1, -1\rangle = (5, -1)$ ✓. Check $C$: $(2,2) + 3\langle -1, 2\rangle = (-1, 8)$ ✓.

    The same center and ratio must work for every vertex.

Ratio $3$, center $(2, 2)$.

A frame between two squares

A square of side $6$ is dilated about its center with ratio $\tfrac13$. Find the area of the frame between the squares and the distance from an outer corner to the nearest inner corner.

  1. Inner side $2$, inner area $4$; frame area $36 - 4 = 32$.

    Area scales by $\left(\tfrac13\right)^2 = \tfrac19$.

  2. Corner $A$ is $3\sqrt2$ from the center $O$ (half a diagonal). Its image $A'$ is on $OA$ at distance $\tfrac13 \cdot 3\sqrt2 = \sqrt2$.

    A dilation moves points along rays from the center.

  3. $AA' = 3\sqrt2 - \sqrt2 = 2\sqrt2$.

    $O$, $A'$, $A$ are collinear in that order.

Frame area $32$; distance $2\sqrt2$.

Enlarging a photo

A $4 \times 6$ photo is enlarged (without distortion) so that its area becomes $54$. What are the new dimensions?

  1. Area ratio $\tfrac{54}{24} = \tfrac94 = k^2$, so $k = \tfrac32$.

    Areas scale by the square of the ratio.

  2. New size: $6 \times 9$.

    Multiply both dimensions by $\tfrac32$.

$6 \times 9$

The center between two circles

Circle $\omega_1$ has center $(0,0)$ and radius $2$; circle $\omega_2$ has center $(9,0)$ and radius $1$. Find the center of the dilation with positive ratio that maps $\omega_1$ to $\omega_2$.

  1. The ratio is $\tfrac{1}{2}$ (radius $2 \to$ radius $1$), and the dilation maps center to center.

    A dilation maps a circle to a circle, center to center, radius times $k$.

  2. $P$ is on line $O_1O_2$ (the $x$-axis), with $O_2 = P + \tfrac12(O_1 - P)$. So $(9, 0) = \tfrac12 P$ and $P = (18, 0)$.

    $O_2$ is on ray $PO_1$ at half the distance.

  3. Check: $P$ is $18$ from $O_1$ and $9$ from $O_2$, ratio $\tfrac12$. ✓

    Both distances along the same ray.

$(18, 0)$

The two common outer tangent lines of the circles pass through this center, since a dilation maps tangent lines to tangent lines.

19.6 The More Things Change...

Hook

A map of your town is not your town, but some things about it are trustworthy: if three shops are on one straight street, they're on one straight line on the map. Knowing exactly which facts a transformation keeps is what makes transformations useful.

Concept

Every transformation in this chapter sends lines to lines, so collinear points stay collinear, and midpoints go to midpoints. Beyond that, they differ:

Keeps…TranslateRotateReflectDilateStretch
lengths✓✓✓×$k$✗
angles (shape)✓✓✓✓✗
area✓✓✓×$k^2$×$k$
orientation✓✓✗✓✓
collinearity, midpoints✓✓✓✓✓

Even when area changes, ratios of areas are kept: if every area is multiplied by the same factor, fractions of the whole don't change. That lets you solve a hard problem about a stretched figure by solving an easy one about the original.

Which keep area?

A figure has area $6$. What is the area of its image under: a translation; a $70^\circ$ rotation; a reflection; a dilation with ratio $3$; a stretch by factor $3$?

  1. Translation, rotation, reflection: $6$ each.

    These are rigid motions: the image is congruent.

  2. Dilation by $3$: $6 \cdot 9 = 54$. Stretch by $3$: $6 \cdot 3 = 18$.

    Dilations scale area by $k^2$, stretches by $k$.

$6, 6, 6, 54, 18$

A chain of transformations

A triangle of area $10$ is dilated by ratio $3$, then reflected, then translated. What is its final area?

  1. After the dilation: $10 \cdot 3^2 = 90$.

    Area scales by $k^2$.

  2. Reflection and translation keep area: still $90$.

    Rigid motions don't change size.

$90$

Two flips make a turn

Reflect over the $x$-axis, then over the $y$-axis. What single transformation is this? Explain using orientation.

  1. $(x, y) \to (x, -y) \to (-x, -y)$.

    Apply the rules in order.

  2. That is the $180^\circ$ rotation about the origin.

    Compare with the half-turn rule.

  3. This fits orientation: each reflection reverses it, so two reflections restore it, as a rotation should.

    Orientation acts like a sign: reversed twice is back to normal.

The $180^\circ$ rotation about the origin.

Dilate, then stretch

A square of area $5$ is dilated by ratio $2$ and then stretched horizontally by factor $3$. Find the final area. Is the image a square?

  1. Area: $5 \cdot 2^2 \cdot 3 = 60$.

    Multiply the area factors.

  2. The dilation keeps it a square; the stretch makes it $3$ times as wide as it is tall, so the image is a non-square rectangle.

    Only the stretch changes shape.

Area $60$; a rectangle, not a square.

Centroids survive a stretch

Triangle $(0,0)$, $(6,0)$, $(0,3)$ is stretched horizontally by factor $2$. Show that the centroid of the image is the image of the centroid.

  1. Original centroid: $\left(\tfrac{0+6+0}{3}, \tfrac{0+0+3}{3}\right) = (2, 1)$. Its image: $(4, 1)$.

    The centroid averages the vertices.

  2. Image triangle: $(0,0)$, $(12,0)$, $(0,3)$, with centroid $(4, 1)$. ✓

    Same point either way.

  3. Reason: a stretch keeps midpoints, so medians map to medians, and their meeting point maps to their meeting point.

    The centroid is built only from midpoints and lines, which stretches keep.

Both give $(4, 1)$.

The image of a circle under a stretch

Find the equation of the image of the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 4$ under $(x, y) \to (x, 2y)$.

  1. $(X, Y)$ is on the image exactly when $\left(X, \tfrac{Y}{2}\right)$ is on the circle.

    Undo the stretch.

  2. $X^2 + \tfrac{Y^2}{4} = 4$, that is, $\dfrac{x^2}{4} + \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1$.

    Substitute, then divide by $4$.

$\dfrac{x^2}{4} + \dfrac{y^2}{16} = 1$, an ellipse $4$ wide and $8$ tall.

Next in the book: Example 19-8 and Exercise 19-8.

The biggest triangle in an ellipse

Granted that the largest triangle inside a circle of radius $1$ is equilateral, with area $\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{4}$, find the largest area of a triangle inside the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{16} + y^2 = 1$.

  1. The ellipse is the unit circle stretched horizontally by $4$.

    $(X, Y)$ is on it exactly when $\left(\tfrac X4, Y\right)$ is on the unit circle.

  2. Triangles inside the ellipse correspond exactly to triangles inside the circle, and every area is multiplied by $4$. So the largest in the ellipse is the image of the largest in the circle.

    Multiplying all areas by the same positive number doesn't change which is biggest.

  3. Largest area $= 4 \cdot \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{4} = 3\sqrt3$.

    Apply the area factor.

$3\sqrt3$

Transform a hard figure into an easy one, solve there, and transform back.

19.7 Transformation Proofs

Hook

Stand between two mirrors that meet at a corner and you'll see yourself turned around, not flipped. Two reflections have combined into a rotation. It looks obvious, but "it looks obvious" is not a proof. This section is about proving the properties we've been using.

Concept

To prove a transformation property, use what you know for certain: the definition of the transformation, and either coordinates or congruent triangles.

  • Coordinates: write the rule, apply it to general points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$, and compute (for example, with the distance formula).
  • Congruent triangles: if a rotation keeps distances, then triangle $ABC$ and its image are congruent by SSS, so all corresponding angles match.
  • Both directions: to show the image of figure $X$ is figure $Y$, show every image point is on $Y$ and every point of $Y$ is an image point.

Reflect in two lines meeting at angle $\theta$: the result is a rotation by $2\theta$ about the crossing point. Reflect in two parallel lines a distance $d$ apart: a translation by $2d$.

Translations keep midpoints

Prove that a translation maps the midpoint of a segment $PQ$ to the midpoint of the image segment $P'Q'$.

  1. Let $P = (x_1, y_1)$, $Q = (x_2, y_2)$ and the translation be $(x,y) \to (x+a, y+b)$. The midpoint $M = \left(\tfrac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \tfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$ maps to $\left(\tfrac{x_1+x_2}{2} + a,\ \tfrac{y_1+y_2}{2} + b\right)$.

    Apply the rule to $M$.

  2. The midpoint of $P'(x_1+a, y_1+b)$ and $Q'(x_2+a, y_2+b)$ is $\left(\tfrac{x_1+x_2+2a}{2}, \tfrac{y_1+y_2+2b}{2}\right)$, the same point.

    Compute the other side and compare.

Both equal $\left(\tfrac{x_1+x_2}{2}+a,\ \tfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}+b\right)$, so $M' $ is the midpoint of $P'Q'$.

Translations keep distances

Prove that a translation preserves the distance between any two points.

  1. With $P$, $Q$ and the translation as before, $P'Q'^2 = \big((x_2+a) - (x_1+a)\big)^2 + \big((y_2+b)-(y_1+b)\big)^2$.

    Distance formula on the images.

  2. The $a$'s and $b$'s cancel: $P'Q'^2 = (x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 = PQ^2$.

    Only differences of coordinates matter, and a translation doesn't change them.

$P'Q' = PQ$ for all $P$, $Q$.

A quarter turn keeps distances

Prove that the rotation $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$ preserves distances.

  1. $P'(-y_1, x_1)$ and $Q'(-y_2, x_2)$: $P'Q'^2 = (-y_2 + y_1)^2 + (x_2 - x_1)^2$.

    Distance formula on the images.

  2. That is $(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 = PQ^2$.

    The same two squares, in the other order; $(-u)^2 = u^2$.

$P'Q' = PQ$.

Two parallel mirrors

Prove that reflecting in $x = 0$ and then in $x = d$ is the translation $(x, y) \to (x + 2d, y)$.

  1. Reflection in $x = 0$: $(x, y) \to (-x, y)$.

    The $y$-axis rule.

  2. Reflection in $x = d$: $(x, y) \to (2d - x, y)$, since $d$ is the average of $x$ and its image.

    As with the mirror $x = 3$ in Section 19.3.

  3. Combined: $(x, y) \to (-x, y) \to \big(2d - (-x),\ y\big) = (x + 2d,\ y)$.

    Feed the first output into the second rule.

The translation by $2d$, perpendicular to the mirrors.

Two crossing mirrors

Show that reflecting in the $x$-axis and then in the line $y = x$ is a rotation by twice the $45^\circ$ angle between the mirrors.

  1. $(x, y) \to (x, -y) \to (-y, x)$.

    Over the $x$-axis, then swap coordinates.

  2. $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$ is the $90^\circ$ counterclockwise rotation about the origin, and $90^\circ = 2 \cdot 45^\circ$.

    Recognize the quarter-turn rule.

A $90^\circ$ rotation about the origin, where the mirrors cross.

Rotations keep angles

Given that rotations preserve distances, prove that they preserve angles: if $A$, $B$, $C$ map to $A'$, $B'$, $C'$, then $\angle ABC = \angle A'B'C'$.

  1. $A'B' = AB$, $B'C' = BC$ and $A'C' = AC$.

    Each is a distance between two points and their images.

  2. So $\triangle A'B'C' \cong \triangle ABC$ by SSS.

    Three pairs of equal sides.

  3. Corresponding angles of congruent triangles are equal: $\angle A'B'C' = \angle ABC$.

    That is the conclusion.

Proved: preserving distances forces preserving angles.

Any transformation that keeps all distances also keeps all angles, by SSS.

Dilations make parallel lines

Prove that the dilation $(x, y) \to (kx, ky)$ with $k > 0$ maps the line $ax + by = c$ onto the line $ax + by = kc$, which is parallel to it (or the same line, if $c = 0$).

  1. Every image point is on the new line: if $ax + by = c$, then the image $(kx, ky)$ satisfies $a(kx) + b(ky) = k(ax + by) = kc$.

    Direction one: image points lie on the claimed line.

  2. Every point of the new line is an image: if $aX + bY = kc$, then $\left(\tfrac Xk, \tfrac Yk\right)$ satisfies $a\tfrac Xk + b\tfrac Yk = c$, so it is on the original line, and it maps to $(X, Y)$.

    Direction two: nothing on the new line is missed. Dividing by $k$ is allowed since $k > 0$.

  3. The lines $ax + by = c$ and $ax + by = kc$ have the same coefficients $a, b$, so they have the same slope (or are both vertical): they are parallel.

    The slope depends only on $a$ and $b$.

The image is exactly the line $ax + by = kc$, parallel to the original.

To identify an image, prove both inclusions: image $\subseteq$ target and target $\subseteq$ image.
