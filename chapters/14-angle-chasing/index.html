Angle Chasing

Some geometry problems ask for one angle and give you a few others. You won't find the answer with one clever formula. You find it by chasing: fill in every angle you can, one small fact at a time, until the one you want is surrounded. This chapter collects the facts you chase with and shows the habits that make the chase short.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • Angles on a line add to $180^\circ$; angles around a point add to $360^\circ$; vertical angles are equal.
  • Parallel lines: corresponding and alternate interior angles are equal; same-side interior angles are supplementary.
  • Triangle: angles add to $180^\circ$, and an exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles. Quadrilateral: angles add to $360^\circ$.
  • Equal sides mean equal opposite angles. Every equilateral angle is $60^\circ$; two radii of a circle form an isosceles triangle.
  • Circles: an inscribed angle is half its arc, a central angle equals its arc, an angle inscribed in a semicircle is $90^\circ$, a tangent is perpendicular to the radius, and opposite angles of an inscribed quadrilateral add to $180^\circ$.
  • Habit: write every angle you find on the diagram, and name one unknown $x$ so that many angles can be written in terms of it.

14.1 Lines, Parallels and Angle Sums

Hook

A road crosses two parallel railway tracks, then bends between them and crosses again. You know the angle at each crossing. What is the angle of the bend? You don't need a protractor; you need one extra line, drawn parallel to the tracks through the bend.

Concept

The basic facts you chase with:

  • On a line, adjacent angles add to $180^\circ$. Around a point, angles add to $360^\circ$. Vertical angles (opposite angles where two lines cross) are equal.
  • Parallel lines cut by a transversal make equal corresponding angles and equal alternate interior angles; same-side interior angles add to $180^\circ$.
  • Triangle sum $180^\circ$; quadrilateral sum $360^\circ$ (a diagonal makes two triangles).
  • Exterior angle: extend one side of a triangle; the outside angle equals the sum of the two interior angles at the other vertices.

$$\text{exterior angle at } C = \angle A + \angle B$$

The chase itself is a routine. Mark everything you are given. Look for any angle that one fact determines, and write it in. Repeat. When the chase stalls, give one unknown angle a name, $x$, and express others in terms of it. Then use some fact (a triangle sum, a straight line) that produces an equation.

If the angles you know are scattered between parallel lines, draw another parallel line through the vertex you care about. It turns one hard angle into two easy alternate angles.

Angles on a line

Two lines cross. Two adjacent angles measure $(3x + 10)^\circ$ and $(5x - 30)^\circ$. Find all four angles.

  1. $(3x + 10) + (5x - 30) = 180$, so $8x - 20 = 180$ and $x = 25$.

    Adjacent angles formed by two crossing lines lie along a straight line.

  2. The angles are $3(25) + 10 = 85^\circ$ and $5(25) - 30 = 95^\circ$.

    Substitute back. They add to $180^\circ$, as they should.

  3. The other two angles are $85^\circ$ and $95^\circ$ as well.

    Each angle is vertical to one of the first two.

$85^\circ, 95^\circ, 85^\circ, 95^\circ$

Angles around a point

Four rays from a point make angles of $90^\circ$, $x^\circ$, $2x^\circ$ and $3x^\circ$, going around. Find $x$.

  1. $90 + x + 2x + 3x = 360$, so $6x = 270$.

    A full turn is $360^\circ$.

  2. $x = 45$, so the angles are $90^\circ, 45^\circ, 90^\circ, 135^\circ$.

    Check: $90 + 45 + 90 + 135 = 360$.

$x = 45$

The exterior angle shortcut

An exterior angle of a triangle is $130^\circ$. The two interior angles not next to it are $x^\circ$ and $(x + 20)^\circ$. Find all three interior angles.

  1. $x + (x + 20) = 130$, so $x = 55$.

    An exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.

  2. The remote angles are $55^\circ$ and $75^\circ$; the third interior angle is $180^\circ - 130^\circ = 50^\circ$.

    The third interior angle and the exterior angle form a straight line.

$55^\circ, 75^\circ, 50^\circ$

A bend between parallel lines

Lines $\ell \parallel m$. Point $A$ is on $\ell$, $B$ is on $m$, and $P$ lies between the lines. Segment $AP$ makes a $35^\circ$ angle with $\ell$, and $BP$ makes a $50^\circ$ angle with $m$, both measured on the side facing $P$'s bend. Find $\angle APB$.

  1. Through $P$, draw a line parallel to $\ell$ (and so to $m$), as dashed above. It splits $\angle APB$ into an upper part and a lower part.

    A new parallel line produces alternate interior angles that are equal to the given ones.

  2. The upper part is $35^\circ$ (alternate to the angle at $A$) and the lower part is $50^\circ$ (alternate to the angle at $B$).

    $AP$ is a transversal of $\ell$ and the new line; $BP$ is a transversal of $m$ and the new line.

  3. $\angle APB = 35^\circ + 50^\circ = 85^\circ$.

    The two parts make up the whole angle.

$85^\circ$

A bend between two parallel lines equals the sum of the angles the two arms make with the lines.

A quadrilateral

The angles of a quadrilateral are $80^\circ$, $95^\circ$, $(2x)^\circ$ and $(3x)^\circ$. Find the two unknown angles.

  1. $80 + 95 + 2x + 3x = 360$, so $5x = 185$ and $x = 37$.

    A diagonal splits a quadrilateral into two triangles, so its angles add to $2 \cdot 180^\circ$.

  2. The angles are $2(37) = 74^\circ$ and $3(37) = 111^\circ$.

    Check: $80 + 95 + 74 + 111 = 360$.

$74^\circ$ and $111^\circ$

Two bisectors between parallel lines

A transversal crosses parallel lines $\ell$ and $m$ at $A$ and $B$. The bisectors of the two same-side interior angles at $A$ and $B$ meet at $P$. Find $\angle APB$.

  1. Call the interior angles at $A$ and $B$ (on the same side) $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Then $\alpha + \beta = 180^\circ$.

    Same-side interior angles of parallel lines are supplementary.

  2. In $\triangle APB$: $\angle PAB = \tfrac12\alpha$ and $\angle PBA = \tfrac12\beta$, which add to $\tfrac12(\alpha + \beta) = 90^\circ$.

    Each bisector takes half of its angle, and the halves are the two base angles of $\triangle APB$.

  3. $\angle APB = 180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$.

    Triangle sum.

$90^\circ$, no matter what angle the transversal makes.

When an answer doesn't depend on the given numbers, look for a sum that is fixed, here $\alpha + \beta = 180^\circ$.

Bisectors of exterior angles

In $\triangle ABC$, $\angle A = 64^\circ$. The bisectors of the exterior angles at $B$ and $C$ meet at $P$. Find $\angle BPC$.

  1. The exterior angle at $B$ is $180^\circ - \angle B$, so the bisector makes $\angle PBC = 90^\circ - \tfrac12\angle B$. Likewise $\angle PCB = 90^\circ - \tfrac12\angle C$.

    The exterior angle and the interior angle at $B$ form a straight line; then we halve.

  2. $\angle BPC = 180^\circ - \left(90^\circ - \tfrac12\angle B\right) - \left(90^\circ - \tfrac12\angle C\right) = \tfrac12(\angle B + \angle C)$.

    Triangle sum in $\triangle BPC$. Only the sum $\angle B + \angle C$ is needed.

  3. $\angle B + \angle C = 180^\circ - 64^\circ = 116^\circ$, so $\angle BPC = 58^\circ$.

    Triangle sum in $\triangle ABC$.

$58^\circ$ (in general $90^\circ - \tfrac12\angle A$).

Name the angles you don't know; often only their sum survives to the end.

14.2 Isosceles and Equilateral Triangles

Hook

Lay toothpicks of equal length in a zigzag between the two sides of a narrow angle. Each new toothpick makes a steeper triangle than the last, and eventually the zigzag can't continue. How many fit? Every step is an isosceles triangle, and equal sides are one of the strongest clues in angle chasing.

Concept

Equal sides are opposite equal angles, and the converse holds too. So whenever a problem mentions equal lengths, turn them into equal angles at once:

$$AB = AC \iff \angle B = \angle C, \qquad \text{base angle} = \frac{180^\circ - \text{vertex angle}}{2}.$$

  • Every angle of an equilateral triangle is $60^\circ$, and every angle of a square is $90^\circ$. When these shapes are glued together, the shared sides create new isosceles triangles.
  • Two radii of the same circle are equal, so any triangle with two radii as sides is isosceles.
  • In chains of isosceles triangles the angles grow by repeated use of the exterior angle theorem: $x$, then $2x$, then $3x$, and so on.

Mark equal segments with tick marks and equal angles with arcs as soon as you see them. Many chases finish with a single equation like $5x = 180^\circ$.

Vertex angle from a base angle

In $\triangle ABC$, $AB = AC$ and $\angle B = 68^\circ$. Find $\angle A$.

  1. $\angle C = \angle B = 68^\circ$.

    $AB = AC$, so the angles opposite them, $\angle C$ and $\angle B$, are equal.

  2. $\angle A = 180^\circ - 68^\circ - 68^\circ = 44^\circ$.

    Triangle sum.

$44^\circ$

Two radii make an isosceles triangle

$AB$ is a chord of a circle with center $O$, and $\angle OAB = 25^\circ$. Find $\angle AOB$.

  1. $OA = OB$ (radii), so $\angle OBA = \angle OAB = 25^\circ$.

    Radii are the hidden equal sides in circle problems.

  2. $\angle AOB = 180^\circ - 25^\circ - 25^\circ = 130^\circ$.

    Triangle sum in $\triangle OAB$.

$130^\circ$

An isosceles triangle inside another

In $\triangle ABC$, $AB = AC$ and $\angle B = 40^\circ$. Point $D$ on $BC$ satisfies $AD = BD$. Find $\angle DAC$ and $\angle ADC$.

  1. $\angle C = 40^\circ$ and $\angle BAC = 180^\circ - 80^\circ = 100^\circ$.

    Base angles of the big isosceles triangle.

  2. $AD = BD$ gives $\angle BAD = \angle B = 40^\circ$, so $\angle DAC = 100^\circ - 40^\circ = 60^\circ$.

    $\triangle ABD$ is isosceles too, with its equal angles at $A$ and $B$.

  3. $\angle ADC = \angle BAD + \angle B = 80^\circ$.

    $\angle ADC$ is an exterior angle of $\triangle ABD$. Check in $\triangle ADC$: $60^\circ + 80^\circ + 40^\circ = 180^\circ$.

$\angle DAC = 60^\circ$, $\angle ADC = 80^\circ$

Extending an equilateral triangle

$\triangle ABC$ is equilateral. Side $BC$ is extended past $C$ to $D$ so that $CD = CA$. Find $\angle BAD$.

  1. $\angle ACD = 180^\circ - 60^\circ = 120^\circ$.

    $\angle ACB$ and $\angle ACD$ lie along line $BD$.

  2. $CA = CD$, so $\angle CAD = \angle CDA = \dfrac{180^\circ - 120^\circ}{2} = 30^\circ$.

    $\triangle ACD$ is isosceles with vertex angle $120^\circ$.

  3. $\angle BAD = \angle BAC + \angle CAD = 60^\circ + 30^\circ = 90^\circ$.

    The two angles are side by side at $A$.

$90^\circ$

An equilateral triangle on a square

Equilateral $\triangle ABC$ sits on side $BC$ of square $BCDE$, outside the square, with $D$ next to $C$ and $E$ next to $B$. Find $\angle DAE$.

  1. $BA = BC = BE$, so $\triangle ABE$ is isosceles with $BA = BE$.

    The triangle and the square share side $BC$, so all their sides are equal.

  2. $\angle ABE = \angle ABC + \angle CBE = 60^\circ + 90^\circ = 150^\circ$, so $\angle BAE = \dfrac{180^\circ - 150^\circ}{2} = 15^\circ$.

    Base angles of an isosceles triangle with vertex angle $150^\circ$.

  3. By symmetry $\angle CAD = 15^\circ$ as well.

    The same argument works on the right with $CA = CD$.

  4. $\angle DAE = \angle BAC - \angle BAE - \angle CAD = 60^\circ - 15^\circ - 15^\circ = 30^\circ$.

    Rays $AE$ and $AD$ lie inside $\angle BAC$, so we subtract the two outer slivers.

$30^\circ$

Shapes glued along a side share a length, and that creates new isosceles triangles.

Three equal segments

In $\triangle ABC$, $AB = AC$. Point $D$ on side $AC$ satisfies $AD = BD = BC$. Find $\angle A$.

  1. Let $\angle A = x$. Since $AD = BD$, $\angle ABD = x$.

    Name one angle and push it as far as it will go.

  2. $\angle BDC = x + x = 2x$.

    Exterior angle of $\triangle ABD$ at $D$.

  3. $BD = BC$, so $\angle C = \angle BDC = 2x$. And $AB = AC$, so $\angle ABC = \angle C = 2x$.

    Two more isosceles triangles: $\triangle BDC$ and $\triangle ABC$.

  4. In $\triangle ABC$: $x + 2x + 2x = 180^\circ$, so $x = 36^\circ$.

    Triangle sum gives the equation.

$\angle A = 36^\circ$

How many equal segments fit?

Rays $OX$ and $OY$ form a $12^\circ$ angle. Starting at $O$, equal segments $OA_1 = A_1A_2 = A_2A_3 = \cdots$ zigzag outward, with $A_1, A_3, \ldots$ on $OX$ and $A_2, A_4, \ldots$ on $OY$, each new point farther from $O$ than the one before on its ray. What is the largest possible number of segments (counting $OA_1$)?

  1. $\triangle OA_1A_2$ has $OA_1 = A_1A_2$, so its base angles are $\angle A_1OA_2 = \angle A_1A_2O = 12^\circ$.

    The first segment pair forms an isosceles triangle with apex $A_1$.

  2. $\triangle A_1A_2A_3$ has $A_1A_2 = A_2A_3$. Its base angle at $A_1$ is the exterior angle of $\triangle OA_1A_2$, which is $12^\circ + 12^\circ = 24^\circ$.

    Exterior angle theorem.

  3. Repeating, the $k$th isosceles triangle has base angles $12k$ degrees: $12^\circ, 24^\circ, 36^\circ, \ldots$

    Each exterior angle adds the fixed $12^\circ$ at $O$ to the previous base angle.

  4. A triangle needs base angles below $90^\circ$: $12k < 90$ means $k \le 7$ (since $7 \cdot 12 = 84$ and $8 \cdot 12 = 96$).

    Two angles of $90^\circ$ or more can't fit in one triangle.

  5. Seven triangles use $OA_1$ plus seven more segments: $8$ in all.

    The first triangle uses two segments; each later triangle adds one.

$8$ segments

In a chain of isosceles triangles, the exterior angle theorem makes the angles grow in a steady pattern.

14.3 Angles in Circles

Hook

Stand anywhere on the curved edge of a circular stage and look at the two ends of a fixed chord. Walk around the edge: the angle between your two lines of sight never changes. Circles hide many equal angles, and finding them is half of every circle chase.

Concept

The facts from Chapter 10, collected for chasing:

  • A central angle equals its arc. An inscribed angle is half its arc. So inscribed angles on the same arc are equal, and an angle inscribed in a semicircle is $90^\circ$.
  • Opposite angles of an inscribed (cyclic) quadrilateral add to $180^\circ$, because their arcs add to the full $360^\circ$.
  • A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact. Two tangents from one point are equal, and the angle between a tangent and a chord is half the arc it cuts off.
  • Two chords crossing inside make an angle equal to half the sum of the intercepted arcs; two secants or tangents meeting outside make half the difference.

$$\text{inscribed angle} = \tfrac12\,\text{arc}, \qquad \text{inside: } \tfrac12(\text{arc}_1 + \text{arc}_2), \qquad \text{outside: } \tfrac12(\text{far arc} - \text{near arc})$$

In circle problems, chase arcs as well as angles. Converting each angle to "half an arc" lets you add and compare them easily, and the arcs around the circle always total $360^\circ$.

Inscribed versus central

An inscribed angle measures $35^\circ$. What is its arc, and what central angle cuts off the same arc?

  1. Arc $= 2 \cdot 35^\circ = 70^\circ$.

    An inscribed angle is half its arc.

  2. The central angle is also $70^\circ$.

    A central angle equals its arc.

Arc $70^\circ$; central angle $70^\circ$.

A right angle from a diameter

$AB$ is a diameter and $C$ is on the circle, with $\angle CAB = 28^\circ$. Find $\angle ABC$.

  1. $\angle ACB = 90^\circ$.

    It is inscribed in a semicircle.

  2. $\angle ABC = 180^\circ - 90^\circ - 28^\circ = 62^\circ$.

    Triangle sum.

$62^\circ$

An inscribed quadrilateral

Quadrilateral $ABCD$ is inscribed in a circle with $\angle A = 75^\circ$ and $\angle B = 110^\circ$. Find $\angle C$ and $\angle D$.

  1. $\angle A$ is half of arc $BCD$ and $\angle C$ is half of arc $DAB$. These two arcs make up the whole circle, so $\angle A + \angle C = \tfrac12 \cdot 360^\circ = 180^\circ$.

    This is why opposite angles of an inscribed quadrilateral are supplementary.

  2. $\angle C = 180^\circ - 75^\circ = 105^\circ$ and $\angle D = 180^\circ - 110^\circ = 70^\circ$.

    Apply the rule to both pairs of opposite angles.

$\angle C = 105^\circ$, $\angle D = 70^\circ$

Two tangents

Tangents from $P$ touch a circle with center $O$ at $A$ and $B$, and $\angle APB = 50^\circ$. Find $\angle AOB$, and the inscribed angle $\angle ACB$ for a point $C$ on the major arc.

  1. $\angle OAP = \angle OBP = 90^\circ$.

    A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.

  2. In quadrilateral $OAPB$: $\angle AOB = 360^\circ - 90^\circ - 90^\circ - 50^\circ = 130^\circ$.

    Quadrilateral angle sum.

  3. $\angle ACB = \tfrac12 \cdot 130^\circ = 65^\circ$.

    $\angle ACB$ is inscribed on the minor arc $AB$, which has measure $130^\circ$ (the central angle).

$\angle AOB = 130^\circ$, $\angle ACB = 65^\circ$

Chords crossing inside

Chords $AC$ and $BD$ cross at $E$ inside a circle. Arc $AB = 80^\circ$ and arc $CD = 40^\circ$ (the arcs cut off by $\angle AEB$ and its vertical angle). Find $\angle AEB$, and explain the formula.

  1. Draw chord $BC$. Then $\angle ACB = \tfrac12 \cdot 80^\circ = 40^\circ$ and $\angle DBC = \tfrac12 \cdot 40^\circ = 20^\circ$.

    Each is an inscribed angle on one of the given arcs.

  2. $\angle AEB$ is an exterior angle of $\triangle EBC$, so $\angle AEB = \angle ECB + \angle EBC = 40^\circ + 20^\circ = 60^\circ$.

    Exterior angle theorem: this is exactly where "half the sum of the arcs" comes from.

$60^\circ = \tfrac12(80^\circ + 40^\circ)$

Equal angles on the same arc

$ABCD$ is inscribed in a circle, and its diagonals meet at $E$. Given $\angle BAC = 30^\circ$ and $\angle ABD = 45^\circ$, find $\angle BDC$, $\angle ACD$ and $\angle AEB$.

  1. $\angle BDC = \angle BAC = 30^\circ$.

    Both are inscribed angles on arc $BC$.

  2. $\angle ACD = \angle ABD = 45^\circ$.

    Both are inscribed angles on arc $AD$.

  3. In $\triangle ABE$: $\angle AEB = 180^\circ - 30^\circ - 45^\circ = 105^\circ$.

    $\angle BAE = \angle BAC$ and $\angle ABE = \angle ABD$, because $E$ lies on both diagonals.

$\angle BDC = 30^\circ$, $\angle ACD = 45^\circ$, $\angle AEB = 105^\circ$

In a cyclic quadrilateral, each diagonal creates pairs of equal inscribed angles.

A tangent meets an extended side

$\triangle ABC$ is inscribed in a circle with $\angle A = 50^\circ$, $\angle B = 60^\circ$, $\angle C = 70^\circ$. The tangent at $A$ meets line $BC$ at $P$. Find $\angle APB$.

  1. The angle between the tangent and chord $AC$ equals an inscribed angle on arc $AC$, which is $\angle B = 60^\circ$. Similarly, the angle between the tangent and chord $AB$ equals $\angle C = 70^\circ$.

    Tangent-chord angles are half their arc, just like inscribed angles on that arc.

  2. Suppose $P$ is beyond $B$ (order $P, B, C$). Then $\triangle PAB$ would have $\angle PAB = 70^\circ$ and $\angle PBA = 180^\circ - 60^\circ = 120^\circ$, which already exceed $180^\circ$. Impossible.

    Test each possible position; a negative angle rules one out.

  3. So $P$ is beyond $C$ (order $B, C, P$). In $\triangle PAC$: $\angle PAC = 60^\circ$ and $\angle PCA = 180^\circ - 70^\circ = 110^\circ$.

    $\angle PCA$ and $\angle ACB$ lie along line $BP$.

  4. $\angle APB = \angle APC = 180^\circ - 60^\circ - 110^\circ = 10^\circ$.

    Triangle sum.

$10^\circ$ (in general the difference $\angle B - \angle C$, in absolute value).

When the position of a point is unclear, try each case; the angle sums will rule out the impossible one.
