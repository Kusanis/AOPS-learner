Chapter 13 · Geometry

Polygons

Triangles and quadrilaterals are just the start. A polygon can have any number of sides, and a handful of counting arguments tell you a surprising amount about all of them at once: how many diagonals, how the angles add up, and, for regular polygons, exactly how big everything is. The regular hexagon, built from six equilateral triangles, gets a section of its own.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • An $n$-gon has $n - 3$ diagonals from each vertex and $\dfrac{n(n-3)}{2}$ diagonals in all (divide by $2$ because each diagonal has two ends).
  • Interior angles add to $180^\circ(n - 2)$: the diagonals from one vertex cut the polygon into $n - 2$ triangles.
  • Exterior angles of any convex polygon add to $360^\circ$, whatever $n$ is.
  • Regular $n$-gon: each exterior angle is $\dfrac{360^\circ}{n}$ and each interior angle is $180^\circ - \dfrac{360^\circ}{n}$.
  • Split a regular polygon into $2n$ right triangles at the centre, each with angle $\dfrac{180^\circ}{n}$: $\tan\dfrac{180^\circ}{n} = \dfrac{l/2}{r}$, $\sin\dfrac{180^\circ}{n} = \dfrac{l/2}{R}$, and Area $= \tfrac12 \cdot$ perimeter $\cdot\, r$.
  • Regular hexagon with side $s$: six equilateral triangles, area $\dfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}s^2$, short diagonal $s\sqrt3$, long diagonal $2s$.

13.1 Types of Polygons

Hook

Ten people stand in a circle, and every pair who are not neighbours shakes hands across the middle. How many handshakes happen? It sounds like a counting puzzle, but it's really a geometry question: you are counting the diagonals of a decagon.

Concept

A polygon is a closed figure in a plane made of straight segments that don't cross each other. A polygon with $n$ sides is an $n$-gon, and the common ones have names:

$3$ triangle · $4$ quadrilateral · $5$ pentagon · $6$ hexagon · $7$ heptagon · $8$ octagon · $9$ nonagon · $10$ decagon · $12$ dodecagon

A polygon is regular if all its sides are equal and all its angles are equal. One condition is not enough: a rhombus has equal sides but needn't have equal angles, and a rectangle has equal angles but needn't have equal sides.

A diagonal joins two vertices that aren't neighbours. From one vertex there are $n - 1$ other vertices; two of them are neighbours (those segments are sides), so $n - 3$ diagonals start there. Multiplying by $n$ vertices counts every diagonal twice, once from each end, so

$$\text{number of diagonals} = \frac{n(n-3)}{2}.$$

Check with a quadrilateral: $\tfrac{4 \cdot 1}{2} = 2$. Forgetting to divide by $2$ would give $4$, which is clearly wrong.

Diagonals from one vertex

How many diagonals can be drawn from one vertex of a decagon?

  1. A decagon has $10$ vertices; one vertex can be joined to the other $9$.

    Count every possible segment from the chosen vertex first.

  2. Two of those are sides, leaving $9 - 2 = 7$ diagonals.

    Segments to the two neighbours are sides, not diagonals. In general this is $n - 3$.

$7$

All the diagonals of a hexagon

How many diagonals does a hexagon have?

  1. Each of the $6$ vertices starts $6 - 3 = 3$ diagonals, giving $6 \times 3 = 18$ counts.

    Count from every vertex.

  2. Each diagonal was counted twice, so there are $18 / 2 = 9$.

    A diagonal is counted once from each endpoint.

$9$

Equal sides, or equal angles, isn't enough

Give a polygon with all sides equal that is not regular, and one with all angles equal that is not regular.

  1. A rhombus with angles $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$ has four equal sides but unequal angles.

    Pushing a square sideways keeps the side lengths but changes the angles.

  2. A $2 \times 5$ rectangle has four $90^\circ$ angles but unequal sides.

    Stretching a square keeps the angles but changes the sides.

A non-square rhombus (equal sides) and a non-square rectangle (equal angles).

"Regular" needs both conditions.

Diagonals of a 20-gon

How many diagonals does a $20$-sided polygon have?

  1. $\dfrac{20(20 - 3)}{2} = \dfrac{20 \cdot 17}{2} = 170$.

    Apply the formula: $17$ diagonals from each of $20$ vertices, halved for double counting.

$170$

Working backward

A polygon has $35$ diagonals. How many sides does it have?

  1. $\dfrac{n(n-3)}{2} = 35 \Rightarrow n(n - 3) = 70$.

    Set the formula equal to the given count.

  2. $n^2 - 3n - 70 = 0 \Rightarrow (n - 10)(n + 7) = 0$, so $n = 10$.

    A number of sides must be positive. (Or spot that $10 \times 7 = 70$.)

$10$ sides (a decagon)

Three times as many diagonals as sides

Which polygon has exactly three times as many diagonals as sides?

  1. $\dfrac{n(n-3)}{2} = 3n$.

    Translate the condition.

  2. Divide by $n$ (it isn't zero): $\dfrac{n - 3}{2} = 3$, so $n = 9$.

    Dividing out $n$ leaves a linear equation.

  3. Check: a nonagon has $\tfrac{9 \cdot 6}{2} = 27 = 3 \times 9$ diagonals.

    Always confirm with the original condition.

The nonagon ($9$ sides)

Diagonals through the centre

How many diagonals of a regular dodecagon do not pass through its centre?

  1. Total: $\dfrac{12 \cdot 9}{2} = 54$ diagonals.

    Start with all diagonals.

  2. A diagonal passes through the centre exactly when it joins opposite vertices. The $12$ vertices form $6$ opposite pairs.

    The vertices lie on a circle centred at the centre, so a chord through the centre is a diameter, joining vertices half-way around.

  3. $54 - 6 = 48$.

    Subtract the ones we don't want; counting the complement is easier.

$48$

When "not" appears, count everything and subtract.

Where diagonals cross

In a convex hexagon where no three diagonals meet at one point, how many points inside the hexagon are crossing points of two diagonals? What changes for a regular hexagon?

  1. Two crossing diagonals have four different endpoints. Conversely, any four vertices of a convex polygon form a convex quadrilateral whose two diagonals cross exactly once.

    So crossing points match up one-to-one with choices of $4$ vertices.

  2. Choosing $4$ of $6$ vertices can be done in $\dbinom{6}{4} = 15$ ways, so there are $15$ crossing points.

    Choosing which $2$ to leave out gives $\tfrac{6 \cdot 5}{2} = 15$. Chapter 25 covers this kind of counting.

  3. In a regular hexagon the three long diagonals all meet at the centre: their $3$ crossings merge into $1$ point, leaving $15 - 3 + 1 = 13$.

    The "no three meet" assumption fails there, so we correct the count.

$15$ crossing points in general; $13$ for a regular hexagon.

13.2 Angles in a Polygon

Hook

Walk around the edge of a park shaped like any convex polygon. At each corner you turn left by some amount. When you get back to the start, facing the way you began, you've turned through exactly one full circle, whether the park had $3$ corners or $300$.

Concept

Draw the $n - 3$ diagonals from one vertex of a convex $n$-gon. They cut it into $n - 2$ triangles, and the triangles' angles exactly fill the polygon's angles:

$$\text{sum of interior angles} = 180^\circ (n - 2).$$

An exterior angle is formed by one side and the extension of its neighbour; it is the amount you turn at that corner. At each vertex, interior $+$ exterior $= 180^\circ$. Adding over all $n$ vertices, the interior and exterior angles together make $180^\circ n$, so the exterior angles make $180^\circ n - 180^\circ(n - 2)$:

$$\text{sum of exterior angles} = 360^\circ.$$

For a concave polygon the interior sum is still $180^\circ(n-2)$. The exterior-angle sum is still $360^\circ$ if you count the turn at a reflex corner as negative (you turn the other way there).

Angle sum of a heptagon

Find the sum of the interior angles of a heptagon.

  1. $180^\circ(7 - 2) = 180^\circ \times 5 = 900^\circ$.

    A heptagon has $7$ sides and splits into $5$ triangles.

$900^\circ$

Exterior angles never change

Find the sum of the exterior angles of a convex $11$-gon. If it is regular, find each exterior angle.

  1. The sum is $360^\circ$.

    The exterior angles of every convex polygon add to one full turn.

  2. Regular: each is $\dfrac{360^\circ}{11} \approx 32.7^\circ$.

    Equal interior angles mean equal exterior angles, sharing $360^\circ$ equally.

$360^\circ$; each is $\tfrac{360}{11}^\circ$ if regular.

How many sides?

The interior angles of a polygon add to $1980^\circ$. How many sides does it have?

  1. $180(n - 2) = 1980 \Rightarrow n - 2 = 11 \Rightarrow n = 13$.

    Divide by $180$ to find the number of triangles, then add $2$.

$13$

The sixth angle of a hexagon

Five angles of a hexagon are $100^\circ$, $110^\circ$, $120^\circ$, $130^\circ$ and $140^\circ$. Find the sixth.

  1. Hexagon total: $180^\circ \times 4 = 720^\circ$.

    $n - 2 = 4$ triangles.

  2. Known angles: $100 + 110 + 120 + 130 + 140 = 600$, so the sixth is $720^\circ - 600^\circ = 120^\circ$.

    Subtract from the total.

$120^\circ$

Interior is four times exterior

At every vertex of a convex polygon, the interior angle is $4$ times the exterior angle. How many sides does it have?

  1. If the exterior angle is $x$, then $4x + x = 180^\circ$, so $x = 36^\circ$.

    Interior and exterior angles at a vertex are supplementary.

  2. All exterior angles equal $36^\circ$ and add to $360^\circ$, so $n = 360 / 36 = 10$.

    Exterior angles always total $360^\circ$.

$10$

Exterior angles are often the quickest route to $n$.

A pentagon's angles in a ratio

The angles of a pentagon are in the ratio $3 : 3 : 3 : 4 : 5$. Find them.

  1. Pentagon total: $180^\circ \times 3 = 540^\circ$.

    $n - 2 = 3$ triangles.

  2. $3k + 3k + 3k + 4k + 5k = 18k = 540^\circ$, so $k = 30^\circ$.

    Write each angle as a multiple of $k$.

$90^\circ, 90^\circ, 90^\circ, 120^\circ, 150^\circ$

One angle left out

The interior angles of a convex polygon, all except one, add to $1000^\circ$. How many sides does it have, and what is the missing angle?

  1. The full sum $180(n - 2)$ is more than $1000$, but by less than $180$, because the missing angle is between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$.

    A convex polygon's angles are all less than $180^\circ$.

  2. Multiples of $180$: $900, 1080, 1260, \dots$ Only $1080$ lies strictly between $1000$ and $1180$.

    The full sum must be a multiple of $180$.

  3. $180(n - 2) = 1080 \Rightarrow n = 8$, and the missing angle is $1080^\circ - 1000^\circ = 80^\circ$.

    Solve for $n$ and subtract.

$8$ sides; the missing angle is $80^\circ$.

How many acute angles?

What is the largest number of acute interior angles a convex polygon can have?

  1. An acute interior angle (less than $90^\circ$) has an exterior angle greater than $90^\circ$.

    They add to $180^\circ$.

  2. Four such exterior angles would add to more than $360^\circ$, which is impossible since all exterior angles together make exactly $360^\circ$ (and the rest are positive).

    The fixed exterior total limits how many big exterior angles fit.

  3. Three is possible: any acute triangle has three acute angles.

    Showing an example proves the bound is reached.

$3$

The $360^\circ$ exterior total is a powerful constraint for any $n$.

13.3 Regular Polygons

Hook

Stop signs are octagons, honeycomb cells are hexagons, and a 50-pence coin is a curved heptagon. Regular polygons are everywhere because they are so symmetric, and that symmetry means one small right triangle tells you everything about them.

Concept

In a regular $n$-gon all angles are equal, so each one is the total divided by $n$:

$$\text{exterior angle} = \frac{360^\circ}{n}, \qquad \text{interior angle} = \frac{180^\circ(n-2)}{n} = 180^\circ - \frac{360^\circ}{n}.$$

A few to recognise: triangle $60^\circ$, square $90^\circ$, pentagon $108^\circ$, hexagon $120^\circ$, octagon $135^\circ$, decagon $144^\circ$, dodecagon $150^\circ$.

Every regular polygon has a centre $O$, equally far from all vertices (the circumradius $R$) and from all sides (the inradius or apothem $r$). The circumscribed and inscribed circles share this centre. Joining $O$ to a vertex $A$ and to the midpoint $X$ of a side creates a right triangle $OXA$; $2n$ copies of it fill the polygon, so the angle at $O$ is $\theta = \dfrac{360^\circ}{2n} = \dfrac{180^\circ}{n}$. With side $l$:

$$\tan\theta = \frac{l/2}{r}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{l/2}{R}, \qquad \text{Area} = 2n \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac{l}{2} \cdot r = \frac{n\,l\,r}{2} = \frac12 (\text{perimeter})(r).$$

Angles of a regular 15-gon

Find each exterior and interior angle of a regular $15$-gon.

  1. Exterior: $\dfrac{360^\circ}{15} = 24^\circ$.

    Exterior angles share $360^\circ$ equally.

  2. Interior: $180^\circ - 24^\circ = 156^\circ$.

    Interior and exterior are supplementary; this is quicker than $\tfrac{180 \cdot 13}{15}$.

Exterior $24^\circ$, interior $156^\circ$.

From angle to number of sides

Each interior angle of a regular polygon is $140^\circ$. How many sides does it have?

  1. Each exterior angle is $180^\circ - 140^\circ = 40^\circ$.

    Switch to exterior angles; their total is fixed.

  2. $n = \dfrac{360}{40} = 9$.

    Nine equal turns of $40^\circ$ make a full circle.

$9$

Next in the book: Exercise 13-1.

Which angles are possible?

Can a regular polygon have interior angles of $150^\circ$? Of $130^\circ$?

  1. $150^\circ$: exterior $30^\circ$, and $360 / 30 = 12$. Yes, the regular dodecagon.

    We need $360$ divided by the exterior angle to be a whole number.

  2. $130^\circ$: exterior $50^\circ$, and $360 / 50 = 7.2$. Not a whole number, so no.

    A polygon can't have $7.2$ sides.

$150^\circ$ yes ($12$ sides); $130^\circ$ no.

Check the formulas on a square

A square has side $10$. Use $\theta = 180^\circ / n$ to find $r$ and $R$, then check that $\tfrac12(\text{perimeter})(r)$ gives the area.

  1. $\theta = 45^\circ$. $\tan 45^\circ = \dfrac{5}{r}$ gives $r = 5$.

    Half the side is $5$; the apothem reaches the middle of a side.

  2. $\sin 45^\circ = \dfrac{5}{R}$ gives $R = \dfrac{5}{\sqrt2/2} = 5\sqrt2$.

    $R$ is half the diagonal, which matches $\tfrac12 \cdot 10\sqrt2$.

  3. $\tfrac12 \cdot 40 \cdot 5 = 100 = 10^2$. ✓

    The general formula agrees with what we already know.

$r = 5$, $R = 5\sqrt2$, area $100$.

The equilateral triangle

An equilateral triangle has side $6$. Find its inradius, circumradius and area.

  1. $\theta = 60^\circ$, half-side $3$. $\tan 60^\circ = \dfrac{3}{r} \Rightarrow r = \dfrac{3}{\sqrt3} = \sqrt3$.

    Right triangle $OXA$ with the $60^\circ$ angle at the centre.

  2. $\sin 60^\circ = \dfrac{3}{R} \Rightarrow R = \dfrac{3}{\sqrt3/2} = 2\sqrt3$.

    Note $R = 2r$: the centre is the centroid, two-thirds of the way down each median.

  3. Area $= \tfrac12 \cdot 18 \cdot \sqrt3 = 9\sqrt3$.

    Matches $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} \cdot 6^2 = 9\sqrt3$.

$r = \sqrt3$, $R = 2\sqrt3$, area $9\sqrt3$.

A dodecagon from its circumradius

A regular dodecagon is inscribed in a circle of radius $4$. Find its area.

  1. Joining $O$ to all $12$ vertices makes $12$ congruent isosceles triangles with two sides $4$ and apex angle $\dfrac{360^\circ}{12} = 30^\circ$.

    Here we know $R$, so whole triangles from the centre are simplest.

  2. Each has area $\tfrac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 4 \cdot \sin 30^\circ = 4$.

    Triangle area $\tfrac12 ab\sin C$.

  3. Total: $12 \times 4 = 48$.

    In general this gives area $3R^2$ for a regular dodecagon.

$48$

The width of an octagon

A regular octagon has side $4$. Find the distance between two opposite (parallel) sides.

  1. Extend four alternate sides to form a square. Each cut-off corner is a right triangle with hypotenuse $4$.

    Each interior angle is $135^\circ$, so each corner triangle has angles $45^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $90^\circ$.

  2. Its legs are $x = \dfrac{4}{\sqrt2} = 2\sqrt2$.

    In a $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle, hypotenuse $=$ leg $\times\sqrt2$.

  3. Width $= x + 4 + x = 4 + 4\sqrt2$.

    The square's side is the distance between opposite octagon sides.

$4 + 4\sqrt2$

Octagons live inside squares with their corners clipped.

A square on a pentagon

Regular pentagon $ABCDE$ has square $ABFG$ built outward on side $AB$. Find $\angle AEG$.

  1. At $A$: $\angle EAB = 108^\circ$ (pentagon) and $\angle BAG = 90^\circ$ (square).

    Regular pentagon interior angle: $180^\circ - 72^\circ = 108^\circ$.

  2. Going around $A$ the other way: $\angle EAG = 360^\circ - 108^\circ - 90^\circ = 162^\circ$.

    The three angles around point $A$ make a full turn.

  3. $AE = AB = AG$, so triangle $AEG$ is isosceles and $\angle AEG = \dfrac{180^\circ - 162^\circ}{2} = 9^\circ$.

    The pentagon and square share side $AB$, so all three lengths match.

$9^\circ$

Shared sides of regular polygons create isosceles triangles.

13.4 Regular Hexagons

Hook

Bees build hexagonal cells, and so do soap bubbles squeezed between two plates. Mathematicians like hexagons for a simpler reason: chop one up from the centre and every piece is an equilateral triangle, so all the numbers come out friendly.

Concept

Join the centre $O$ of a regular hexagon to its six vertices. Each central angle is $360^\circ / 6 = 60^\circ$, and $OA = OB$, so each of the six triangles is isosceles with a $60^\circ$ apex, which makes it equilateral. So the circumradius equals the side: $R = s$. Everything follows:

$$[ABCDEF] = 6 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{4}s^2 = \frac{3\sqrt3}{2}s^2, \qquad AD = 2s, \qquad AC = s\sqrt3, \qquad r = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}s.$$

The long diagonals (like $AD$) pass through the centre and are two sides long. The short diagonals (like $AC$) are $s\sqrt3$: in triangle $ACD$, $AD$ is a diameter of the circumcircle, so $\angle ACD = 90^\circ$ and $ACD$ is a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle.

Attack hexagon problems by cutting them into equilateral triangles, or into $30$-$60$-$90$ triangles.

Area from the side

Find the area of a regular hexagon with side $4$.

  1. Each equilateral triangle has area $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{4} \cdot 16 = 4\sqrt3$.

    The hexagon is six equilateral triangles of side $4$.

  2. Total: $6 \times 4\sqrt3 = 24\sqrt3$.

    Same as $\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2} \cdot 16$.

$24\sqrt3$

Long diagonal to perimeter

The longest diagonal of a regular hexagon is $14$. Find its perimeter.

  1. Long diagonal $= 2s = 14$, so $s = 7$.

    It runs through the centre, across two equilateral triangles.

  2. Perimeter $= 6 \times 7 = 42$.

    Six equal sides.

$42$

The short diagonal

Find the length of diagonal $AC$ in regular hexagon $ABCDEF$ with side $5$.

  1. In triangle $ABC$, $AB = BC = 5$ and $\angle ABC = 120^\circ$, so the base angles are $30^\circ$.

    Isosceles triangle with a $120^\circ$ apex.

  2. The altitude from $B$ splits it into two $30$-$60$-$90$ triangles with hypotenuse $5$, so half of $AC$ is $\tfrac{5\sqrt3}{2}$.

    The side opposite $60^\circ$ is $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ of the hypotenuse.

  3. $AC = 5\sqrt3$.

    Double the half.

$5\sqrt3$

From area to apothem

A regular hexagon has area $54\sqrt3$. Find its inradius (apothem).

  1. $\dfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}s^2 = 54\sqrt3 \Rightarrow s^2 = 36 \Rightarrow s = 6$.

    Solve the area formula for $s$.

  2. $r = \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \cdot 6 = 3\sqrt3$.

    The apothem is the height of one equilateral triangle.

$3\sqrt3$

Triangle $ACE$ is half

In regular hexagon $ABCDEF$, what fraction of the hexagon's area does triangle $ACE$ cover?

  1. $ACE$ is equilateral with side $s\sqrt3$ (three short diagonals), so its area is $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{4} \cdot 3s^2 = \dfrac{3\sqrt3}{4}s^2$.

    All three sides are short diagonals of the same length.

  2. $\dfrac{3\sqrt3 s^2/4}{3\sqrt3 s^2/2} = \dfrac12$.

    Divide by the hexagon's area.

  3. A picture proof: each of the three triangles cut off ($ABC$, $CDE$, $EFA$) is congruent to one of the three pieces $OAC$, $OCE$, $OEA$ of $ACE$.

    Each cut-off triangle has sides $s$, $s$ and angle $120^\circ$, exactly like $OAC$.

$\dfrac12$

A right triangle hiding in a hexagon

Regular hexagon $ABCDEF$ has side $6$. Find the area of triangle $ABD$.

  1. $AD$ is a diameter of the circumcircle (it passes through $O$), and $B$ is on that circle, so $\angle ABD = 90^\circ$.

    An angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle.

  2. $AB = 6$ and $AD = 12$, so $BD = \sqrt{144 - 36} = 6\sqrt3$.

    Pythagoras (or: $BD$ is a short diagonal, $s\sqrt3$).

  3. Area $= \tfrac12 \cdot 6 \cdot 6\sqrt3 = 18\sqrt3$.

    The legs of a right triangle are a base and its height.

$18\sqrt3$

To the middle of a far side

Regular hexagon $ABCDEF$ has side $1$, and $M$ is the midpoint of $CD$. Find $AM$.

  1. $\angle ACD = 90^\circ$, because $AD$ is a diameter of the circumcircle.

    The same fact as in the previous example, now with $C$.

  2. $AC = \sqrt3$ (short diagonal) and $CM = \tfrac12$.

    $M$ is halfway along a side of length $1$.

  3. $AM = \sqrt{3 + \tfrac14} = \sqrt{\tfrac{13}{4}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{13}}{2}$.

    Pythagoras in right triangle $ACM$.

$\dfrac{\sqrt{13}}{2}$

Long diagonals are diameters: look for the right angles they create.
