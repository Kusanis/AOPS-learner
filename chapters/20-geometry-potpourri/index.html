Chapter 20 · Geometry

A Potpourri of Geometry

In the book this chapter is a single large set of mixed problems, the kind where you are not told which tool to use. This page groups the ideas those problems lean on most into four themes: circles that touch, right triangles hiding in plain sight, areas found by more than one route, and reflections that turn bent paths straight. Each worked example practises recognising which tool a figure is asking for.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact. Draw that radius.
  • Tangent circles: the centers and the point of contact are collinear; the distance between centers is $r_1 + r_2$ (outside) or $|r_1 - r_2|$ (inside).
  • Common tangent lengths: external $\sqrt{d^2 - (r_1 - r_2)^2}$, internal $\sqrt{d^2 - (r_1 + r_2)^2}$. Slide the tangent to the center to see the right triangle.
  • Right triangles: the perpendicular from the center bisects a chord; an angle inscribed in a semicircle is right; the altitude to the hypotenuse gives $h^2 = pq$ and $h = \frac{ab}{c}$.
  • Inradius: $r = \dfrac{\text{Area}}{s}$; for a right triangle, also $r = \dfrac{a + b - c}{2}$.
  • Areas: triangles with the same height have areas in the ratio of their bases; compute an area two ways to find a length; perpendicular diagonals give area $\frac12 d_1 d_2$.
  • Shortest paths that touch a line: reflect one endpoint across the line and draw a straight segment.

20.1 Circles That Touch

Hook

A bicycle chain wraps around two gears of different sizes. The straight runs of chain are common tangents to two circles, and the curved parts hug arcs of the gears. How long is the chain? Every problem about touching circles starts the same way: draw the radii to the points of contact and look for right triangles.

Concept

Two facts do almost all the work:

1. A tangent line is perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of tangency.

2. If two circles are tangent, their centers and the point of contact lie on one line, so the distance between centers is $r_1 + r_2$ (externally tangent) or $|r_1 - r_2|$ (internally tangent).

A line tangent to both circles is a common tangent: external if both circles lie on the same side of it, internal if it passes between them. To find the length between the two points of tangency, draw both radii (they are parallel, since both are perpendicular to the tangent), then slide the tangent segment over until it starts at one center. That makes a right triangle with hypotenuse $d$, the distance between centers:

$$\text{external: } \sqrt{d^2 - (r_1 - r_2)^2}, \qquad \text{internal: } \sqrt{d^2 - (r_1 + r_2)^2}.$$

The number of common tangents depends only on $d$ compared with $r_1 + r_2$ and $|r_1 - r_2|$: four when the circles are apart, three when externally tangent, two when they cross, one when internally tangent, none when one is inside the other.

Distance between centers

Circles of radius $3$ and $5$ are tangent. How far apart are their centers?

  1. Externally tangent (side by side): $d = 3 + 5 = 8$.

    The contact point lies on the line of centers, between them, so the two radii add.

  2. Internally tangent (small one inside, touching): $d = 5 - 3 = 2$.

    Now both centers are on the same side of the contact point, so the radii subtract.

$8$ if externally tangent, $2$ if internally tangent.

Counting common tangents

Circles have radii $4$ and $6$. How many common tangents are there if the distance between centers is $12$, $10$, $5$, $2$, or $1$?

  1. Compare with $r_1 + r_2 = 10$ and $|r_1 - r_2| = 2$.

    These two numbers mark the moments when the circles touch.

  2. $d = 12 > 10$: apart, $4$ tangents. $d = 10$: externally tangent, $3$. $2 < d = 5 < 10$: crossing, $2$.

    When the circles touch from outside, the two internal tangents merge into one line through the contact point.

  3. $d = 2$: internally tangent, $1$. $d = 1 < 2$: one inside the other, $0$.

    A line tangent to the inner circle cuts through the outer one.

$4, 3, 2, 1, 0$

An external tangent

Circles of radius $9$ and $4$ have centers $13$ apart. Find the length of a common external tangent between its points of tangency.

  1. Draw radii $O_1T_1$ and $O_2T_2$ to the points of tangency; both are perpendicular to $T_1T_2$, so $O_1T_1T_2O_2$ is a right trapezoid.

    Two lines perpendicular to the same line are parallel.

  2. From $O_2$, draw a segment parallel to $T_1T_2$ to meet $O_1T_1$. It has length $T_1T_2$ and makes a right triangle with legs $T_1T_2$ and $9 - 4 = 5$, hypotenuse $13$.

    The parallel cuts off a rectangle, leaving $r_1 - r_2$ of the bigger radius.

  3. $T_1T_2 = \sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = 12$.

    A $5$-$12$-$13$ right triangle.

$12$

An internal tangent

Circles of radius $2$ and $3$ have centers $10$ apart. Find the length of a common internal tangent between its points of tangency.

  1. Now the radii point in opposite directions from the tangent. Slide the tangent segment to start at $O_2$; it meets the extension of $O_1T_1$ at distance $2 + 3 = 5$ from $O_1$.

    For an internal tangent, the circles are on opposite sides, so the radii add.

  2. Length $= \sqrt{10^2 - 5^2} = \sqrt{75} = 5\sqrt3$.

    Right triangle with hypotenuse $d = 10$ and one leg $r_1 + r_2 = 5$.

$5\sqrt3$

Three coins

Three circles of radii $1$, $2$ and $3$ are each externally tangent to the other two. Find the area of the triangle formed by their centers.

  1. Sides: $1 + 2 = 3$, $1 + 3 = 4$, $2 + 3 = 5$.

    Each side joins two tangent centers, so it is the sum of their radii.

  2. $3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$, so it is a right triangle with area $\tfrac12 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 6$.

    Recognise the Pythagorean triple; the legs are perpendicular.

$6$

A circle in the corner

A circle is inscribed in a square of side $2$. A smaller circle sits in one corner, tangent to two sides of the square and to the big circle. Find its radius.

  1. Put the corner at the origin. The big circle has center $(1, 1)$ and radius $1$; the small circle, tangent to both sides, has center $(r, r)$.

    A circle tangent to both axes has its center equally far from each, on the diagonal.

  2. The circles are externally tangent, so the distance between centers is $1 + r$. That distance is along the diagonal: $\sqrt2\,(1 - r)$.

    Both centers lie on the line $y = x$, and moving $(1 - r)$ in each direction covers $\sqrt2(1 - r)$.

  3. $\sqrt2 - \sqrt2\,r = 1 + r$, so $r = \dfrac{\sqrt2 - 1}{\sqrt2 + 1} = (\sqrt2 - 1)^2 = 3 - 2\sqrt2$.

    Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt2 - 1$ to rationalize.

$3 - 2\sqrt2 \approx 0.17$

Next in the book: Problems 375 and 380 use the same idea.

A belt around two pulleys

A tight belt goes around two pulleys of radius $3$ and $1$ whose centers are $4$ apart (the belt does not cross itself). Find the length of the belt.

  1. Each straight part is an external tangent: $\sqrt{4^2 - (3 - 1)^2} = \sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt3$. There are two, giving $4\sqrt3$.

    The external tangent formula.

  2. In that right triangle the leg $2$ is half the hypotenuse $4$, so the radii make a $60^\circ$ angle with the line of centers.

    A right triangle with one leg half the hypotenuse is a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle.

  3. On the big pulley the belt covers $360^\circ - 2 \cdot 60^\circ = 240^\circ$: length $\tfrac{240}{360} \cdot 2\pi \cdot 3 = 4\pi$. On the small pulley it covers $360^\circ - 2 \cdot 120^\circ = 120^\circ$: length $\tfrac{120}{360} \cdot 2\pi = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}$.

    The radii to the tangent points are parallel, so on the small pulley they make $180^\circ - 60^\circ = 120^\circ$ with the line pointing back to the big center.

  4. Total: $4\sqrt3 + 4\pi + \tfrac{2\pi}{3} = 4\sqrt3 + \tfrac{14\pi}{3}$.

    Add the straight and curved parts. Check: the two arcs, $240^\circ + 120^\circ = 360^\circ$, make one full turn in total.

$4\sqrt3 + \dfrac{14\pi}{3} \approx 21.6$

Radii to tangent points are parallel; the angles they make decide the arcs.

20.2 Right Triangles Everywhere

Hook

Many geometry problems look as if they have nothing to do with right triangles, until you draw one extra segment: a radius, a perpendicular, a diameter. Then a right triangle appears and Pythagoras finishes the job. Learning which segment to draw is most of the skill.

Concept

Ways a right angle sneaks into a figure:

The perpendicular from a circle's center to a chord bisects the chord: half-chord, distance and radius form a right triangle.

An angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle (it cuts off an arc of $180^\circ$).

A tangent meets the radius at a right angle.

In a right triangle with legs $a, b$, hypotenuse $c$ and altitude $h$ to the hypotenuse, splitting it into pieces $p$ and $q$, all three triangles are similar. That gives

$$h = \frac{ab}{c}, \qquad h^2 = pq, \qquad a^2 = qc, \qquad b^2 = pc$$

(with $q$ the piece next to leg $a$, and $p$ the piece next to leg $b$).

For the inscribed circle, the area of any triangle equals $rs$ (split it into three triangles from the incenter), so $r = \frac{\text{Area}}{s}$. In a right triangle the tangent lengths also give $r = \frac{a + b - c}{2}$.

The formula $h = \frac{ab}{c}$ is just the area computed two ways: $\frac12 ab = \frac12 ch$.

How far is the chord?

A chord of length $24$ lies in a circle of radius $13$. How far is it from the center?

  1. The perpendicular from the center bisects the chord, giving a right triangle with hypotenuse $13$ and one leg $12$.

    Radius to an endpoint, half the chord, and the distance form a right triangle.

  2. Distance $= \sqrt{13^2 - 12^2} = 5$.

    A $5$-$12$-$13$ triangle.

$5$

The altitude to the hypotenuse

A right triangle has legs $6$ and $8$. Find the altitude to the hypotenuse and the two pieces it cuts the hypotenuse into.

  1. Hypotenuse $c = 10$. Area two ways: $\tfrac12 \cdot 6 \cdot 8 = \tfrac12 \cdot 10 \cdot h$, so $h = 4.8$.

    Either leg can serve as a base for the other; the hypotenuse is a base for $h$.

  2. Piece next to leg $6$: $\tfrac{6^2}{10} = 3.6$. Piece next to leg $8$: $\tfrac{8^2}{10} = 6.4$.

    From $a^2 = qc$: each leg squared equals the hypotenuse times the adjacent piece.

  3. Check: $3.6 + 6.4 = 10$ and $3.6 \times 6.4 = 23.04 = 4.8^2$.

    The relation $h^2 = pq$ confirms the numbers.

$h = 4.8$; pieces $3.6$ and $6.4$.

An inradius

Find the inradius of the right triangle with sides $9$, $40$, $41$.

  1. Area $= \tfrac12 \cdot 9 \cdot 40 = 180$, semiperimeter $s = \tfrac{90}{2} = 45$.

    The legs are perpendicular, so they serve as base and height.

  2. $r = \tfrac{180}{45} = 4$.

    Area $= rs$. Check with $\tfrac{a + b - c}{2} = \tfrac{9 + 40 - 41}{2} = 4$. ✓

$4$

Circles of an equilateral triangle

An equilateral triangle has side $12$. Find the radii of its inscribed and circumscribed circles.

  1. Altitude $= \sqrt{12^2 - 6^2} = 6\sqrt3$.

    The altitude bisects the base, making a right triangle with legs $6$ and $h$.

  2. In an equilateral triangle the incenter, circumcenter and centroid coincide, and the centroid is $\tfrac23$ of the way down each median. So $R = \tfrac23 \cdot 6\sqrt3 = 4\sqrt3$ and $r = \tfrac13 \cdot 6\sqrt3 = 2\sqrt3$.

    The altitude is also a median, and the centroid splits medians $2 : 1$.

$r = 2\sqrt3$, $R = 4\sqrt3$.

The sliding ladder

A $25$-foot ladder leans against a wall with its foot $7$ feet from the wall. The top slides down $4$ feet. How far does the foot slide out?

  1. Top height at first: $\sqrt{25^2 - 7^2} = 24$.

    Wall, ground and ladder form a right triangle.

  2. New height $20$, so the new distance is $\sqrt{25^2 - 20^2} = 15$.

    The ladder's length is unchanged; it is still the hypotenuse.

  3. The foot moves $15 - 7 = 8$ feet.

    Notice the top moved $4$ but the foot moved $8$: the changes are not equal.

$8$ feet

Two parallel chords

Parallel chords of length $10$ and $24$ lie on opposite sides of the center of a circle, $17$ apart. Find the radius.

  1. Let the distances from the center be $d_1$ (to the chord of length $10$) and $d_2$. Then $d_1 + d_2 = 17$, and $r^2 = 5^2 + d_1^2 = 12^2 + d_2^2$.

    Each chord gives a right triangle with its half-chord; the chords are on opposite sides, so the distances add.

  2. $d_1^2 - d_2^2 = 144 - 25 = 119$, and $d_1^2 - d_2^2 = (d_1 + d_2)(d_1 - d_2) = 17(d_1 - d_2)$, so $d_1 - d_2 = 7$.

    Difference of squares turns two equations into a linear pair.

  3. $d_1 = 12$, $d_2 = 5$, and $r = \sqrt{25 + 144} = 13$.

    Check the other chord: $\sqrt{144 + 25} = 13$. ✓

$13$

Incenter to circumcenter

In a right triangle with sides $5$, $12$, $13$, find the distance between the center of the inscribed circle and the center of the circumscribed circle.

  1. Place the right angle at the origin, the leg of length $12$ along the $x$-axis and the leg of length $5$ along the $y$-axis: vertices $(0, 0)$, $(12, 0)$, $(0, 5)$.

    Coordinates work well when there is a right angle to put the axes on (Chapter 16).

  2. Inradius $r = \tfrac{5 + 12 - 13}{2} = 2$. The incircle touches both axes, so the incenter is $(2, 2)$.

    The incenter is $r$ away from each leg.

  3. The circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse: $(6, 2.5)$.

    The hypotenuse is a diameter of the circumcircle, because the right angle is inscribed in a semicircle.

  4. Distance $= \sqrt{(6 - 2)^2 + (2.5 - 2)^2} = \sqrt{16.25} = \dfrac{\sqrt{65}}{2}$.

    Distance formula.

$\dfrac{\sqrt{65}}{2} \approx 4.03$

Mix tools: a right angle invites coordinates, and the semicircle fact locates the circumcenter.

20.3 Areas by More Than One Route

Hook

Push a triangle's top vertex sideways, keeping it at the same height, and the triangle leans over like a tipped-up book. It looks completely different, yet its area never changes. Area is stubborn in useful ways, and those ways let you find lengths and areas that seem out of reach.

Concept

Three habits for area problems:

1. Same height: triangles with the same height have areas in the ratio of their bases. Triangles with the same base and height have equal area.

2. Two ways: compute one area with two different base-height pairs, then solve for the unknown length.

3. Split or surround: cut a region into pieces you know, or enclose it in a rectangle and subtract the corners.

Useful consequences: a median splits a triangle into two equal areas; the three medians split it into six equal areas; a quadrilateral with perpendicular diagonals $d_1, d_2$ has area $\frac12 d_1 d_2$; a trapezoid's area is its height times its median (the average of the bases).

If $D$ is on side $AB$ and $E$ on side $AC$, then $\dfrac{[ADE]}{[ABC]} = \dfrac{AD}{AB} \cdot \dfrac{AE}{AC}$. Square brackets $[\;]$ denote area.

Splitting by a cevian

Point $D$ lies on side $BC$ of $\triangle ABC$ with $BD = 3$ and $DC = 5$. If $[ABC] = 40$, find $[ABD]$.

  1. $\triangle ABD$ and $\triangle ABC$ share the height from $A$ to line $BC$.

    Same height means the areas are in the ratio of the bases.

  2. $[ABD] = \tfrac{3}{8} \cdot 40 = 15$.

    $BD$ is $3$ of the $8$ units of $BC$.

$15$

A length from an area

A right triangle has sides $15$, $20$, $25$. Find the altitude to the hypotenuse.

  1. Area with the legs: $\tfrac12 \cdot 15 \cdot 20 = 150$.

    The legs are a base-height pair.

  2. Area with the hypotenuse: $\tfrac12 \cdot 25 \cdot h = 150$, so $h = 12$.

    Same area, different base: solve for the height.

$12$

Triangle versus trapezoid

A triangle and a trapezoid have the same area and the same height. The triangle's base is $14$. Find the trapezoid's median.

  1. Triangle: $\tfrac12 \cdot 14 \cdot h = 7h$. Trapezoid: $m \cdot h$, where $m$ is the median.

    Trapezoid area = average of the bases times height, and the median is that average.

  2. $7h = mh$, so $m = 7$.

    The common height cancels.

$7$

Perpendicular diagonals

A rhombus has diagonals $10$ and $24$. Find its area and side length.

  1. The diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular, so the area is $\tfrac12 \cdot 10 \cdot 24 = 120$.

    The diagonals cut it into four right triangles that rearrange into half of a $10 \times 24$ rectangle.

  2. The diagonals bisect each other, so each right triangle has legs $5$ and $12$ and hypotenuse (the side) $13$.

    Pythagoras on one of the four triangles.

Area $120$, side $13$.

Six equal pieces

The medians of $\triangle ABC$, of area $36$, meet at the centroid $G$. Let $M$, $N$, $P$ be the midpoints of $BC$, $AB$, $AC$. Find $[GBC]$ and the area of quadrilateral $ANGP$.

  1. The three medians cut the triangle into six small triangles of equal area, each $36 / 6 = 6$.

    The two small triangles on either side of a midpoint have equal bases and the same height from $G$, so they match in pairs; and each median halves the whole triangle, which forces all three pairs to be equal.

  2. $\triangle GBC$ is made of two of them, $\triangle GBM$ and $\triangle GMC$: $[GBC] = 12$.

    The median from $A$ passes through $G$ and $M$, splitting $\triangle GBC$ in two.

  3. Quadrilateral $ANGP$ is made of $\triangle ANG$ and $\triangle AGP$, two more of the pieces: area $12$.

    Segment $AG$ lies along the median from $A$ and splits the quadrilateral into two of the six small triangles.

$[GBC] = 12$ and $[ANGP] = 12$.

A trapezoid with perpendicular diagonals

An isosceles trapezoid has bases $10$ and $6$, and its diagonals are perpendicular. Find its area.

  1. Let the diagonals meet at $E$. By symmetry, $E$ is equally far from the two ends of each base, so the triangles $E$ makes with each base are isosceles right triangles.

    In an isosceles trapezoid the diagonals are equal and cut each other into matching pieces; with a right angle at $E$, the base angles are $45^\circ$.

  2. An isosceles right triangle's height to its hypotenuse is half the hypotenuse: $5$ for the long base and $3$ for the short base. The trapezoid's height is $5 + 3 = 8$.

    The two heights from $E$ lie on the same line (the axis of symmetry).

  3. Area $= \tfrac{10 + 6}{2} \cdot 8 = 64$.

    Check: each diagonal is $5\sqrt2 + 3\sqrt2 = 8\sqrt2$, and $\tfrac12 (8\sqrt2)^2 = 64$. ✓

$64$

Next in the book: Problems 351 and 399.

A corner triangle

In $\triangle ABC$ of area $30$, $D$ is on $AB$ with $AD : DB = 2 : 3$, and $E$ is the midpoint of $AC$. Find $[ADE]$.

  1. $[ABE] = \tfrac12 [ABC] = 15$.

    $BE$ is a median: triangles $ABE$ and $CBE$ have equal bases $AE = EC$ and the same height from $B$.

  2. Inside $\triangle ABE$, triangles $ADE$ and $DBE$ share the height from $E$ to $AB$, so $[ADE] = \tfrac{2}{5} \cdot 15 = 6$.

    $AD$ is $2$ of the $5$ parts of $AB$.

$6$

Chain same-height ratios: $[ADE] = \frac{AD}{AB} \cdot \frac{AE}{AC} \cdot [ABC]$.

Surround and subtract

Find the area of the triangle with vertices $(1, 1)$, $(7, 3)$, $(3, 8)$.

  1. Enclose it in the rectangle $1 \le x \le 7$, $1 \le y \le 8$: area $6 \times 7 = 42$.

    The triangle touches all four sides of this box.

  2. The three right-triangle corners outside it have areas $\tfrac12 \cdot 6 \cdot 2 = 6$, $\tfrac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 = 10$ and $\tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot 7 = 7$.

    Each corner triangle has legs along the box: for example, from $(1, 1)$ to $(7, 3)$ is $6$ across and $2$ up.

  3. $42 - (6 + 10 + 7) = 19$.

    What is left after removing the corners is the triangle.

$19$

20.4 Symmetry and Shortest Paths

Hook

You are camping, and before walking back to your tent you must fetch water from a straight river. Where along the bank should you go to make the walk as short as possible? Picture the river as a mirror: the trip to the tent's reflection is a straight line, and straight lines are shortest.

Concept

Reflecting across a line $\ell$ preserves distances, and every point $P$ of $\ell$ is equally far from $B$ and from its mirror image $B'$. So for any path $A \to P \to B$ with $P$ on $\ell$,

$$AP + PB = AP + PB' \ \ge\ AB',$$

with equality exactly when $P$ lies on segment $AB'$ (here $A$ and $B$ are on the same side of $\ell$).

The best path bounces off $\ell$ like light off a mirror: the angles it makes with $\ell$ on the way in and out are equal. For a path that must touch two lines, reflect twice.

Symmetry also explains folding: when paper is folded so that point $A$ lands on point $C$, the crease is the perpendicular bisector of $AC$, because every point of the crease is equally far from $A$ and $C$.

Reflections are studied in full in Chapter 19. In coordinates, reflecting in the $x$-axis just flips the sign of $y$.

A point and its mirror image

Reflect $B = (3, 5)$ in the $x$-axis. Show that the point $P = (7, 0)$ is equally far from $B$ and its image.

  1. $B' = (3, -5)$.

    Reflecting in the $x$-axis keeps $x$ and flips the sign of $y$.

  2. $PB = \sqrt{4^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{41}$ and $PB' = \sqrt{4^2 + (-5)^2} = \sqrt{41}$.

    The mirror line is the perpendicular bisector of $BB'$, so every point on it is equidistant from $B$ and $B'$.

$B' = (3, -5)$, and $PB = PB' = \sqrt{41}$.

The river problem

Camp is at $A = (0, 3)$ and the tent at $B = (8, 3)$; the river is the $x$-axis. Find the shortest walk from camp to the river to the tent.

  1. Reflect the tent: $B' = (8, -3)$.

    Any path $A \to P \to B$ has the same length as $A \to P \to B'$.

  2. Shortest: $AB' = \sqrt{8^2 + 6^2} = 10$.

    A straight segment is the shortest path from $A$ to $B'$.

  3. The segment crosses the $x$-axis halfway, at $P = (4, 0)$.

    $A$ is $3$ above and $B'$ is $3$ below, so by symmetry the crossing is in the middle.

$10$, fetching water at $(4, 0)$.

An uneven river trip

Now $A = (1, 2)$ and $B = (7, 6)$, with the river along the $x$-axis. Find the shortest walk and where to touch the river.

  1. $B' = (7, -6)$, so the shortest length is $\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10$.

    Reflect, then measure straight.

  2. The segment from $(1, 2)$ to $(7, -6)$ has slope $-\tfrac43$. It drops $2$ to reach $y = 0$, which takes a run of $2 \cdot \tfrac34 = 1.5$: $P = (2.5, 0)$.

    Walk along the line from $A$ until $y = 0$.

$10$, touching the river at $(2.5, 0)$.

A bank shot

A ball at $(2, 3)$ is aimed at the cushion along the $x$-axis so that it bounces and hits a ball at $(6, 1)$. Where should it hit the cushion, and how far does it travel?

  1. Aim at the mirror image $(6, -1)$. The line from $(2, 3)$ has slope $\tfrac{-4}{4} = -1$ and reaches $y = 0$ at $x = 5$.

    A ball bounces with equal angles, which is exactly the reflected straight path.

  2. Distance $= \sqrt{4^2 + 4^2} = 4\sqrt2$.

    The bent path has the same length as the straight one to the image.

Hit $(5, 0)$; the ball travels $4\sqrt2$.

Touching two walls

Walk from $A = (1, 4)$ to the $y$-axis, then to the $x$-axis, then to $B = (5, 2)$. Find the shortest such walk.

  1. Reflect $A$ in the $y$-axis: $A' = (-1, 4)$. Reflect $B$ in the $x$-axis: $B' = (5, -2)$.

    The first leg touches the $y$-axis, so mirror $A$ there; the last leg touches the $x$-axis, so mirror $B$ there.

  2. Length $\ge A'B' = \sqrt{6^2 + 6^2} = 6\sqrt2$.

    The three legs unfold into a path from $A'$ to $B'$.

  3. Line $A'B'$ is $y = 3 - x$: it crosses the $y$-axis at $(0, 3)$ and the $x$-axis at $(3, 0)$, in the right order. Check: $\sqrt2 + 3\sqrt2 + 2\sqrt2 = 6\sqrt2$.

    The crossing points must lie on the correct parts of the walls for the unfolded path to be a real walk.

$6\sqrt2$, via $(0, 3)$ and $(3, 0)$.

Distances to the sides

$P$ is any point inside an equilateral triangle of side $2$. Show that the sum of its distances to the three sides is always $\sqrt3$.

  1. Join $P$ to the three vertices, splitting the triangle into three triangles with bases $2$ (the sides) and heights $d_1, d_2, d_3$ (the distances).

    Split the region into pieces whose areas involve the unknown distances.

  2. Total area: $\tfrac12 \cdot 2 (d_1 + d_2 + d_3) = d_1 + d_2 + d_3$.

    The three bases are equal, so the areas combine neatly.

  3. But the area is also $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} \cdot 2^2 = \sqrt3$. So $d_1 + d_2 + d_3 = \sqrt3$, the altitude.

    Area computed two ways, using the symmetry that all three sides are equal.

The sum is always $\sqrt3$, the height of the triangle.

The crease of a fold

A $8 \times 6$ rectangle is folded so that two opposite corners meet. How long is the crease?

  1. Place corners at $A = (0, 0)$ and $C = (8, 6)$. The crease is the perpendicular bisector of $AC$: it passes through the midpoint $(4, 3)$ with slope $-\tfrac43$.

    Folding $A$ onto $C$ means every point of the crease is equally far from both, and $AC$ has slope $\tfrac34$.

  2. At $y = 0$: $-3 = -\tfrac43(x - 4)$, so $x = \tfrac{25}{4}$. At $y = 6$: $3 = -\tfrac43(x - 4)$, so $x = \tfrac74$.

    The crease runs across the paper between those two edges.

  3. Length $= \sqrt{\left(\tfrac{25}{4} - \tfrac74\right)^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{\tfrac{81}{4} + 36} = \sqrt{\tfrac{225}{4}} = \tfrac{15}{2}$.
