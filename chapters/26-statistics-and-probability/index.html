Chapter 26 · Advanced Topics

Statistics and Probability

A pile of numbers means little until you summarize it, and a game of chance looks hopeless until you count its outcomes. This chapter gives you the tools for both: averages that describe data, and probabilities, odds and expected values that describe luck.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • Mean $=\dfrac{\text{sum}}{\text{count}}$, so sum $=$ mean $\times$ count. Work with sums, not averages, when data are added or removed.
  • Median: the middle value of the sorted list (average the two middle values for an even count). Mode: the most frequent value. Range: largest minus smallest.
  • Geometric mean of $n$ positive numbers: the $n$th root of their product. For two numbers, $\dfrac{a+b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab}$.
  • With equally likely outcomes, $P(A) = \dfrac{\text{desired outcomes}}{\text{all outcomes}}$, and $P(A) = 1 - P(\text{not } A)$.
  • Independent events multiply: $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B)$. Without replacement, update the counts before the next factor.
  • Casework: split into non-overlapping cases, find each probability, then add.
  • Odds against $=P(\text{not }A) : P(A)$; odds against $m:n$ means $P(A) = \dfrac{n}{m+n}$.
  • Expected value $=\sum (\text{value}) \times (\text{probability})$: the long-run average result.

26.1 Statistics

Hook

Ten friends compare weekly allowances. Nine of them get 10 dollars, and one gets 500 dollars. The "average" allowance is 59 dollars, but nobody actually gets anything close to that. Which single number honestly describes this group? The answer depends on the question you are asking, and that is why statisticians keep several different kinds of "middle" in their toolbox.

Concept

Given a list of numbers (the data), four quick summaries are:

  • the mean (arithmetic mean, or average): add the numbers and divide by how many there are;
  • the median: sort the list; the median is the middle entry, or the average of the two middle entries if the list has even length;
  • the mode: the value that occurs most often (a list can have several modes, or none worth mentioning);
  • the range: the largest value minus the smallest.

The single most useful fact about the mean is that it can be undone:

$$\text{mean} = \frac{\text{sum}}{n} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{sum} = n \times \text{mean}.$$

Whenever numbers join or leave a list, convert every average to a sum first, do the bookkeeping with sums, and convert back at the end.

The geometric mean of $n$ positive numbers multiplies instead of adding: it is $\sqrt[n]{x_1 x_2 \cdots x_n}$. Both kinds of mean always lie between the smallest and largest data value. For two positive numbers the arithmetic mean is never smaller than the geometric mean:

$$\frac{a+b}{2} \;\ge\; \sqrt{ab}, \quad\text{with equality only when } a = b.$$

How many times each value appears is its frequency. A bar chart of frequencies is called a histogram; the dot plot below is a simple version of one.

Four summaries of one list

Find the mean, median, mode and range of $9,\ 5,\ 2,\ 12,\ 5,\ 6$.

  1. Sort: $2,\ 5,\ 5,\ 6,\ 9,\ 12$.

    The median, mode and range are all easiest to read from a sorted list.

  2. Mean $=\dfrac{2+5+5+6+9+12}{6} = \dfrac{39}{6} = 6.5$.

    Add all six values and divide by how many there are.

  3. Median $=\dfrac{5+6}{2} = 5.5$.

    With six values there is no single middle entry, so average the 3rd and 4th.

  4. Mode $=5$; range $=12-2=10$.

    $5$ is the only value that appears twice; the range is largest minus smallest.

Mean $6.5$, median $5.5$, mode $5$, range $10$.

A geometric mean

Find the geometric mean of $2$, $9$ and $12$, and compare it with their arithmetic mean.

  1. Product: $2 \cdot 9 \cdot 12 = 216$.

    The geometric mean of three numbers starts with their product.

  2. $\sqrt[3]{216} = 6$, since $6^3 = 216$.

    Three numbers, so take the cube root.

  3. Arithmetic mean $=\dfrac{2+9+12}{3} = \dfrac{23}{3} \approx 7.67$.

    For comparison. As promised, the arithmetic mean is larger.

Geometric mean $6$; arithmetic mean $\tfrac{23}{3} \approx 7.67$.

What score do I need?

Priya's average on three tests is $85$. What must she score on the fourth test to bring her average up to $88$?

  1. Current total: $3 \times 85 = 255$.

    Turn the average back into a sum: sum $=$ count $\times$ mean.

  2. Needed total for four tests: $4 \times 88 = 352$.

    The goal is also an average, so convert it to a sum too.

  3. Fourth score $= 352 - 255 = 97$.

    The new test must supply the missing points.

$97$

Combining two classes

A class of $12$ averages $90$ on a quiz, and a class of $18$ averages $80$. What is the average of all $30$ students?

  1. Totals: $12 \times 90 = 1080$ and $18 \times 80 = 1440$.

    The classes are different sizes, so we can't just average the two averages. Sums handle the sizes correctly.

  2. Overall mean $=\dfrac{1080 + 1440}{30} = \dfrac{2520}{30} = 84$.

    All the points divided by all the students.

$84$

The combined mean is pulled toward the bigger group: $84$ is closer to $80$ than to $90$.

Which number was removed?

Ten numbers have mean $20$. When one of them is removed, the mean of the other nine is $18$. What number was removed?

  1. Sum of all ten: $10 \times 20 = 200$.

    Convert the average to a sum.

  2. Sum of the remaining nine: $9 \times 18 = 162$.

    The same conversion after the removal.

  3. Removed number $= 200 - 162 = 38$.

    The two sums differ by exactly the removed number.

$38$

Building a list from its statistics

Five positive integers have mean $8$, median $9$, and a unique mode of $12$. What is the largest possible range?

  1. Sort them as $a \le b \le 9 \le d \le e$. Their sum is $5 \times 8 = 40$.

    The median of five sorted numbers is the third one; the mean gives the sum.

  2. $12$ must appear at least twice, and it is bigger than $9$, so $d = e = 12$.

    Only the two spots above the median can hold numbers larger than $9$.

  3. Then $a + b = 40 - 9 - 24 = 7$, with $a \ne b$.

    If $a = b$, that value would also appear twice and $12$ would not be the unique mode.

  4. To maximize $e - a = 12 - a$, make $a$ as small as possible: $a = 1$, $b = 6$. The list $1, 6, 9, 12, 12$ works.

    Check: sum $40$, median $9$, and only $12$ repeats.

$11$

Two numbers from their means

Two positive numbers have arithmetic mean $10$ and geometric mean $8$. Find them.

  1. $\dfrac{x+y}{2} = 10 \Rightarrow x + y = 20$, and $\sqrt{xy} = 8 \Rightarrow xy = 64$.

    Each mean gives one equation about the sum or product.

  2. $x$ and $y$ are the roots of $t^2 - 20t + 64 = 0$, which factors as $(t-4)(t-16) = 0$.

    Knowing a sum and a product is exactly what a quadratic's coefficients encode (Chapter 6).

$4$ and $16$

Why the arithmetic mean wins

Prove that for positive numbers $a$ and $b$, $\dfrac{a+b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab}$, with equality only when $a = b$.

  1. Start from $\left(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}\right)^2 \ge 0$.

    Any real number squared is at least zero. This is the whole idea.

  2. Expand: $a - 2\sqrt{ab} + b \ge 0$, so $a + b \ge 2\sqrt{ab}$.

    $(\sqrt a)^2 = a$ and $\sqrt a \sqrt b = \sqrt{ab}$.

  3. Divide by $2$: $\dfrac{a+b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab}$. Equality needs $\sqrt a - \sqrt b = 0$, that is, $a = b$.

    A square is zero only when the thing being squared is zero.

$\dfrac{a+b}{2} - \sqrt{ab} = \dfrac{\left(\sqrt a - \sqrt b\right)^2}{2} \ge 0$, with equality exactly when $a = b$.

"A square is never negative" proves a surprising number of inequalities.

26.2 Probability and Common Sense

Hook

A game show host says: "Behind one of these five doors is a car." Without any training at all, you would say your chance of picking it is one in five. That instinct is exactly right, and it is the whole definition of probability. The rest of this chapter is learning to count carefully enough that your instinct keeps working when the situation gets complicated.

Concept

When an experiment has finitely many equally likely outcomes, the probability of an event $A$ is

$$P(A) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in } A}{\text{total number of outcomes}}.$$

So probability is really counting twice, and everything from Chapter 25 applies. Some facts follow at once:

  • $0 \le P(A) \le 1$: an impossible event has probability $0$ and a certain one has probability $1$.
  • The probabilities of all the possible outcomes add up to $1$.
  • Either $A$ happens or it doesn't, so

$$P(A) = 1 - P(\text{not } A).$$

This complement rule is a lifesaver when "not $A$" is easier to count, as with "at least one" questions.

A standard deck has $52$ cards: $4$ suits (spades and clubs are black, hearts and diamonds are red) of $13$ values each (A, 2–10, J, Q, K). The jack, queen and king are the face cards, so there are $12$ of them.

Marbles in a bag

A bag holds $3$ red, $5$ blue and $2$ green marbles. One is drawn at random. Find $P(\text{blue})$ and $P(\text{not green})$.

  1. There are $3 + 5 + 2 = 10$ equally likely marbles.

    "At random" means each marble is equally likely to be chosen.

  2. $P(\text{blue}) = \dfrac{5}{10} = \dfrac12$.

    Five of the ten outcomes are blue.

  3. $P(\text{not green}) = 1 - \dfrac{2}{10} = \dfrac{4}{5}$.

    The complement rule; counting the $8$ non-green marbles directly gives the same answer.

$P(\text{blue}) = \tfrac12$, $P(\text{not green}) = \tfrac45$.

One die

A fair six-sided die is rolled. What is the probability of rolling a prime number?

  1. The primes among $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ are $2, 3, 5$.

    Remember that $1$ is not prime.

  2. $P(\text{prime}) = \dfrac{3}{6} = \dfrac12$.

    Three desired outcomes out of six equally likely ones.

$\tfrac12$

Careful with "or"

One card is drawn from a standard deck. Find the probability that it is a king or a heart.

  1. There are $4$ kings and $13$ hearts.

    Count each kind of desired card.

  2. The king of hearts is in both groups, so the number of desired cards is $4 + 13 - 1 = 16$.

    Subtract the overlap so no card is counted twice (more on this in Chapter 27).

  3. $P = \dfrac{16}{52} = \dfrac{4}{13}$.

    Desired over total, then reduce.

$\tfrac{4}{13}$

Next in the book: Exercise 26-4.

Drawing two at once

A box has $4$ red and $2$ blue balls. Two are drawn at the same time. What is the probability both are red?

  1. Total pairs: $\dbinom{6}{2} = 15$.

    The two balls form an unordered pair, so count combinations.

  2. Red pairs: $\dbinom{4}{2} = 6$.

    Both balls must come from the four reds.

  3. $P = \dfrac{6}{15} = \dfrac25$.

    Desired pairs over all pairs.

$\tfrac25$

"At least one" via the complement

Same box ($4$ red, $2$ blue), two balls drawn. What is the probability that at least one is blue?

  1. "At least one blue" fails exactly when both balls are red.

    Its complement is a single, simple case.

  2. $P(\text{at least one blue}) = 1 - P(\text{both red}) = 1 - \dfrac25 = \dfrac35$.

    We found $P(\text{both red})$ in the previous example.

  3. Check directly: one blue in $4 \cdot 2 = 8$ ways, two blue in $1$ way, so $\dfrac{9}{15} = \dfrac35$. ✓

    Counting the cases directly gives the same answer, with more work.

$\tfrac35$

Divisible by 3 or 5

An integer from $1$ to $100$ is chosen at random. What is the probability it is divisible by $3$ or by $5$?

  1. Multiples of $3$: $\lfloor 100/3 \rfloor = 33$. Multiples of $5$: $20$.

    Count each group.

  2. Multiples of both, i.e. of $15$: $\lfloor 100/15 \rfloor = 6$.

    These were counted in both groups.

  3. Desired: $33 + 20 - 6 = 47$, so $P = \dfrac{47}{100}$.

    Remove the double count, then divide by the $100$ outcomes.

$\tfrac{47}{100}$

Digits that climb

A three-digit number ($100$ to $999$) is chosen at random. What is the probability that its digits are strictly increasing from left to right (like $258$)?

  1. Total: $999 - 100 + 1 = 900$ numbers.

    Count the sample space first.

  2. The first digit is at least $1$, and the others are bigger, so all three digits come from $\{1, \dots, 9\}$.

    $0$ can't lead, and can't follow a larger digit in an increasing list.

  3. Any $3$ distinct digits from $1$–$9$ can be arranged in increasing order in exactly one way, so there are $\dbinom{9}{3} = 84$ such numbers.

    Choosing the set of digits decides the number completely.

  4. $P = \dfrac{84}{900} = \dfrac{7}{75}$.

    Reduce by $12$.

$\tfrac{7}{75}$

When order is forced, an arrangement is just a choice: count with combinations.

Next in the book: Exercise 26-5.

One card of every suit

Four cards are dealt from a standard deck. What is the probability that all four suits are represented?

  1. Total hands: $\dbinom{52}{4} = 270725$.

    A hand is an unordered set of four cards.

  2. Choose one spade, one heart, one diamond and one club: $13^4 = 28561$ hands.

    Each suit contributes exactly one card, chosen in $13$ ways. No ordering issue arises because the suits label the cards.

  3. $P = \dfrac{28561}{270725} = \dfrac{2197}{20825} \approx 0.105$.

    Both numbers share a factor of $13$.

$\dfrac{2197}{20825} \approx 0.105$

26.3 Multiplying Probabilities

Hook

A weather app says there's a 50% chance of rain on Saturday and a 50% chance on Sunday. Does that make rain over the weekend certain? Of course not; but what is the chance of a dry weekend? Multiplying probabilities answers it in one line.

Concept

Two events are independent when one has no effect on the other, like two different coin flips. If the first experiment has $T_A$ equally likely outcomes, $N_A$ of them good, and the second has $T_B$ outcomes, $N_B$ good, then the multiplication principle gives $T_A T_B$ combined outcomes and $N_A N_B$ good ones. So

$$P(A \text{ and } B) = \frac{N_A N_B}{T_A T_B} = P(A)\,P(B) \qquad (A, B \text{ independent}).$$

When the first event does change the situation, as when you draw a card and don't put it back, you can still multiply, but the second factor must be computed in the changed situation:

$$P(A \text{ then } B) = P(A) \times P(B \text{, given that } A \text{ happened}).$$

Combined with the complement rule, this makes "at least one" easy: $P(\text{at least one success}) = 1 - P(\text{every trial fails})$.

Never multiply probabilities of events that influence each other as if they were independent. Ask: "after the first thing happens, has anything changed?"

Coins and a die

Two coins are flipped and a die is rolled. What is the probability of two heads and a six?

  1. $P(H) = \tfrac12$ for each coin and $P(6) = \tfrac16$.

    Find each individual probability first.

  2. $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac16 = \tfrac{1}{24}$.

    The three results don't affect each other, so multiply.

$\tfrac{1}{24}$

A dry weekend

Rain on Saturday and rain on Sunday are independent, each with probability $0.5$. What is the probability it rains on at least one day?

  1. $P(\text{dry both days}) = 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$.

    Dry on each day has probability $0.5$, and the days are independent.

  2. $P(\text{rain at least once}) = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75$.

    "At least one rainy day" is the complement of "no rainy days."

$0.75$

Make one, miss one

A player makes $70\%$ of her free throws, independently. What is the probability she makes the first of two shots and misses the second?

  1. $P(\text{make}) = 0.7$ and $P(\text{miss}) = 1 - 0.7 = 0.3$.

    A miss is the complement of a make.

  2. $0.7 \times 0.3 = 0.21$.

    The shots are independent, and the order is specified, so there is just one product.

$0.21$

Without replacement

A bag has $5$ red and $3$ blue marbles. Two are drawn one after the other, without replacement. Find the probability the first is red and the second is blue.

  1. $P(\text{first red}) = \tfrac58$.

    Five of the eight marbles are red.

  2. Given that, the bag has $4$ red and $3$ blue, so $P(\text{second blue}) = \tfrac37$.

    The first draw changed the bag, so recompute in the new situation.

  3. $\tfrac58 \cdot \tfrac37 = \tfrac{15}{56}$.

    Multiply the first probability by the updated second one.

$\tfrac{15}{56}$

Next in the book: Example 26-11 and Exercise 26-7.

Either order

Same bag ($5$ red, $3$ blue), two drawn without replacement. What is the probability of getting one of each colour?

  1. Red then blue: $\tfrac58 \cdot \tfrac37 = \tfrac{15}{56}$. Blue then red: $\tfrac38 \cdot \tfrac57 = \tfrac{15}{56}$.

    "One of each" can happen in two different orders; these are separate outcomes.

  2. Total: $\tfrac{15}{56} + \tfrac{15}{56} = \tfrac{30}{56} = \tfrac{15}{28}$.

    The two orders can't both happen, so their probabilities add.

  3. Check: $\dfrac{5 \cdot 3}{\binom{8}{2}} = \dfrac{15}{28}$. ✓

    Counting unordered pairs gives the same answer.

$\tfrac{15}{28}$

At least one six

A die is rolled four times. What is the probability of at least one six?

  1. $P(\text{no six in one roll}) = \tfrac56$.

    The complement of a single roll's success.

  2. $P(\text{no six in four rolls}) = \left(\tfrac56\right)^4 = \tfrac{625}{1296}$.

    The rolls are independent, so the four failures multiply.

  3. $P(\text{at least one six}) = 1 - \tfrac{625}{1296} = \tfrac{671}{1296} \approx 0.518$.

    Complement rule. Counting "exactly one, exactly two, …" would take four separate cases.

$\tfrac{671}{1296} \approx 0.518$

How many rolls for even money?

What is the smallest number of rolls of a die for which the chance of at least one six is better than $\tfrac12$?

  1. With $n$ rolls, $P(\text{at least one six}) = 1 - \left(\tfrac56\right)^n$. We need $\left(\tfrac56\right)^n < \tfrac12$.

    The same complement idea as the previous example, for general $n$.

  2. $\left(\tfrac56\right)^3 = \tfrac{125}{216} \approx 0.579$, too big. $\left(\tfrac56\right)^4 \approx 0.482$, small enough.

    Test values of $n$; the powers shrink as $n$ grows.

$4$ rolls

Shared birth months

Five people are chosen at random. Assuming each is equally likely to be born in any of the $12$ months, independently, what is the probability that at least two share a birth month?

  1. Find $P(\text{all different})$ first. Person 1 can have any month; person 2 must avoid one month: $\tfrac{11}{12}$; person 3 must avoid two: $\tfrac{10}{12}$; and so on.

    "At least two share" has many cases; its complement is one clean chain of conditions.

  2. $P(\text{all different}) = \tfrac{12}{12} \cdot \tfrac{11}{12} \cdot \tfrac{10}{12} \cdot \tfrac{9}{12} \cdot \tfrac{8}{12} = \tfrac{95040}{248832} = \tfrac{55}{144}$.

    Each factor is computed given that the earlier people all differ.

  3. $P(\text{some month shared}) = 1 - \tfrac{55}{144} = \tfrac{89}{144} \approx 0.618$.

    Complement rule.

$\tfrac{89}{144} \approx 0.618$

With only five people, a shared month is more likely than not. Coincidences are cheaper than intuition suggests.

26.4 Casework

Hook

Roll two dice. Is a total of $7$ more likely than a total of $12$? Gamblers figured out long ago that it is, six times more likely in fact, and the reason is simply that there are more ways to make $7$. Listing those ways, case by case, is the most common technique in all of probability.

Concept

To find a complicated probability, split the event into cases that don't overlap and together cover everything. Find the probability of each case, often by multiplying, and add:

$$P(A) = P(\text{case 1}) + P(\text{case 2}) + \cdots \qquad (\text{cases mutually exclusive}).$$

Two common patterns:

  • Two-stage experiments. First something random happens (rain or not, which urn), then something else. Split on the first stage: $P(A) = P(\text{stage 1 result}) \times P(A \text{ given it})$, summed over the results.
  • Dice totals. List the combinations that give the total. With two dice the $36$ ordered outcomes are equally likely, so count ordered pairs: $3$–$4$ and $4$–$3$ are different.

For two dice, the number of ways to roll a total $s$ climbs $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ for $s = 2, \dots, 7$, then falls back $5, 4, 3, 2, 1$ for $s = 8, \dots, 12$.

Lucky seven

Two dice are rolled. What is the probability that the total is $7$?

  1. Ordered pairs with total $7$: $(1,6)$, $(2,5)$, $(3,4)$, $(4,3)$, $(5,2)$, $(6,1)$, six in all.

    List the cases. Order matters because the two dice are different.

  2. $P = \tfrac{6}{36} = \tfrac16$.

    Each of the $36$ ordered outcomes is equally likely.

$\tfrac16$

A small total

Two dice are rolled. What is the probability that the total is at most $4$?

  1. Cases: total $2$ ($1$ way), total $3$ ($2$ ways), total $4$ ($3$ ways).

    Split by the exact total; these cases can't overlap.

  2. $\tfrac{1 + 2 + 3}{36} = \tfrac{6}{36} = \tfrac16$.

    Add the cases and divide by $36$.

$\tfrac16$

A late bus

The bus is late $30\%$ of the time. When it is late, you miss the bell with probability $0.6$; when it is on time, with probability $0.05$. What is the probability you miss the bell?

  1. Late and miss: $0.3 \times 0.6 = 0.18$.

    Case 1: the bus is late. Multiply along this branch.

  2. On time and miss: $0.7 \times 0.05 = 0.035$.

    Case 2: the bus is on time, with probability $1 - 0.3$.

  3. $0.18 + 0.035 = 0.215$.

    The bus can't be both late and on time, so the cases add.

$0.215$

Pick an urn, then a marble

A coin is flipped. On heads you draw from urn A ($3$ red, $1$ blue); on tails from urn B ($2$ red, $3$ blue). What is the probability of drawing red?

  1. Heads, then red: $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac34 = \tfrac38$.

    Case 1: urn A is used.

  2. Tails, then red: $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac25 = \tfrac15$.

    Case 2: urn B is used.

  3. $\tfrac38 + \tfrac15 = \tfrac{15}{40} + \tfrac{8}{40} = \tfrac{23}{40}$.

    The marbles are not equally likely: each marble in the smaller urn A is more likely to be drawn. Casework respects that.

$\tfrac{23}{40}$

Three dice, total five

Three dice are rolled. What is the probability the total is $5$?

  1. Unordered ways to write $5$ as three numbers from $1$ to $6$: $1+1+3$ and $1+2+2$.

    First find the combinations, ignoring order.

  2. Each can be arranged in $3$ orders ($113, 131, 311$ and $122, 212, 221$), giving $6$ ordered outcomes.

    The $216$ equally likely outcomes are ordered, so count orders.

  3. $P = \tfrac{6}{216} = \tfrac{1}{36}$.

    There are $6^3 = 216$ outcomes in all.

$\tfrac{1}{36}$

The larger die shows 4

Two dice are rolled. What is the probability that the larger of the two numbers is exactly $4$?

  1. Case both are $4$: $1$ outcome.

    Split by how many dice show the $4$.

  2. Case exactly one $4$, other die $1$–$3$: $3$ outcomes with the red die showing $4$, and $3$ with the blue die showing it.

    The other die must be smaller than $4$ for $4$ to be the larger number.

  3. $\tfrac{1 + 3 + 3}{36} = \tfrac{7}{36}$.

    Add the cases. (Shortcut: both dice $\le 4$ in $16$ ways, minus both $\le 3$ in $9$ ways.)

$\tfrac{7}{36}$

Three dice, total ten

Three dice are rolled. What is the probability the total is $10$?

  1. Split on the first die $d$: the other two must total $10 - d$.

    Conditioning on one die reduces the problem to two-dice totals.

  2. $d = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ need two-dice totals $9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4$, which occur in $4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3$ ways.

    Read these off the two-dice pattern $1,2,3,4,5,6,5,4,3,2,1$.

  3. $4 + 5 + 6 + 5 + 4 + 3 = 27$ outcomes, so $P = \tfrac{27}{216} = \tfrac18$.

    Add the cases; there are $216$ outcomes in all.

$\tfrac18$

Treat two dice as a unit and the third die as a separate case variable.

First to roll a six

Ana and Ben take turns rolling a die, Ana first. The first person to roll a six wins. What is the probability that Ana wins?

  1. Ana wins on her 1st turn with probability $\tfrac16$; on her 2nd turn with probability $\left(\tfrac56\right)^2 \tfrac16$; on her 3rd with $\left(\tfrac56\right)^4 \tfrac16$; and so on.

    Split by the turn on which Ana wins. To reach her $k$th turn, both players must have failed $k-1$ times each.

  2. $P = \tfrac16\left(1 + \tfrac{25}{36} + \left(\tfrac{25}{36}\right)^2 + \cdots\right) = \dfrac{1/6}{1 - 25/36}$.

    The cases form an infinite geometric series with ratio $\tfrac{25}{36}$ (Chapter 24).

  3. $\dfrac{1/6}{11/36} = \dfrac{6}{11}$.

    Simplify. Going first is a real advantage: $\tfrac{6}{11} > \tfrac12$.

$\tfrac{6}{11}$

Infinitely many cases are fine if their probabilities form a series you can sum.

26.5 Odds

Hook

A sports commentator announces that an underdog team is "9 to 1 against." That isn't a probability like $0.1$ or $\tfrac{1}{10}$, but it carries exactly the same information in a different package. Learning to unwrap it takes one minute and saves a lifetime of confusion.

Concept

The odds against an event compare the chance it fails to the chance it happens:

$$\text{odds against } A = P(\text{not } A) : P(A).$$

With equally likely outcomes this is simply (unfavourable outcomes) $:$ (favourable outcomes), written with whole numbers in lowest terms. Rolling a $3$ on a die has $5$ unfavourable outcomes and $1$ favourable, so the odds against are $5 : 1$, read "five to one." The odds for an event are the reverse ratio, $P(A) : P(\text{not } A)$. When people just say "odds," they usually mean odds against.

To convert back, think of the two numbers as counts of outcomes:

$$\text{odds against } m : n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(A) = \frac{n}{m+n}.$$

A memory aid: "100 to 1" sounds like a long shot, so the big number must describe failure. That is why failure comes first in odds against.

From probability to odds

A die is rolled. What are the odds against rolling a $5$ or a $6$?

  1. Favourable: $2$ outcomes. Unfavourable: $4$ outcomes.

    Split the six outcomes into the two groups.

  2. Odds against $= 4 : 2 = 2 : 1$.

    Unfavourable first, then reduce like a fraction.

$2 : 1$ against

From odds to probability

The odds against an event are $3 : 5$. What is its probability?

  1. Think of $3$ unfavourable and $5$ favourable outcomes, $8$ in all.

    Odds against list failure first, success second.

  2. $P = \tfrac58$.

    Favourable over total. Odds against smaller than $1:1$ mean the event is more likely than not.

$\tfrac58$

A decimal probability

An event has probability $0.2$. Give the odds against it and the odds for it.

  1. $P(\text{not } A) = 0.8$, so odds against $= 0.8 : 0.2$.

    Use the definition directly.

  2. Multiply both parts by $5$: $4 : 1$ against, so $1 : 4$ for.

    Odds are written with whole numbers; scaling both parts doesn't change a ratio.

$4 : 1$ against; $1 : 4$ for.

Drawing a heart

What are the odds against drawing a heart from a standard deck?

  1. $13$ hearts and $39$ other cards.

    Favourable and unfavourable counts.

  2. $39 : 13 = 3 : 1$.

    Divide both parts by $13$.

$3 : 1$ against

Odds that don't reduce nicely

A horse has a $7$ in $100$ chance of winning. What are the odds against it?

  1. Out of $100$ equally likely "races," it wins $7$ and loses $100 - 7 = 93$.

    The second number in odds is not the total; it's the favourable part only.

  2. Odds against $= 93 : 7$.

    $93$ and $7$ share no common factor, so this is lowest terms.

$93 : 7$ against

Is the bet fair?

A carnival booth offers: bet 1 dollar on a single die; if it shows $6$ you get your dollar back plus 5 dollars, otherwise you lose the dollar. Is the bet fair?

  1. The true odds against a six are $5 : 1$.

    Five losing faces, one winning face.

  2. The payout is also $5$ to $1$: win 5 dollars for each 1 dollar risked.

    A bet is fair when the payout ratio matches the true odds against.

  3. Average result per game: $\tfrac16(5) + \tfrac56(-1) = 0$ dollars.

    Over many games, wins and losses cancel. (Section 26.6 develops this idea.)

Yes, it is exactly fair.

Odds for two events at once

Independent events $A$ and $B$ have odds against of $3 : 1$ and $4 : 1$. What are the odds against both happening?

  1. $P(A) = \tfrac{1}{3+1} = \tfrac14$ and $P(B) = \tfrac{1}{4+1} = \tfrac15$.

    Convert odds to probabilities before combining. Odds themselves don't multiply sensibly.

  2. $P(A \text{ and } B) = \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac15 = \tfrac{1}{20}$.

    Independent events multiply.

  3. Odds against $= \tfrac{19}{20} : \tfrac{1}{20} = 19 : 1$.

    Convert back. Note it is not $12 : 1$, the product of the odds.

$19 : 1$ against

Do the arithmetic in probabilities; translate to odds only at the start and the end.

26.6 What Did You Expect?

Hook

A raffle ticket costs 2 dollars and could win 400. A game at the fair costs 1 dollar and pays 5 on a six. Which is the better deal? Neither the price nor the prize alone tells you. What matters is what you'd win on average if you played over and over, a number called the expected value.

Concept

If an experiment has outcomes worth $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k$ occurring with probabilities $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_k$, its expected value is

$$E = x_1 p_1 + x_2 p_2 + \cdots + x_k p_k.$$

It is a weighted average: each outcome counts in proportion to how often it happens. If you repeat the experiment many times, your average result per trial gets close to $E$. A game is fair when the price to play equals the expected winnings.

Two things to keep in mind:

  • $E$ need not be a possible outcome. A die's expected roll is $3.5$, which never comes up.
  • Expected values add: the expected total of several experiments is the sum of their expected values, even when they are not independent.

The average die roll

What is the expected value of one roll of a fair die?

  1. $E = \tfrac16(1) + \tfrac16(2) + \cdots + \tfrac16(6) = \tfrac{21}{6}$.

    Each face has probability $\tfrac16$; multiply each value by its probability and add.

  2. $\tfrac{21}{6} = 3.5$.

    Simplify. The die can never show $3.5$; it is the long-run average.

$3.5$

A spinner game

A spinner pays nothing with probability $\tfrac12$, 2 dollars with probability $\tfrac13$, and 9 dollars with probability $\tfrac16$. What is the expected payout?

  1. $E = 0 \cdot \tfrac12 + 2 \cdot \tfrac13 + 9 \cdot \tfrac16$.

    Value times probability for each outcome.

  2. $= \tfrac23 + \tfrac32 = \tfrac{4}{6} + \tfrac{9}{6} = \tfrac{13}{6}$.

    Add with a common denominator.

$\tfrac{13}{6} \approx 2.17$ dollars

Is the raffle worth it?

$500$ raffle tickets are sold for 2 dollars each. There is one 400-dollar prize and two 100-dollar prizes. What is the expected net gain from buying one ticket?

  1. Expected prize: $400 \cdot \tfrac{1}{500} + 100 \cdot \tfrac{2}{500} = \tfrac{600}{500} = 1.2$ dollars.

    Your ticket wins the big prize with probability $\tfrac{1}{500}$ and a small one with probability $\tfrac{2}{500}$.

  2. Net: $1.2 - 2 = -0.8$ dollars.

    Subtract the price paid.

A loss of 80 cents per ticket, on average.

Expected heads

Four fair coins are flipped. What is the expected number of heads?

  1. $P(k \text{ heads}) = \dbinom{4}{k}\big/16$, giving probabilities $\tfrac{1}{16}, \tfrac{4}{16}, \tfrac{6}{16}, \tfrac{4}{16}, \tfrac{1}{16}$ for $k = 0, \dots, 4$.

    Choose which $k$ of the $4$ coins are heads, out of $2^4 = 16$ equally likely outcomes.

  2. $E = \dfrac{0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 6 + 3 \cdot 4 + 4 \cdot 1}{16} = \dfrac{32}{16} = 2$.

    Weighted average of the possible counts.

  3. Shortcut: each coin contributes $\tfrac12$ head on average, and $4 \cdot \tfrac12 = 2$.

    Expected values add, so the expected total is the sum of four expected values.

$2$

A fair price

A game pays 10 dollars if two dice total $7$ or $11$, and nothing otherwise. What price makes the game fair?

  1. Total $7$: $6$ ways; total $11$: $2$ ways. $P(\text{win}) = \tfrac{8}{36} = \tfrac29$.

    Casework on the two winning totals.

  2. $E = 10 \cdot \tfrac29 = \tfrac{20}{9} \approx 2.22$ dollars.

    The only nonzero outcome is the 10-dollar win.

$\tfrac{20}{9} \approx 2.22$ dollars

Ten dice at once

Ten dice are rolled. What is the expected total?

  1. Each die has expected value $3.5$.

    From the first example of this section.

  2. Expected total $= 10 \times 3.5 = 35$.

    Expected values add, so we never need the messy distribution of the total.

$35$

Expected maximum of two dice

Two dice are rolled. What is the expected value of the larger number?

  1. $P(\max = k) = \dfrac{k^2 - (k-1)^2}{36} = \dfrac{2k - 1}{36}$.

    Both dice $\le k$ in $k^2$ ways; remove the $(k-1)^2$ ways where both are $\le k-1$. (Compare the "larger die shows 4" example in 26.4.)

  2. $$E = \frac{1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 5 + 4 \cdot 7 + 5 \cdot 9 + 6 \cdot 11}{36}$$

    $= \dfrac{1 + 6 + 15 + 28 + 45 + 66}{36} = \dfrac{161}{36}$.

    Value times probability, summed. The numerators $1, 3, 5, \dots, 11$ add to $36$, as they must.

$\tfrac{161}{36} \approx 4.47$

Waiting for a six

You roll a die until a six appears. What is the expected number of rolls?

  1. Let $E$ be the expected number of rolls. Roll once. With probability $\tfrac16$ you are done; with probability $\tfrac56$ you are back where you started, having used one roll.

    The die has no memory, so a failed roll resets the game.

  2. $E = 1 + \tfrac56 E$.

    One roll always happens; then, with probability $\tfrac56$, you still expect $E$ more.

  3. $\tfrac16 E = 1$, so $E = 6$.

    Solve the linear equation.

$6$ rolls
