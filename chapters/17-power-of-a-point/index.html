Power of a Point

Pick any point and any circle. Draw a line through the point that hits the circle twice, and multiply the two distances from the point to the circle. Now tilt the line: the product does not change. That single fact handles chords, secants and tangents all at once, and it is one of the most useful tools in geometry.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • Power of a Point: for a point $P$ and any line through $P$ meeting a circle at $A$ and $B$, the product $PA \cdot PB$ is the same for every such line.
  • Two chords through $P$: $PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD$. Two secants from $P$: $PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD$ (whole secant times outside part). Tangent and secant: $PT^2 = PA \cdot PB$.
  • Two tangents from one point are equal. Label equal tangent lengths with one variable.
  • Incircle: the tangent lengths from the vertices are $s - a$, $s - b$, $s - c$, where $s$ is the semiperimeter.
  • A quadrilateral with an inscribed circle has $AB + CD = BC + DA$.
  • The power equals $|d^2 - r^2|$, where $d$ is the distance from $P$ to the center.
  • Proofs: ratios of lengths suggest similar triangles; inscribed angles and tangent-chord angles supply the equal angles.

17.1 Introduction

Hook

Stick a pin anywhere inside a round pizza and lay a ruler across the pin. The ruler crosses the crust at two points, splitting into a short piece and a long piece. Turn the ruler: one piece shrinks and the other grows, in exactly the right way to keep their product fixed. The pin's position alone decides that product.

Concept

Power of a Point Theorem. Given a point $P$ and a circle, draw any line through $P$ that meets the circle at $A$ and $B$. Then $PA \cdot PB$ is the same for every such line. This number is called the power of $P$.

It shows up in four familiar pictures:

1. Two chords $AB$ and $CD$ crossing at $P$ inside: $\;PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD$.

2. Two secants from $P$ outside, meeting the circle at $A, B$ and $C, D$: $\;PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD$.

3. A tangent $PT$ and a secant $PAB$ from $P$ outside: $\;PT^2 = PA \cdot PB$.

4. Two tangents $PT$ and $PU$ from $P$: $\;PT = PU$.

For a secant, always measure both lengths from $P$: the outside part times the whole secant, not the outside part times the chord. A tangent is the limiting case where the two meeting points merge, so both factors become $PT$.

Equal tangents are especially handy. If a circle is inscribed in triangle $ABC$ with sides $a, b, c$ and semiperimeter $s = \frac{a + b + c}{2}$, the two tangent lengths from $A$ are both $s - a$, from $B$ both $s - b$, and from $C$ both $s - c$.

The power can be computed from the center $O$ and radius $r$: a line through $P$ and $O$ meets the circle at distances $d - r$ and $d + r$ from $P$ (with $d = PO$), so the power is $|d^2 - r^2|$. Points outside have $PT^2 = d^2 - r^2$, which is just Pythagoras in the right triangle $PTO$.

Crossing chords

Chords $AB$ and $CD$ meet at $P$. If $PA = 4$, $PB = 6$ and $PC = 3$, find $PD$.

  1. $PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD$, so $4 \cdot 6 = 3 \cdot PD$.

    Both chords pass through $P$, so the two products of pieces agree.

  2. $PD = 24 / 3 = 8$.

    Divide both sides by $3$.

$PD = 8$

Equal tangents

From $P$, two tangents touch a circle at $T$ and $U$. If $PT = x + 3$ and $PU = 2x - 1$, find $PT$.

  1. $x + 3 = 2x - 1$, so $x = 4$.

    Tangents to a circle from the same point are equal.

  2. $PT = 4 + 3 = 7$.

    Check: $PU = 2 \cdot 4 - 1 = 7$. ✓

$PT = 7$

Tangent and secant

From $P$, a tangent touches a circle at $T$ with $PT = 6$, and a secant meets the circle at $A$ and then $B$ with $PA = 3$. Find the chord $AB$.

  1. $PT^2 = PA \cdot PB$: $36 = 3 \cdot PB$, so $PB = 12$.

    The tangent squared equals outside part times whole secant.

  2. $AB = PB - PA = 12 - 3 = 9$.

    The chord is the whole secant minus its outside part.

$AB = 9$

Two secants

Two secants from $P$ meet a circle at $A, B$ and at $C, D$ (with $A$ and $C$ nearer $P$). If $PA = 4$, $AB = 5$ and $PC = 3$, find $CD$.

  1. $PB = PA + AB = 9$, so the power of $P$ is $PA \cdot PB = 4 \cdot 9 = 36$.

    Measure both lengths from $P$: the whole secant, not just the chord.

  2. $PC \cdot PD = 36$, so $PD = 12$ and $CD = 12 - 3 = 9$.

    The second secant has the same power.

$CD = 9$

Power from the center

A point $P$ is $10$ units from the center of a circle of radius $6$. Find the length of a tangent from $P$, and the product $PA \cdot PB$ for any secant $PAB$.

  1. The radius to the point of tangency $T$ is perpendicular to $PT$, so $PT^2 + 6^2 = 10^2$ and $PT = 8$.

    A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact, giving a right triangle.

  2. Every secant has $PA \cdot PB = PT^2 = 64$.

    Power of a Point: all lines through $P$ share one product, and the tangent gives $d^2 - r^2 = 100 - 36$.

$PT = 8$; $PA \cdot PB = 64$.

Shortest chord through a point

A point $P$ is $3$ units from the center $O$ of a circle of radius $5$. Find the lengths of the longest and shortest chords through $P$.

  1. The chord through $O$ is a diameter: pieces $5 - 3 = 2$ and $5 + 3 = 8$, so the power is $16$ and the longest chord is $10$.

    A diameter is the longest chord; computing its pieces gives the power $r^2 - d^2$.

  2. For the chord perpendicular to $OP$, $P$ is its midpoint (the perpendicular from the center bisects a chord). So both pieces equal $x$ with $x^2 = 16$, $x = 4$, and the chord is $8$.

    For a fixed product, the sum of two positive numbers is smallest when they are equal.

Longest $10$, shortest $8$.

Tangent lengths in a triangle

A circle is inscribed in a triangle with sides $BC = 7$, $CA = 8$, $AB = 9$. Find the tangent lengths from each vertex.

  1. Call the tangent lengths from $A$, $B$, $C$ by $x$, $y$, $z$. Then $x + y = 9$, $y + z = 7$, $z + x = 8$.

    Each side is made of two tangent segments, one from each end, and tangents from one vertex are equal.

  2. Add: $2(x + y + z) = 24$, so $x + y + z = 12 = s$.

    The sum of all three is the semiperimeter.

  3. $x = 12 - 7 = 5$, $y = 12 - 8 = 4$, $z = 12 - 9 = 3$.

    Subtract the opposite side from $s$: this is the $s - a$ rule.

From $A$: $5$; from $B$: $4$; from $C$: $3$.

A quadrilateral around a circle

Quadrilateral $ABCD$ has a circle tangent to all four sides. If $AB = 7$, $BC = 9$ and $CD = 10$, find $DA$.

  1. Let the tangent lengths from $A, B, C, D$ be $w, x, y, z$. Then $AB + CD = (w + x) + (y + z)$ and $BC + DA = (x + y) + (z + w)$.

    Each side splits into tangents from its two ends.

  2. Both sums equal $w + x + y + z$, so $AB + CD = BC + DA$: $7 + 10 = 9 + DA$.

    Opposite sides of a quadrilateral with an inscribed circle have equal sums.

$DA = 8$

Name each tangent length once; equal tangents do the rest.

Finding a radius

A point $P$ is $11$ units from the center of a circle. A secant from $P$ meets the circle at $Q$ and $R$, with $PQ = 5$ and $QR = 7$. Find the radius.

  1. Power from the secant: $PQ \cdot PR = 5 \cdot 12 = 60$.

    $PR = PQ + QR = 12$.

  2. Power from the center: $11^2 - r^2 = 121 - r^2$.

    The secant through the center has pieces $11 - r$ and $11 + r$.

  3. $121 - r^2 = 60$, so $r^2 = 61$ and $r = \sqrt{61}$.

    Two expressions for the same power must be equal.

$r = \sqrt{61}$

Tangents to two circles

Two circles cross at $X$ and $Y$. A point $P$ on line $XY$, outside both circles, has $PX = 4$ and $PY = 9$. Find the length of a tangent from $P$ to each circle.

  1. Line $PXY$ is a secant of the first circle, so a tangent $PT_1$ satisfies $PT_1^2 = PX \cdot PY = 36$.

    Tangent squared equals the secant product.

  2. The same line is a secant of the second circle too, so $PT_2^2 = 36$ as well. Both tangents have length $6$.

    $X$ and $Y$ lie on both circles, so $P$ has the same power with respect to each.

Both tangents have length $6$.

Every point on the line through two crossing points has equal power to both circles.

17.2 Power of a Point Proofs

Hook

An equation like $PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD$ can be rearranged into $\frac{PA}{PC} = \frac{PD}{PB}$, a statement about ratios. Whenever you see equal ratios of lengths, a pair of similar triangles is usually hiding nearby. In a circle, inscribed angles hand you the equal angles you need to find them.

Concept

Point inside (two chords). Chords $AB$ and $CD$ cross at $E$. Draw $AC$ and $DB$. Then $\angle AEC = \angle DEB$ (vertical angles), and $\angle CAB = \angle CDB$ (inscribed angles on the same arc $CB$). So $\triangle EAC \sim \triangle EDB$, which gives

$$\frac{EA}{ED} = \frac{EC}{EB} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad EA \cdot EB = EC \cdot ED.$$

Point outside (tangent and secant). A tangent from $P$ touches at $T$; a secant meets the circle at $A$ (near) and $B$ (far). The tangent-chord angle $\angle PTA$ equals half of arc $TA$, and so does the inscribed angle $\angle TBA$. With the shared angle at $P$, $\triangle PTA \sim \triangle PBT$, so

$$\frac{PT}{PB} = \frac{PA}{PT} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad PT^2 = PA \cdot PB.$$

Two secants follow at once: each secant product equals the same $PT^2$. Two tangents follow too: $PT^2 = PA \cdot PB = PU^2$, so $PT = PU$.

The toolkit for these proofs: ratios of sides suggest similar triangles; similar triangles need equal angles; circles supply equal angles through arcs. Keep this pattern in mind for your own proofs.

Spot the equal angles

Chords $AB$ and $CD$ cross at $E$. Which angles of $\triangle EAC$ and $\triangle EDB$ are equal, and why?

  1. $\angle AEC = \angle DEB$.

    They are vertical angles, formed by the same two crossing lines.

  2. $\angle CAE = \angle BDE$, that is, $\angle CAB = \angle CDB$.

    Both are inscribed angles that cut off the same arc $CB$, so each is half of it.

  3. The third angles, $\angle ACE$ and $\angle DBE$, are then equal too.

    Angles in a triangle sum to $180^\circ$. (They also cut off the same arc $AD$.)

All three pairs match, so $\triangle EAC \sim \triangle EDB$.

Proof for crossing chords

Prove that if chords $AB$ and $CD$ cross at $E$, then $EA \cdot EB = EC \cdot ED$.

  1. Draw segments $AC$ and $DB$ to form $\triangle EAC$ and $\triangle EDB$.

    The products involve four segments from $E$; two triangles that use them are the natural thing to compare.

  2. By the previous example, the triangles are similar (AA), with $A \leftrightarrow D$, $C \leftrightarrow B$, $E \leftrightarrow E$.

    Two pairs of equal angles are enough for similarity.

  3. Corresponding sides are proportional: $\dfrac{EA}{ED} = \dfrac{EC}{EB}$. Cross-multiply: $EA \cdot EB = EC \cdot ED$. ∎

    Match the sides carefully: $EA$ is opposite the angle at $C$, which matches the angle at $B$, opposite $ED$.

$EA \cdot EB = EC \cdot ED$, from $\triangle EAC \sim \triangle EDB$.

Two unknown pieces

Chords $AB$ and $CD$ cross at $E$ with $AE = 6$, $EB = 4$, and $CD = 11$. Find $CE$ and $ED$.

  1. Let $CE = x$, so $ED = 11 - x$. Then $x(11 - x) = 6 \cdot 4 = 24$.

    Power of the point $E$.

  2. $x^2 - 11x + 24 = 0$, so $(x - 3)(x - 8) = 0$ and $x = 3$ or $8$.

    Either root works; it just depends on which end you call $C$.

The pieces are $3$ and $8$.

Why two tangents are equal

Tangents from $P$ touch a circle with center $O$ at $T$ and $U$. Prove $PT = PU$ directly with congruent triangles.

  1. $\angle PTO = \angle PUO = 90^\circ$.

    A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.

  2. $OT = OU$ (radii) and $OP$ is shared, so right triangles $PTO$ and $PUO$ are congruent (hypotenuse-leg).

    Two right triangles with equal hypotenuses and one pair of equal legs are congruent.

  3. Hence $PT = PU$. ∎ As a bonus, $\angle TPO = \angle UPO$: $PO$ bisects the angle between the tangents.

    Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal.

$\triangle PTO \cong \triangle PUO$, so $PT = PU$.

Proof for tangent and secant

From an outside point $P$, a tangent touches a circle at $T$, and a secant meets it at $A$ and then $B$. Prove $PT^2 = PA \cdot PB$.

  1. Draw $TA$ and $TB$. Compare $\triangle PTA$ and $\triangle PBT$; they share $\angle P$.

    $PT^2 = PA \cdot PB$ rearranges to $\frac{PT}{PA} = \frac{PB}{PT}$, so look for triangles with sides $PT, PA$ and $PB, PT$.

  2. $\angle PTA = \tfrac12 \overset{\frown}{TA}$ (tangent-chord angle) and $\angle TBA = \tfrac12 \overset{\frown}{TA}$ (inscribed angle). So $\angle PTA = \angle PBT$.

    Both angles are measured by half of the same arc $TA$ (Chapter 10).

  3. By AA, $\triangle PTA \sim \triangle PBT$, so $\dfrac{PT}{PB} = \dfrac{PA}{PT}$ and $PT^2 = PA \cdot PB$. ∎

    Match vertices: $P \leftrightarrow P$, $T \leftrightarrow B$, $A \leftrightarrow T$.

$\triangle PTA \sim \triangle PBT$ gives $PT^2 = PA \cdot PB$.

The power is $|d^2 - r^2|$

A circle has center $O$ and radius $r$; let $d = PO$. Show that the power of $P$ is $d^2 - r^2$ if $P$ is outside and $r^2 - d^2$ if $P$ is inside.

  1. Use the line through $P$ and $O$. It meets the circle at the two ends of a diameter.

    Since every line through $P$ gives the same product, choose the easiest one.

  2. Outside: the distances from $P$ are $d - r$ and $d + r$, with product $d^2 - r^2$.

    The near end is $r$ closer than $O$; the far end is $r$ farther.

  3. Inside: the distances are $r - d$ and $r + d$, with product $r^2 - d^2$.

    Now $P$ is between the two ends of the diameter.

Power $= |d^2 - r^2|$ in both cases.

The geometric mean in a semicircle

The diameter of a semicircle is split by a point $X$ into pieces of length $4$ and $9$. A perpendicular to the diameter at $X$ meets the semicircle at $C$. Find $XC$.

  1. Complete the full circle and extend $CX$ to a chord $CD$. The diameter is perpendicular to $CD$, so it bisects it: $XD = XC$.

    A diameter perpendicular to a chord bisects the chord.

  2. Power of $X$: $XC \cdot XD = 4 \cdot 9$, so $XC^2 = 36$ and $XC = 6$.

    The chord $CD$ and the diameter cross at $X$.

$XC = 6$, the geometric mean of $4$ and $9$.

A test for tangency

A secant from an outside point $P$ meets a circle at $A$ and $B$, and $X$ is a point on the circle with $PX^2 = PA \cdot PB$. Prove that $PX$ is tangent to the circle.

  1. Suppose not. Then line $PX$ meets the circle at a second point $Y$.

    A line through a point of a circle is either tangent there or crosses at a second point.

  2. Power of $P$: $PX \cdot PY = PA \cdot PB = PX^2$, so $PY = PX$.

    Line $PXY$ is another secant from $P$.

  3. Since $P$ is outside the circle, $X$ and $Y$ lie on the same ray from $P$, so $PY = PX$ forces $Y = X$. This contradicts $Y$ being a second point, so $PX$ is tangent. ∎

    From an outside point, both meeting points of a secant are on the same side of $P$.

$PX$ is tangent, because a second meeting point would have to coincide with $X$.

Power of a Point works backward too: $PX^2 = PA \cdot PB$ proves tangency.

The common tangent is bisected

Two circles cross at $X$ and $Y$. A common tangent touches the first circle at $T_1$ and the second at $T_2$. Prove that line $XY$ passes through the midpoint of $T_1T_2$.

  1. Extend $XY$ to meet line $T_1T_2$ at $M$.

    We will show $MT_1 = MT_2$.

  2. For the first circle, $MT_1$ is a tangent and $MXY$ a secant: $MT_1^2 = MX \cdot MY$.

    Tangent-secant form of Power of a Point.

  3. For the second circle, likewise $MT_2^2 = MX \cdot MY$. So $MT_1 = MT_2$, and $M$ is the midpoint. ∎

    The same secant works for both circles because $X$ and $Y$ are on both.

$MT_1^2 = MX \cdot MY = MT_2^2$, so $M$ is the midpoint of $T_1T_2$.
