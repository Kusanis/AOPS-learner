Chapter 10 · Geometry

Angles

Angles are the language of geometry. In this chapter you'll name and measure them, in degrees and in radians; use parallel lines to prove that a triangle's angles add to $180^\circ$; measure arcs and sectors; and discover the surprisingly simple rules for angles made by chords, secants and tangents of a circle, then prove those rules yourself.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • If $X$ is on segment $AB$, then $AX + XB = AB$. The midpoint splits $AB$ into two equal halves.
  • A full turn is $360^\circ = 2\pi$ radians, so multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180^\circ}$ or $\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}$ to convert. $1^\circ = 60'$ and $1' = 60''$.
  • Complementary angles sum to $90^\circ$, supplementary to $180^\circ$. Vertical angles are equal.
  • A transversal of parallel lines makes corresponding angles equal, alternate interior angles equal, and same-side interior angles supplementary. When stuck, draw an extra parallel line.
  • Triangle angles sum to $180^\circ$; an exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.
  • Arc length $= r\theta$ and sector area $= \frac12 r^2\theta$ ($\theta$ in radians), or take the fraction $\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}$ of the whole. Segment $=$ sector $-$ triangle.
  • Circle angles: central $=$ arc; inscribed $= \frac12$ arc; tangent-chord $= \frac12$ arc; two chords $= \frac12$(sum of arcs); two secants or tangents from outside $= \frac12$(difference of arcs).
  • Inscribed angles on the same arc are equal; an angle inscribed in a semicircle is $90^\circ$.

10.1 Lines, Rays, and Segments

Hook

A laser pointer beam starts at the pointer and runs off forever in one direction. A taut string between two nails stops at both ends. A perfectly straight road that never ends in either direction goes on forever both ways. Geometry has a name for each: a ray, a segment, and a line.

Concept
  • A segment $\overline{AB}$ is the straight path between its two endpoints $A$ and $B$. In equations, $AB$ means its length. The midpoint is the point of the segment exactly halfway between the endpoints.
  • A ray $\overrightarrow{AB}$ starts at its origin $A$ and goes on forever through $B$. Order matters: $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{BA}$ point opposite ways.
  • A line $\overleftrightarrow{AB}$ continues forever in both directions. Two points determine exactly one line.
  • Points on the same line are collinear.

If $X$ lies on segment $\overline{AB}$, then $AX + XB = AB$. If $X$ is anywhere else, $AX + XB > AB$.

The second sentence says the straight path is the shortest one; Chapter 11 calls it the triangle inequality. On a number line, the distance between points at $a$ and $b$ is $|a - b|$, and the midpoint is at $\frac{a+b}{2}$.

Length and midpoint on a number line

Points $A$ and $B$ are at $-3$ and $7$ on a number line. Find $AB$ and the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$.

  1. $AB = |7 - (-3)| = 10$.

    Distance on a number line is the absolute value of the difference.

  2. Midpoint: $\dfrac{-3 + 7}{2} = 2$.

    The midpoint is the average of the coordinates. Check: $2$ is $5$ from each endpoint.

$AB = 10$; midpoint at $2$.

Same or different?

For two points $P$ and $Q$, are $\overline{PQ}$ and $\overline{QP}$ the same? $\overrightarrow{PQ}$ and $\overrightarrow{QP}$? $\overleftrightarrow{PQ}$ and $\overleftrightarrow{QP}$?

  1. $\overline{PQ} = \overline{QP}$ and $\overleftrightarrow{PQ} = \overleftrightarrow{QP}$.

    A segment and a line have no preferred direction; listing the points in the other order describes the same set.

  2. $\overrightarrow{PQ} \ne \overrightarrow{QP}$.

    $\overrightarrow{PQ}$ starts at $P$ and heads through $Q$; $\overrightarrow{QP}$ starts at $Q$ and heads the other way. They share only the segment $\overline{PQ}$.

Segments: same. Lines: same. Rays: different.

Adding segments

$A$, $B$, $C$ are collinear with $B$ between $A$ and $C$. If $AB = 7$ and $BC = 5$, find $AC$.

  1. Since $B$ is on $\overline{AC}$, $AC = AB + BC$.

    Segment addition: the two pieces make up the whole.

  2. $AC = 7 + 5 = 12$.

    Substitute.

$12$

Algebra with a midpoint

$M$ is the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$, with $AM = 2x + 3$ and $MB = 5x - 9$. Find $AB$.

  1. $2x + 3 = 5x - 9$, so $3x = 12$ and $x = 4$.

    A midpoint makes the two halves equal.

  2. $AM = 11$, so $AB = 2 \cdot 11 = 22$.

    Check: $MB = 20 - 9 = 11$ as well. ✓

$22$

Overlapping segments

Points $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ lie on a line in that order. $AC = 10$, $BD = 12$ and $AD = 17$. Find $BC$.

  1. $AC + BD = (AB + BC) + (BC + CD) = AD + BC$.

    $AB + BC + CD = AD$, and $BC$ is the overlap counted in both.

  2. $BC = 10 + 12 - 17 = 5$.

    Subtract the whole from the sum of the overlapping pieces.

$5$

Overlapping lengths: (sum of pieces) $-$ (whole) $=$ overlap.

A point that divides in a ratio

$P$ is on $\overline{AB}$ with $AP : PB = 2 : 3$ and $AB = 20$. $Q$ is the midpoint of $\overline{PB}$. Find $AQ$.

  1. $AP = \frac25 \cdot 20 = 8$ and $PB = 12$.

    The ratio $2:3$ splits $AB$ into $5$ equal parts.

  2. $PQ = 6$, so $AQ = AP + PQ = 14$.

    $Q$ is halfway along $\overline{PB}$, and $P$ is between $A$ and $Q$.

$14$

How close can the ends be?

Points $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ lie on a line (in some order) with $AB = 2$, $BC = 3$ and $CD = 4$. What are the largest and smallest possible values of $AD$?

  1. Put $A$ at $0$. Then $B = \pm 2$, $C = B \pm 3$, $D = C \pm 4$, so $AD = |\pm 2 \pm 3 \pm 4|$.

    Each step can go left or right along the line.

  2. Largest: $2 + 3 + 4 = 9$ (all in one direction).

    The distance can't exceed the total of the steps.

  3. Every choice of signs gives an odd number, since $2 + 3 + 4 = 9$ is odd and flipping a sign changes the sum by an even amount. So $AD \ge 1$, and $2 + 3 - 4 = 1$ achieves it.

    Changing $+k$ to $-k$ changes the total by $2k$, which keeps it odd. So $0$ is impossible.

Largest $9$, smallest $1$.

On a line, distances are sums with signs; parity can rule out values.

10.2 Classification and Measurement

Hook

Why does a full turn have $360$ degrees and not $100$? Because $360$ divides evenly by $2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, \ldots$: a third of a turn is a tidy $120^\circ$, a sixth is $60^\circ$. Mathematicians also use a second unit, the radian, which comes straight from the circle itself and makes many formulas simpler.

Concept

Two rays with a common origin form an angle; the rays are its sides and the origin is its vertex. We write $\angle AOB$ with the vertex in the middle, or just $\angle O$ when there's no confusion. An angle is measured by the fraction of a circle (centered at the vertex) that it cuts off; we say it subtends that arc.

$$360^\circ = 2\pi \text{ radians}, \qquad 1^\circ = 60' \ (\text{minutes}), \qquad 1' = 60'' \ (\text{seconds}).$$

To convert, multiply by the conversion factor $\dfrac{\pi}{180^\circ}$ (degrees to radians) or $\dfrac{180^\circ}{\pi}$ (radians to degrees).

  • Acute: less than $90^\circ$. Right: exactly $90^\circ$ (lines meeting at a right angle are perpendicular, $\perp$). Obtuse: between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$. Straight: $180^\circ$. Reflex: more than $180^\circ$.
  • Complementary angles add to $90^\circ$; supplementary angles add to $180^\circ$.

When two lines cross, angles next to each other form a straight line, so they are supplementary. The angles across from each other, called vertical angles, are therefore equal: if $\alpha + \beta = 180^\circ$ and $\beta + \gamma = 180^\circ$, then $\alpha = \gamma$.

Name the type

Classify angles of $35^\circ$, $90^\circ$, $140^\circ$, $180^\circ$ and $250^\circ$.

  1. $35^\circ$: acute. $90^\circ$: right. $140^\circ$: obtuse.

    Compare each with $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$.

  2. $180^\circ$: straight. $250^\circ$: reflex.

    A straight angle is a line; anything beyond it is reflex.

Acute, right, obtuse, straight, reflex.

Complement and supplement

Find the complement and the supplement of a $38^\circ$ angle.

  1. Complement: $90^\circ - 38^\circ = 52^\circ$.

    Complementary angles sum to $90^\circ$.

  2. Supplement: $180^\circ - 38^\circ = 142^\circ$.

    Supplementary angles sum to $180^\circ$. Notice the supplement is always $90^\circ$ more than the complement.

Complement $52^\circ$, supplement $142^\circ$.

Degrees and radians

Convert $\dfrac{5\pi}{6}$ radians to degrees and $210^\circ$ to radians.

  1. $\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\cdot\dfrac{180^\circ}{\pi} = 5 \cdot 30^\circ = 150^\circ$.

    The factor $\frac{180^\circ}{\pi}$ equals $1$, so multiplying by it changes only the units; the $\pi$'s cancel.

  2. $210^\circ \cdot \dfrac{\pi}{180^\circ} = \dfrac{210\pi}{180} = \dfrac{7\pi}{6}$.

    Now the degree units cancel; reduce the fraction.

$150^\circ$ and $\dfrac{7\pi}{6}$

Minutes and seconds

Write $47.36^\circ$ in degrees, minutes and seconds.

  1. $0.36^\circ = 0.36 \times 60' = 21.6'$.

    There are $60$ minutes in a degree.

  2. $0.6' = 0.6 \times 60'' = 36''$.

    There are $60$ seconds in a minute.

  3. $47.36^\circ = 47^\circ\,21'\,36''$.

    Collect the whole parts: $47$ degrees, $21$ minutes, $36$ seconds.

$47^\circ\,21'\,36''$

Next in the book: Example 10-2.

Vertical angles with algebra

Two lines cross. One angle measures $(3x + 10)^\circ$ and the angle vertical to it measures $(5x - 30)^\circ$. Find all four angles.

  1. $3x + 10 = 5x - 30$, so $x = 20$.

    Vertical angles are equal.

  2. Those two angles are $70^\circ$; the other two are $180^\circ - 70^\circ = 110^\circ$.

    Neighbouring angles at a crossing are supplementary.

$70^\circ, 110^\circ, 70^\circ, 110^\circ$

Supplement versus complement

The supplement of an angle is four times its complement. Find the angle.

  1. Let the angle be $x$: $180 - x = 4(90 - x)$.

    Translate the words directly.

  2. $180 - x = 360 - 4x$, so $3x = 180$ and $x = 60$.

    Collect like terms.

  3. Check: supplement $120^\circ$, complement $30^\circ$, and $120 = 4 \cdot 30$. ✓

    Always substitute back into the words.

$60^\circ$

Angle between clock hands

What is the angle between the hour and minute hands at 3:30?

  1. The minute hand moves $360^\circ/60 = 6^\circ$ per minute; at :30 it points at $180^\circ$ (measured clockwise from 12).

    Measure both hands from the 12 o'clock position.

  2. The hour hand moves $30^\circ$ per hour, which is $0.5^\circ$ per minute. At 3:30 it is at $3 \cdot 30^\circ + 30 \cdot 0.5^\circ = 105^\circ$.

    The hour hand has moved halfway from the 3 toward the 4.

  3. Angle $= 180^\circ - 105^\circ = 75^\circ$.

    Subtract the two positions.

$75^\circ$

When do the hands meet?

At what time after 3:00 do the hour and minute hands first point in exactly the same direction?

  1. After $t$ minutes, the minute hand is at $6t$ degrees and the hour hand at $90 + 0.5t$ degrees.

    At 3:00 the hour hand starts at $90^\circ$; each hand turns at its own steady rate.

  2. Set them equal: $6t = 90 + 0.5t$, so $5.5t = 90$ and $t = \dfrac{180}{11} = 16\tfrac{4}{11}$ minutes.

    The minute hand gains $5.5^\circ$ per minute and must make up $90^\circ$.

  3. $\tfrac{4}{11}$ minute $= \tfrac{240}{11} \approx 21.8$ seconds, so the time is about 3:16:22.

    Convert the fraction of a minute into seconds.

At $3{:}16\tfrac{4}{11}$, about 3:16:22.

Treat moving hands as a rate problem: the gap closes at the difference of the rates.

10.3 Angles and Parallel Lines

Hook

Tear the three corners off a paper triangle and line them up point to point. They always make a straight line. Why? Slide one of the triangle's sides along a parallel line and the angles fall into place. Parallel lines turn angle questions into bookkeeping.

Concept

Parallel lines ($\ell \parallel m$) lie in the same plane and never meet. A line crossing both is a transversal; it makes eight angles, four at each crossing.

Corresponding angles are equal. Alternate interior angles are equal. Same-side interior angles are supplementary.

In fact, only two angle sizes appear: some angle $\theta$ and its supplement $180^\circ - \theta$. In diagrams, mark equal angles with the same number of small arcs, and equal lengths with the same number of tick marks. A careful, well-marked diagram is the most important tool in geometry.

Triangle sum. Through vertex $C$ of triangle $ABC$, draw the line parallel to $\overline{AB}$. The two angles it makes with $\overline{CA}$ and $\overline{CB}$ are alternate interior angles equal to $\angle A$ and $\angle B$, and together with $\angle C$ they form a straight line. So

$$\angle A + \angle B + \angle C = 180^\circ.$$

Exterior angle theorem. Extend a side past a vertex. The exterior angle formed is supplementary to the interior angle there, so it equals the sum of the other two (remote interior) angles.

When the standard moves don't work, look for a good place to draw an extra line parallel to one you already have.

Fill in all eight angles

A transversal crosses two parallel lines, making a $65^\circ$ angle at one crossing. What are the eight angles?

  1. At that crossing: $65^\circ$, its vertical angle $65^\circ$, and two supplements $115^\circ$.

    Vertical angles are equal; neighbouring angles are supplementary.

  2. The other crossing has the same four angles in the same positions.

    Corresponding angles are equal, so the second crossing copies the first.

Four angles of $65^\circ$ and four of $115^\circ$.

Same-side interior angles

Two same-side interior angles of parallel lines measure $3x$ and $2x$ degrees. Find them.

  1. $3x + 2x = 180$, so $x = 36$.

    Same-side interior angles are supplementary.

  2. The angles are $108^\circ$ and $72^\circ$.

    Substitute $x = 36$.

$108^\circ$ and $72^\circ$

The third angle

Two angles of a triangle are $47^\circ$ and $68^\circ$. Find the third.

  1. $180^\circ - 47^\circ - 68^\circ = 65^\circ$.

    The three angles of a triangle sum to $180^\circ$.

$65^\circ$

Angles in a ratio

The angles of a triangle are in the ratio $2 : 3 : 4$. Find them.

  1. Write them as $2k, 3k, 4k$: $9k = 180$, so $k = 20$.

    A ratio means equal-sized "parts"; there are $9$ parts in $180^\circ$.

  2. $40^\circ, 60^\circ, 80^\circ$.

    Multiply each part count by $20^\circ$.

$40^\circ$, $60^\circ$, $80^\circ$

An exterior angle

An exterior angle of a triangle is $120^\circ$. Of the two remote interior angles, one is twice the other. Find all three interior angles.

  1. Remote interior angles $x$ and $2x$: $x + 2x = 120$, so $x = 40$.

    Exterior angle theorem: the exterior angle equals the sum of the remote interior angles.

  2. Interior angles: $40^\circ$, $80^\circ$, and $180^\circ - 120^\circ = 60^\circ$.

    The interior angle at the extended vertex is supplementary to the exterior angle. Check: $40 + 80 + 60 = 180$. ✓

$40^\circ$, $80^\circ$, $60^\circ$

A bend between parallel lines

Lines $\ell$ and $m$ are parallel. A path goes from a point on $\ell$ to point $P$ between the lines, then to a point on $m$. The first piece makes a $30^\circ$ angle with $\ell$ and the second makes a $45^\circ$ angle with $m$, as shown. Find the angle of the bend at $P$.

  1. Draw the line through $P$ parallel to $\ell$ and $m$ (dashed). It splits the bend into two angles.

    An extra parallel line creates alternate interior angles with each piece of the path.

  2. The upper part equals $30^\circ$ and the lower part equals $45^\circ$.

    Each is an alternate interior angle for a transversal crossing the dashed line and $\ell$ (or $m$).

  3. Bend $= 30^\circ + 45^\circ = 75^\circ$.

    The two parts together make the angle at $P$.

$75^\circ$

A bend between parallel lines equals the sum of the angles the two pieces make with the lines.

The points of a star

A five-pointed star has tips $A, B, C, D, E$ in order around it, drawn with the five strokes $\overline{AC}, \overline{CE}, \overline{EB}, \overline{BD}, \overline{DA}$. Show that the five tip angles add to $180^\circ$, whatever the shape of the star.

  1. Let stroke $\overline{EB}$ cross $\overline{AC}$ at $X$ and $\overline{AD}$ at $Y$. We will show the angles of triangle $AXY$ are $\angle A$, $\angle C + \angle E$ and $\angle B + \angle D$.

    The plan: gather all five tip angles into one triangle, whose angles add to $180^\circ$.

  2. Triangle $CEX$ has angles $\angle C$ and $\angle E$ at the tips. Its exterior angle at $X$ (between ray $XA$, which extends $\overline{CX}$, and ray $XE$) is $\angle AXY = \angle C + \angle E$.

    Exterior angle theorem. $Y$ lies on ray $XE$, so this exterior angle is the angle of triangle $AXY$ at $X$.

  3. Likewise triangle $BDY$ has exterior angle $\angle AYX = \angle B + \angle D$ at $Y$.

    The same argument with the strokes through $Y$.

  4. In triangle $AXY$: $\angle A + (\angle C + \angle E) + (\angle B + \angle D) = 180^\circ$.

    Triangle angle sum.

The tip angles always sum to $180^\circ$.

Exterior angles let you gather scattered angles into a single triangle.

10.4 Arcs, Segments, Sectors, and Angles

Hook

A pizza of radius $20$ cm is cut into $8$ equal slices. Each slice's crust is one eighth of the circumference and each slice's area is one eighth of the pizza. Measuring arcs and sectors is just a matter of knowing what fraction of the whole circle you have.

Concept

An arc can be measured two ways: by its angle (the central angle $\angle AOB$ that cuts it off, written beside the arc in diagrams) or by its length. The fraction of the circle is the same either way:

$$\frac{\text{arc length}}{2\pi r} = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} = \frac{\theta_{\text{rad}}}{2\pi}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \text{arc length} = r\,\theta_{\text{rad}}.$$

A sector with central angle $\theta$ is the same fraction of the disk:

$$\text{sector area} = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\,\pi r^2 = \frac12 r^2 \theta_{\text{rad}} = \frac12 r \cdot (\text{arc length}).$$

A circular segment (between a chord $\overline{AB}$ and its arc) is the sector $AOB$ minus the triangle $AOB$. Chapter 11 gives the tools for triangle areas; right and equilateral triangles are already within reach.

The radian formulas are why radians are "natural": arc length is simply radius times angle.

A sixth of a circle

In a circle of radius $6$, a central angle of $60^\circ$ cuts off an arc. Find the arc length and the sector area.

  1. Fraction: $\dfrac{60^\circ}{360^\circ} = \dfrac16$.

    Work out the fraction of the circle once and use it twice.

  2. Arc $= \dfrac16 \cdot 2\pi(6) = 2\pi$; sector $= \dfrac16 \cdot \pi(6)^2 = 6\pi$.

    Take the fraction of the circumference and of the area.

Arc $2\pi$; sector area $6\pi$.

Using radians directly

A sector of radius $4$ has central angle $1.5$ radians. Find its arc length and area.

  1. Arc $= r\theta = 4(1.5) = 6$.

    With radians, arc length is radius times angle.

  2. Area $= \tfrac12 r^2\theta = \tfrac12 (16)(1.5) = 12$.

    Or $\tfrac12 r \cdot \text{arc} = \tfrac12 (4)(6) = 12$.

Arc $6$; area $12$.

Find the angle from the arc

An arc of length $5\pi$ lies on a circle of radius $10$. What is its central angle in degrees?

  1. $\dfrac{5\pi}{2\pi \cdot 10} = \dfrac14$ of the circle.

    Compare the arc with the whole circumference $20\pi$.

  2. $\dfrac14 \cdot 360^\circ = 90^\circ$.

    The angle is the same fraction of $360^\circ$.

$90^\circ$

Find the angle from the area

A sector of a circle of radius $6$ has area $15\pi$. Find its central angle.

  1. The whole disk has area $36\pi$, so the sector is $\dfrac{15}{36} = \dfrac{5}{12}$ of it.

    Area fraction equals angle fraction.

  2. $\dfrac{5}{12}\cdot 360^\circ = 150^\circ$.

    Multiply the fraction by a full turn.

$150^\circ$

A quarter-circle segment

In a circle of radius $4$, chord $\overline{AB}$ cuts off a $90^\circ$ arc. Find the area of the circular segment between the chord and that arc.

  1. Sector $AOB$: $\dfrac14 \pi (4)^2 = 4\pi$.

    A $90^\circ$ sector is a quarter of the disk.

  2. Triangle $AOB$ is a right triangle with legs $4$ and $4$: area $\tfrac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 4 = 8$.

    The two radii are perpendicular, so they serve as base and height.

  3. Segment $= 4\pi - 8 \approx 4.57$.

    Segment $=$ sector $-$ triangle.

$4\pi - 8$

A sixty-degree segment

In a circle of radius $6$, chord $\overline{AB}$ cuts off a $60^\circ$ arc. Find the area of the segment. (An equilateral triangle of side $s$ has area $\frac{\sqrt3}{4}s^2$; see Chapter 11.)

  1. Sector: $\dfrac16 \pi (6)^2 = 6\pi$.

    $60^\circ$ is one sixth of a turn.

  2. Triangle $AOB$ is equilateral with side $6$, so its area is $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{4}\cdot 36 = 9\sqrt3$.

    $OA = OB$, so the other two angles are equal and must each be $60^\circ$.

  3. Segment $= 6\pi - 9\sqrt3 \approx 3.26$.

    Sector minus triangle.

$6\pi - 9\sqrt3$

Recover the sector from its arc and area

A sector has arc length $4\pi$ and area $12\pi$. Find its radius and its central angle in degrees.

  1. Area $= \tfrac12 r \cdot (\text{arc length})$: $12\pi = \tfrac12 r (4\pi) = 2\pi r$, so $r = 6$.

    A sector behaves like a triangle with "base" the arc and "height" the radius.

  2. $\theta = \dfrac{\text{arc}}{r} = \dfrac{4\pi}{6} = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}$ radians $= 120^\circ$.

    Arc length $= r\theta$ in radians, then convert.

  3. Check: $\dfrac{120}{360}\pi(36) = 12\pi$ ✓ and $\dfrac{120}{360}\cdot 12\pi = 4\pi$ ✓.

    Both given facts hold.

$r = 6$, $\theta = 120^\circ$

Sector area $= \frac12 r \times$ arc length, just like $\frac12 \times$ base $\times$ height.

10.5 Angles Formed by Lines Intersecting a Circle

Hook

Stand anywhere on the curved edge of a circular stage and look at the two ends of its straight front edge. Walk along the curve: the ends stay the same angle apart in your view, no matter where you stand. Circles keep angles constant in a way that makes them a geometry superpower.

Concept

A central angle has its vertex at the center; it equals its arc. Every other angle formed by lines meeting a circle is measured by half of an arc, or half a sum or difference of arcs:

Inscribed angle (vertex on the circle, sides are chords): $\tfrac12$ the intercepted arc.

Tangent and chord (vertex on the circle): $\tfrac12$ the intercepted arc.

Two chords crossing inside: $\tfrac12$ (sum of the arcs cut off by the angle and by its vertical angle).

Two secants, a secant and a tangent, or two tangents meeting outside: $\tfrac12$ (far arc $-$ near arc).

Some consequences are used constantly:

  • An angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle (it cuts off $180^\circ$).
  • A radius drawn to the point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent.
  • Inscribed angles that cut off the same arc are equal. If you need to show two angles are equal and a circle passes through both vertices and the two points where their sides cross the circle, you're done.
  • Opposite angles of a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle add to $180^\circ$, since their arcs make up the whole circle.

An inscribed angle

An inscribed angle cuts off an arc of $100^\circ$. How big is the angle? How big is the central angle on the same arc?

  1. Inscribed angle $= \tfrac12 \cdot 100^\circ = 50^\circ$.

    An inscribed angle is half its arc.

  2. Central angle $= 100^\circ$.

    A central angle equals its arc, so it is twice the inscribed angle.

$50^\circ$; the central angle is $100^\circ$.

A triangle on a diameter

Triangle $ABC$ is inscribed in a circle with $\overline{AB}$ a diameter. If $\angle A = 35^\circ$, find $\angle B$ and $\angle C$.

  1. $\angle C = 90^\circ$.

    $\angle C$ is inscribed in a semicircle, cutting off $180^\circ$.

  2. $\angle B = 180^\circ - 90^\circ - 35^\circ = 55^\circ$.

    Triangle angle sum.

$\angle B = 55^\circ$, $\angle C = 90^\circ$

Two chords crossing

Two chords cross inside a circle. The angle at the crossing and its vertical angle cut off arcs of $70^\circ$ and $50^\circ$. Find all four angles at the crossing.

  1. $\tfrac12(70^\circ + 50^\circ) = 60^\circ$.

    Two chords: half the sum of the arcs cut off by the angle and its vertical angle.

  2. The other pair: $180^\circ - 60^\circ = 120^\circ$.

    Neighbouring angles at the crossing are supplementary.

$60^\circ, 120^\circ, 60^\circ, 120^\circ$

Two secants from outside

Two secants from point $P$ outside a circle cut off a far arc of $120^\circ$ and a near arc of $40^\circ$. Find $\angle P$.

  1. $\angle P = \tfrac12(120^\circ - 40^\circ) = 40^\circ$.

    From outside, the angle is half the difference of the far and near arcs.

$40^\circ$

A tangent and a chord

A tangent at $A$ and a chord $\overline{AB}$ form an angle that cuts off an arc $\overset{\frown}{AB}$ of $140^\circ$. Find the angle, and any inscribed angle on the other side of the chord that cuts off the same arc.

  1. Tangent-chord angle $= \tfrac12 \cdot 140^\circ = 70^\circ$.

    A tangent-chord angle is half its arc.

  2. An inscribed angle $\angle ACB$ on $\overset{\frown}{AB}$ is also $70^\circ$.

    Both are half the same arc, so they are equal.

$70^\circ$ for both.

Two tangents

Tangents from $P$ touch a circle at $A$ and $B$. The minor arc $\overset{\frown}{AB}$ is $110^\circ$. Find $\angle APB$.

  1. Major arc $= 360^\circ - 110^\circ = 250^\circ$.

    The two tangents cut off both arcs: the far one and the near one.

  2. $\angle P = \tfrac12(250^\circ - 110^\circ) = 70^\circ$.

    Half the difference, as for secants.

  3. Check another way: radii $OA$, $OB$ are perpendicular to the tangents, so quadrilateral $OAPB$ has angles $90^\circ + 90^\circ + 110^\circ + \angle P = 360^\circ$, giving $70^\circ$. ✓

    A quadrilateral's angles sum to $360^\circ$ (two triangles).

$70^\circ$

For two tangents, $\angle P = 180^\circ - (\text{minor arc})$.

Opposite angles of an inscribed quadrilateral

Quadrilateral $ABCD$ is inscribed in a circle with $\angle A = 75^\circ$ and $\angle B = 100^\circ$. Find $\angle C$ and $\angle D$.

  1. $\angle A$ cuts off arc $\overset{\frown}{BCD}$ and $\angle C$ cuts off arc $\overset{\frown}{DAB}$; together these arcs are the whole circle.

    Each angle opens toward the arc opposite its vertex.

  2. So $\angle A + \angle C = \tfrac12 \cdot 360^\circ = 180^\circ$, giving $\angle C = 105^\circ$. Likewise $\angle D = 80^\circ$.

    Each inscribed angle is half its arc. Check: $75 + 100 + 105 + 80 = 360$. ✓

$\angle C = 105^\circ$, $\angle D = 80^\circ$

Equal inscribed angles at work

Points $A, B, C, D$ lie on a circle in that order. $\angle BAC = 30^\circ$ and $\angle CAD = 40^\circ$. Find the three angles of triangle $BCD$.

  1. $\angle BDC$ and $\angle BAC$ both cut off arc $\overset{\frown}{BC}$, so $\angle BDC = 30^\circ$.

    Inscribed angles on the same arc are equal.

  2. $\angle CBD$ and $\angle CAD$ both cut off arc $\overset{\frown}{CD}$, so $\angle CBD = 40^\circ$.

    Same reason, a different arc.

  3. $\angle BCD = 180^\circ - 30^\circ - 40^\circ = 110^\circ$.

    Triangle sum. Also $\angle BCD = 180^\circ - \angle BAD = 180^\circ - 70^\circ$, since $ABCD$ is inscribed. ✓

$\angle BDC = 30^\circ$, $\angle CBD = 40^\circ$, $\angle BCD = 110^\circ$

To move an angle somewhere useful, slide its vertex along the circle.

10.6 The Burden of Proof

Hook

Why should an angle on the edge of a circle be exactly half the angle at the center? Measuring a hundred drawings would convince you, but it wouldn't tell you why, or guarantee the hundred-and-first. A proof does both, and the moves it uses (adding a line, spotting isosceles triangles, splitting into cases) will solve problems for years.

Concept

The key fact: any triangle with two radii as sides is isosceles, so its two base angles are equal (see Chapter 11). To prove the inscribed angle theorem, $\angle ACB = \tfrac12 \angle AOB$:

  1. Simplest case: the center lies on a side, say on $\overline{CB}$. Triangle $AOC$ is isosceles ($OA = OC$), so $\angle OAC = \angle OCA = x$. The angle $\angle AOB$ is an exterior angle of triangle $AOC$, so it equals $x + x = 2x$.
  2. Center inside the angle. Draw the diameter from $C$ through $O$. It splits $\angle ACB$ into $x + y$, and by case 1 the central angle splits into $2x + 2y$.
  3. Center outside the angle. The same diameter now gives a difference: $\angle ACB = x - y$ and $\angle AOB = 2x - 2y$.

Lessons in technique:

  • Add a line that creates triangles you understand (here, the diameter through $C$).
  • Use every given condition: "points on a circle" means equal radii, which means isosceles triangles.
  • Work backwards from what you want to what you need to show.
  • Check all cases: a proof that only works for one picture isn't finished.
  • Avoid circular reasoning: don't use a fact whose proof depends on what you are proving.

With the inscribed angle theorem in hand, the other rules follow by drawing one extra chord and using the exterior angle theorem.

Radii make isosceles triangles

In circle $O$, chord $\overline{AB}$ makes $\angle OAB = 35^\circ$. Find $\angle OBA$ and $\angle AOB$.

  1. $OA = OB$ (radii), so $\angle OBA = \angle OAB = 35^\circ$.

    Base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal.

  2. $\angle AOB = 180^\circ - 70^\circ = 110^\circ$.

    Triangle angle sum.

$\angle OBA = 35^\circ$, $\angle AOB = 110^\circ$

Case 1: the center on a side

$\overline{BC}$ is a diameter of circle $O$ and $A$ is another point on the circle. Prove that $\angle AOB = 2\angle ACB$.

  1. Let $\angle ACB = \angle ACO = x$. Since $OA = OC$, triangle $AOC$ is isosceles and $\angle OAC = x$.

    $O$ lies on $\overline{CB}$, so $\angle ACB$ and $\angle ACO$ are the same angle.

  2. $\angle AOB$ is an exterior angle of triangle $AOC$ (side $\overline{CO}$ extended through $O$ to $B$).

    $C$, $O$, $B$ are collinear.

  3. So $\angle AOB = \angle OAC + \angle OCA = 2x = 2\angle ACB$.

    Exterior angle theorem.

$\angle AOB = 2\angle ACB$, as required.

Case 2 with numbers

The center $O$ is inside $\angle ACB$, with $\angle OCA = 20^\circ$ and $\angle OCB = 30^\circ$. Find $\angle AOB$ by drawing the diameter $\overline{CD}$.

  1. By case 1 applied to each half: $\angle AOD = 2 \cdot 20^\circ = 40^\circ$ and $\angle BOD = 2 \cdot 30^\circ = 60^\circ$.

    The diameter $\overline{CD}$ puts the center on a side of each smaller angle.

  2. $\angle AOB = 40^\circ + 60^\circ = 100^\circ = 2(20^\circ + 30^\circ)$.

    The diameter lies between $\overline{OA}$ and $\overline{OB}$, so the parts add.

$100^\circ$, twice $\angle ACB = 50^\circ$.

Case 3: the center outside

The center $O$ is outside $\angle ACB$, with diameter $\overline{CD}$ making $\angle DCB = 50^\circ$ and $\angle DCA = 20^\circ$ (so ray $CA$ lies between rays $CD$ and $CB$). Find $\angle ACB$ and $\angle AOB$.

  1. $\angle ACB = 50^\circ - 20^\circ = 30^\circ$.

    Now the angles subtract instead of add.

  2. Case 1 gives $\angle BOD = 100^\circ$ and $\angle AOD = 40^\circ$, so $\angle AOB = 100^\circ - 40^\circ = 60^\circ$.

    Ray $OA$ lies between rays $OD$ and $OB$.

  3. $60^\circ = 2 \cdot 30^\circ$. ✓

    The theorem holds in this case too.

$\angle ACB = 30^\circ$, $\angle AOB = 60^\circ$

A complete proof handles every position the figure can take.

Prove the two-chord rule

Chords $\overline{AC}$ and $\overline{BD}$ cross at $E$ inside a circle. Prove that $\angle AEB = \tfrac12\left(\overset{\frown}{AB} + \overset{\frown}{CD}\right)$.

  1. Draw chord $\overline{BC}$, forming triangle $EBC$.

    Its angles at $B$ and $C$ are inscribed angles on the arcs we want.

  2. $\angle AEB$ is an exterior angle of triangle $EBC$, so $\angle AEB = \angle EBC + \angle ECB$.

    $A$, $E$, $C$ are collinear, so $\angle AEB$ is next to $\angle BEC$ on a straight line.

  3. $\angle EBC = \angle DBC = \tfrac12 \overset{\frown}{CD}$ and $\angle ECB = \angle ACB = \tfrac12 \overset{\frown}{AB}$.

    Inscribed angle theorem.

  4. So $\angle AEB = \tfrac12\left(\overset{\frown}{AB} + \overset{\frown}{CD}\right)$.

    Add the two halves.

Proved: $\angle AEB = \tfrac12\left(\overset{\frown}{AB} + \overset{\frown}{CD}\right)$.

Prove the two-secant rule

From $P$ outside a circle, one secant meets the circle at $A$ then $B$, and another at $C$ then $D$ ($A$, $C$ nearer to $P$). Prove that $\angle P = \tfrac12\left(\overset{\frown}{BD} - \overset{\frown}{AC}\right)$.

  1. Draw chord $\overline{BC}$, forming triangle $PBC$.

    Again, one extra chord produces inscribed angles on the two arcs.

  2. $\angle BCD$ is an exterior angle of triangle $PBC$ at $C$, since $P$, $C$, $D$ are collinear. So $\angle BCD = \angle P + \angle PBC$.

    Exterior angle theorem.

  3. $\angle BCD = \tfrac12 \overset{\frown}{BD}$ and $\angle PBC = \angle ABC = \tfrac12 \overset{\frown}{AC}$.

    Both are inscribed angles.

  4. So $\angle P = \tfrac12 \overset{\frown}{BD} - \tfrac12 \overset{\frown}{AC} = \tfrac12\left(\overset{\frown}{BD} - \overset{\frown}{AC}\right)$.

    Rearrange the equation from the exterior angle.

Proved: $\angle P = \tfrac12\left(\overset{\frown}{BD} - \overset{\frown}{AC}\right)$.

Add one chord, find an exterior angle, and replace inscribed angles by half-arcs.
