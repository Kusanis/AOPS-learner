Chapter 15 · Geometry

Areas

You already know the area formulas for triangles, quadrilaterals and circles. This chapter is about using them cleverly: comparing areas without computing them, sliding a vertex without changing an area, and finding the area of strange shaded regions by adding and subtracting simple pieces like a jigsaw.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • If two figures are similar with ratio $k$, their areas have ratio $k^2$. Going back, the length ratio is the square root of the area ratio.
  • Triangles with the same altitude have areas in the ratio of their bases; triangles with the same base have areas in the ratio of their altitudes.
  • "Same base" is often literally the same segment, and "same altitude" often means a shared vertex with bases on one line.
  • Sliding a vertex parallel to the opposite side keeps a triangle's area fixed.
  • For a strange region, find simple pieces (triangles, rectangles, sectors) and add or subtract them.
  • Circular segment $=$ sector $-$ triangle. Draw radii to the ends of every arc.
  • Overlaps counted twice can be found by "adding everything, then subtracting the whole."

15.1 Similar Figures

Hook

A 16-inch pizza costs a bit more than an 8-inch one. Is it a good deal? Its diameter is only twice as big, but it has four times as much pizza. Doubling every length quadruples the area, and that is true for every shape, not just circles.

Concept

Two figures are similar if one is a scaled copy of the other: every length is multiplied by the same factor $k$. Area is length times length, so it gets multiplied by $k \cdot k$:

$$\frac{\text{area of copy}}{\text{area of original}} = k^2, \qquad \text{so} \qquad k = \sqrt{\frac{\text{area of copy}}{\text{area of original}}}.$$

You can see it directly: a triangle scaled by $3$ can be tiled by exactly $3^2 = 9$ copies of the original. The same goes for any shape, because any shape can be approximated by tiny squares, and every tiny square grows by $k^2$.

All circles are similar, all squares are similar, and all equilateral triangles are similar. Two triangles are similar when you can prove it by AA, SAS or SSS similarity (Chapter 11). Once you know two figures are similar, any pair of corresponding lengths (sides, heights, diagonals, perimeters, radii) gives the ratio $k$.

Perimeters scale by $k$, areas by $k^2$. Don't mix them up.

Triple the sides

Triangle $T$ has area $5$. A similar triangle has sides $3$ times as long. Find its area.

  1. Area ratio $= 3^2 = 9$, so the area is $9 \times 5 = 45$.

    Areas scale by the square of the length ratio.

$45$

Two circles

One circle has radius $2$ and another has radius $6$. How many times larger is the second circle's area?

  1. The radius ratio is $3$, so the area ratio is $9$.

    All circles are similar, and the radius is a corresponding length.

  2. Check: $\pi \cdot 6^2 = 36\pi$ and $\pi \cdot 2^2 = 4\pi$; $36\pi / 4\pi = 9$.

    Computing both areas confirms the shortcut.

$9$ times

From areas back to lengths

Two similar pentagons have areas $49$ and $25$. The smaller one has perimeter $20$. Find the perimeter of the larger.

  1. Length ratio $= \sqrt{49/25} = 7/5$.

    Take the square root of the area ratio to get the length ratio.

  2. Perimeter $= 20 \times \tfrac75 = 28$.

    Perimeter is a length, so it scales by $k$, not $k^2$.

$28$

The midpoint triangle

A triangle has area $60$. Joining the midpoints of its sides forms a smaller triangle. Find its area.

  1. Each side of the midpoint triangle is half of a side of the original, so the two are similar with $k = \tfrac12$.

    A segment joining two midpoints is parallel to the third side and half as long; that gives SSS similarity.

  2. Area $= \left(\tfrac12\right)^2 \cdot 60 = 15$.

    Square the length ratio.

  3. Check: the three corner triangles are also similar with $k = \tfrac12$, so the four pieces are $15$ each, total $60$.

    The midpoint triangle and the three corners tile the original.

$15$

Diagonals of two squares

The area of one square is $8$ times the area of another. Find the ratio of their diagonals.

  1. Length ratio $= \sqrt8 = 2\sqrt2$.

    All squares are similar, and a diagonal is a corresponding length.

$2\sqrt2 : 1$

Triangles in a trapezoid

In trapezoid $ABCD$ with $AB \parallel CD$, $AB = 5$, $CD = 15$, and the diagonals meet at $E$. If $[ABE] = 4$, find $[CDE]$.

  1. $\triangle ABE \sim \triangle CDE$ by AA.

    Alternate angles along the parallel bases: $\angle BAE = \angle DCE$ and $\angle ABE = \angle CDE$.

  2. $k = CD / AB = 3$, so $[CDE] = 9 \times 4 = 36$.

    Corresponding sides give the ratio; square it for areas.

$36$

Cutting a triangle in half

A triangle has height $10$. A line parallel to the base cuts off a small triangle at the top with exactly half the area. How far below the apex is the line?

  1. The small triangle is similar to the whole (AA, because the line is parallel to the base).

    Parallel lines give equal corresponding angles.

  2. Area ratio $\tfrac12$, so $k = \sqrt{\tfrac12} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$.

    Lengths scale by the square root of the area ratio.

  3. Height of the small triangle $= \dfrac{10}{\sqrt2} = 5\sqrt2 \approx 7.07$.

    Heights are corresponding lengths too. Not $5$: halving the height would leave only a quarter of the area.

$5\sqrt2 \approx 7.07$

To halve an area, scale lengths by $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, not $\tfrac12$.

Three equal strips

Triangle $ABC$ has base $BC = 12$. Two lines parallel to $BC$ divide it into three regions of equal area. Find the lengths of the two parallel segments inside the triangle.

  1. Measured from $A$, the two lines cut off triangles with $\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac23$ of the total area.

    The top triangle is one region; the top triangle plus the next strip is two regions.

  2. Their length ratios are $\sqrt{\tfrac13}$ and $\sqrt{\tfrac23}$.

    Each cut-off triangle is similar to $ABC$.

  3. Segments: $12\sqrt{\tfrac13} = \dfrac{12}{\sqrt3} = 4\sqrt3$ and $12\sqrt{\tfrac23} = \dfrac{12\sqrt6}{3} = 4\sqrt6$.

    Each parallel segment is the base of its similar triangle.

$4\sqrt3 \approx 6.93$ and $4\sqrt6 \approx 9.80$

Think about the cut-off triangles from the apex, not the strips.

15.2 Same Base/Same Altitude

Hook

Stretch a rubber band between two pins to make a base, and loop it around a pencil held at a fixed height above. Slide the pencil left and right: the triangle leans wildly, yet its area never changes. Area only cares about base and height.

Concept

Since $[\triangle] = \tfrac12 \cdot \text{base} \cdot \text{height}$, if two triangles share a height $h$, the $\tfrac12 h$ cancels when we compare them:

$$\text{same altitude:}\quad \frac{[ABD]}{[ADC]} = \frac{BD}{DC}, \qquad \text{same base:}\quad \frac{[XYZ_1]}{[XYZ_2]} = \frac{h_1}{h_2}.$$

The most common setting: a vertex $A$ and a point $D$ on the opposite side $BC$. Triangles $ABD$ and $ADC$ have the same altitude from $A$, so the segment $AD$ splits the area in the ratio $BD : DC$. In particular a median splits a triangle into two equal areas.

The flip side: if $X$ moves along a line parallel to $AB$, the height from $X$ to $AB$ never changes, so $[ABX]$ stays the same. This is how you prove two areas are equal without computing either one.

Notation reminder: $[ABC]$ means the area of triangle $ABC$.

Splitting by a cevian

In triangle $ABC$, point $D$ on $BC$ has $BD = 3$ and $DC = 5$. If $[ABC] = 40$, find $[ABD]$ and $[ADC]$.

  1. $ABD$ and $ADC$ have the same altitude from $A$, so $[ABD] : [ADC] = 3 : 5$.

    Their bases lie on the same line $BC$.

  2. $[ABD] = \tfrac38 \cdot 40 = 15$, $[ADC] = \tfrac58 \cdot 40 = 25$.

    Split $40$ in the ratio $3 : 5$.

$[ABD] = 15$, $[ADC] = 25$

Sliding the apex

Segment $AB$ has length $8$, and line $\ell$ is parallel to $AB$ at distance $5$. For points $X$ on $\ell$, what values can $[ABX]$ take?

  1. For every $X$ on $\ell$, the height from $X$ to $AB$ is $5$.

    Parallel lines are the same distance apart everywhere.

  2. $[ABX] = \tfrac12 \cdot 8 \cdot 5 = 20$, always.

    Same base, same height, same area.

Only $20$.

Medians make six equal pieces

The three medians of a triangle with area $72$ meet at the centroid $G$. Find the area of each of the six small triangles, and $[ABG]$.

  1. Median $AD$ splits $ABC$ into two halves of area $36$; so $[ADC] = 36$.

    $BD = DC$ and the triangles share the altitude from $A$.

  2. $G$ divides $AD$ with $GD = \tfrac13 AD$, so $[GDC] = \tfrac13 [ADC] = 12$.

    $GDC$ and $ADC$ share the altitude from $C$, and their bases lie on line $AD$. (The centroid fact is from Chapter 11.)

  3. By the same argument all six pieces have area $12$, and $[ABG]$ is two of them: $24$.

    $ABG$ consists of the two small triangles touching side $AB$.

Each piece $12$; $[ABG] = 24$.

Two steps of ratios

In triangle $ABC$ with area $54$, point $D$ on $AB$ has $AD : DB = 2 : 1$, and $E$ is the midpoint of $AC$. Find $[ADE]$.

  1. $[ADC] = \tfrac23 [ABC] = 36$.

    $ADC$ and $ABC$ share the altitude from $C$; their bases are $AD$ and $AB$, with $AD = \tfrac23 AB$.

  2. $[ADE] = \tfrac12 [ADC] = 18$.

    $ADE$ and $ADC$ share the altitude from $D$; their bases are $AE$ and $AC$, with $AE = \tfrac12 AC$.

$18$

Chain several same-altitude steps, choosing a new apex each time.

A point inside a cevian

In triangle $ABC$ with area $60$, $D$ is on $BC$ with $BD : DC = 1 : 2$, and $E$ is on $AD$ with $AE : ED = 3 : 1$. Find $[BED]$.

  1. $[ABD] = \tfrac13 \cdot 60 = 20$.

    Same altitude from $A$; $BD$ is one third of $BC$.

  2. $[BED] = \tfrac{ED}{AD} \cdot [BAD] = \tfrac14 \cdot 20 = 5$.

    Both have apex $B$ and bases $ED$ and $AD$ on line $AD$, so they share the altitude from $B$.

$5$

Equal side triangles in a trapezoid

In trapezoid $ABCD$ with $AB \parallel CD$, the diagonals meet at $E$. Prove $[ADE] = [BCE]$.

  1. $[ADC] = [BDC]$.

    Same base $DC$, and $A$ and $B$ are the same distance from $DC$ because $AB \parallel DC$.

  2. $[ADE] = [ADC] - [DEC]$ and $[BCE] = [BDC] - [DEC]$.

    Each big triangle is a side triangle plus the bottom triangle $DEC$.

  3. Equal amounts minus the same amount are equal, so $[ADE] = [BCE]$.

    Subtracting a shared piece preserves equality.

$[ADE] = [BCE]$

The whole trapezoid from one small triangle

In trapezoid $ABCD$, $AB \parallel CD$, $AB = 5$, $CD = 15$, and the diagonals meet at $E$. If $[ABE] = 4$, find $[ABCD]$.

  1. $[CDE] = 3^2 \cdot 4 = 36$.

    $\triangle ABE \sim \triangle CDE$ with ratio $CD/AB = 3$ (as in §15.1).

  2. $\dfrac{[ADE]}{[ABE]} = \dfrac{DE}{EB} = 3$, so $[ADE] = 12$.

    Same altitude from $A$, bases on line $BD$; and $DE : EB = 3 : 1$ from the similar triangles.

  3. $[BCE] = [ADE] = 12$, so $[ABCD] = 4 + 36 + 12 + 12 = 64$.

    The previous example showed the two side triangles are equal.

$64$

Notice $64 = (\sqrt4 + \sqrt{36})^2$: the side triangles are each $\sqrt{[ABE][CDE]}$.

15.3 Complicated Figures

Hook

No formula gives the area of a crescent moon, a lens, or a four-leaf clover drawn with a compass. But each is what's left over when simple shapes overlap. Treat them like a jigsaw: find pieces whose areas you know, then add and subtract.

Concept

The method is always the same: identify simple pieces (rectangles, triangles, circles, sectors) and combine them with $+$ and $-$ to make the shaded region. Some reliable moves:

  • Draw radii to the ends of every arc. That separates sectors and circular segments from the rest.
  • A circular segment (between a chord and its arc) is a sector minus a triangle: with central angle $\theta$ and radius $r$, $\dfrac{\theta}{360^\circ}\pi r^2 - \tfrac12 r^2 \sin\theta$.
  • Look for right and equilateral triangles; draw the missing side to cut them out.
  • For polygons, split along diagonals or enclose in a rectangle and subtract the corners.
  • If shapes overlap, adding their areas counts the overlap twice: $\text{(sum of pieces)} - \text{(whole)} = \text{(overlap)}$.

A ring between two circles with the same centre is an annulus: its area is $\pi R^2 - \pi r^2$.

An annulus

Two circles share a centre and have radii $5$ and $3$. Find the area of the ring between them.

  1. Big circle: $25\pi$. Small circle: $9\pi$.

    The ring is the big disc with the small disc removed.

  2. Ring $= 25\pi - 9\pi = 16\pi$.

    Subtract the piece that isn't shaded.

$16\pi$

An L-shaped room

A room is a $10 \times 8$ rectangle with a $4 \times 3$ rectangle cut out of one corner. Find its area.

  1. $10 \times 8 - 4 \times 3 = 80 - 12 = 68$.

    Enclose the L in a rectangle and subtract the missing corner; it's quicker than cutting the L into two pieces.

$68$

The corners outside a circle

A circle is inscribed in a square of side $6$. Find the total area inside the square but outside the circle.

  1. The circle touches all four sides, so its diameter is $6$ and its radius is $3$.

    An inscribed circle's diameter equals the square's side.

  2. $36 - 9\pi \approx 7.73$.

    Square minus circle.

$36 - 9\pi$

A regular octagon, from a square

Find the area of a regular octagon with side $4$.

  1. Extend alternate sides to form a square. Each corner cut off is an isosceles right triangle with hypotenuse $4$, so legs $2\sqrt2$.

    The octagon's $135^\circ$ angles leave $45^\circ$ angles at the corners.

  2. Square side $= 2\sqrt2 + 4 + 2\sqrt2 = 4 + 4\sqrt2$; square area $= (4 + 4\sqrt2)^2 = 48 + 32\sqrt2$.

    $(4 + 4\sqrt2)^2 = 16 + 32\sqrt2 + 32$.

  3. Each corner has area $\tfrac12 (2\sqrt2)^2 = 4$, so the octagon is $48 + 32\sqrt2 - 16 = 32 + 32\sqrt2$.

    Square minus the four corners.

$32 + 32\sqrt2 \approx 77.25$

A circular segment

A chord of a circle of radius $6$ subtends a $90^\circ$ angle at the centre. Find the area between the chord and the shorter arc.

  1. Sector: $\dfrac{90}{360} \cdot 36\pi = 9\pi$.

    Draw the radii to the chord's ends; the sector is a quarter of the circle.

  2. Triangle: $\tfrac12 \cdot 6 \cdot 6 = 18$.

    The two radii are perpendicular legs.

  3. Segment $= 9\pi - 18$.

    The segment is the sector with the triangle removed.

$9\pi - 18 \approx 10.27$

A lens in a square

Square $ABCD$ has side $6$. Quarter circles of radius $6$ are drawn inside it, centred at $A$ and at $C$. Find the area of the lens-shaped region where they overlap.

  1. Both arcs pass through $B$ and $D$, so diagonal $BD$ cuts the lens into two pieces.

    $AB = AD = 6$ and $CB = CD = 6$.

  2. The piece on $C$'s side of $BD$ is a $90^\circ$ segment of the circle centred at $A$: area $9\pi - 18$ (previous example).

    Quarter circle at $A$ minus triangle $ABD$.

  3. The other piece is the same by symmetry, so the lens is $2(9\pi - 18) = 18\pi - 36$.

    Reflecting across $BD$ swaps the two circles.

$18\pi - 36 \approx 20.55$

A lens is two segments back to back: cut it along its chord.

Two circles through each other's centres

Two circles of radius $6$ each pass through the other's centre. Find the area of their overlap.

  1. Let the centres be $O_1, O_2$ and the crossing points $P, Q$. Then $O_1O_2 = O_1P = O_2P = 6$: triangle $O_1O_2P$ is equilateral, and so is $O_1O_2Q$.

    Every one of those segments is a radius of one of the circles.

  2. So $\angle PO_1Q = 120^\circ$. The segment of circle $O_1$ cut off by chord $PQ$ has area $\dfrac{120}{360}\cdot 36\pi - \tfrac12 \cdot 6^2 \sin 120^\circ = 12\pi - 9\sqrt3$.

    Sector minus triangle $PO_1Q$.

  3. The overlap is two such segments: $24\pi - 18\sqrt3$.

    Chord $PQ$ splits the lens into one segment from each circle.

$24\pi - 18\sqrt3 \approx 44.22$

Next in the book: Example 15-12, which uses the same idea with four circles.

The shoemaker's knife

Point $C$ lies on segment $AB$ with $AC = 6$ and $CB = 4$. Semicircles are drawn on the same side of $AB$ with diameters $AB$, $AC$ and $CB$. Find the area inside the big semicircle but outside the two small ones. Then show it equals the area of the circle whose diameter is the perpendicular segment from $C$ up to the big semicircle.

  1. A semicircle with diameter $d$ has area $\tfrac{\pi d^2}{8}$, so the region is $\tfrac{\pi}{8}(10^2 - 6^2 - 4^2) = \tfrac{\pi}{8}\cdot 48 = 6\pi$.

    Big semicircle minus the two small ones.

  2. Let $D$ be where the perpendicular at $C$ meets the big semicircle. $\angle ADB = 90^\circ$, and $\triangle ACD \sim \triangle DCB$, so $\dfrac{AC}{CD} = \dfrac{CD}{CB}$ and $CD^2 = 6 \cdot 4 = 24$.

    An angle in a semicircle is right; the altitude to the hypotenuse makes two similar right triangles.

  3. The circle with diameter $CD$ has area $\pi \cdot \dfrac{CD^2}{4} = \pi \cdot \dfrac{24}{4} = 6\pi$. ✓

    Radius squared is $CD^2/4$.

$6\pi$, the same as the circle on $CD$.

Expanding $(a + b)^2 - a^2 - b^2 = 2ab$ is what makes the two areas match.
