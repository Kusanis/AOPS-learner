Chapter 18 · Geometry

Three Dimensional Geometry

Step off the page and into space. Here you'll meet planes, spheres, boxes, prisms, cylinders, pyramids, cones and polyhedra, learn how to measure how much wrapping they need and how much they hold, and pick up the single most useful habit in solid geometry: turning a 3D problem into a few flat ones.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • Three noncollinear points determine a plane. Two lines in space are parallel, intersecting, or skew (never meet, not parallel).
  • Sphere of radius $r$: surface area $4\pi r^2$, volume $\tfrac43\pi r^3$. A plane at distance $d$ from the center cuts a circle of radius $\sqrt{r^2-d^2}$.
  • Box $l \times w \times h$: surface area $2(lw+wh+hl)$, volume $lwh$, diagonal $\sqrt{l^2+w^2+h^2}$. A cube of edge $s$ has diagonal $s\sqrt3$.
  • Prisms and cylinders: volume $=$ (base area)(height). Lateral area of a right prism $=$ (base perimeter)(height); of a cylinder, $2\pi r h$.
  • Pyramids and cones: volume $=\tfrac13$(base area)(height). Lateral area of a regular pyramid $=\tfrac12 p\ell$; of a cone, $\pi r \ell$, where $\ell$ is the slant height.
  • Euler's formula for any polyhedron: $V - E + F = 2$. Count edges by adding up the sides of all faces and halving.
  • To find a length, area or angle in space, find a flat triangle (very often a right triangle) that contains it. To find a surface path, unfold the surface.

18.1 Planes, Surface Area, and Volume

Hook

Why does a three-legged stool never wobble, while a four-legged chair often does? Any three points (the feet) always sit on one flat plane: the floor can always meet all three. A fourth foot has to be lucky enough to lie in that same plane.

Concept

A plane is a flat surface that goes on forever in every direction, the two-dimensional cousin of a line. Three points that are not on one line determine exactly one plane. Four or more points may or may not lie in a common plane; if they do, they are coplanar.

In a plane, two different lines either meet or are parallel. In space there is a third option: two lines that point in different directions but never meet are skew. Think of a road on the ground and a power line overhead crossing it at an angle.

A line and a plane can relate in three ways: the line lies in the plane, it crosses the plane at exactly one point, or it is parallel to the plane and never meets it. A line is perpendicular to a plane when it is perpendicular to every line in the plane through its foot. The distance from a point to a plane is the length of the perpendicular segment from the point to the plane.

Total surface area: the area of the "skin" of a solid. Lateral surface area: the skin minus the top and bottom. Volume: the amount of space inside, measured in unit cubes.

If a problem just says "surface area," it means the total surface area.

Planes through four points

Four points in space do not all lie in one plane, and no three of them are on a line. How many different planes pass through three of the four points?

  1. Each choice of three points gives a triangle, which lies in exactly one plane.

    Three noncollinear points determine a plane.

  2. The number of ways to choose 3 of the 4 points is $\binom{4}{3} = 4$.

    Equivalently, choose which one point to leave out.

  3. No two of these planes coincide, since a plane containing all four points would make them coplanar.

    We must check we aren't counting the same plane twice.

$4$ planes (they are the four faces of a triangular pyramid).

Parallel, intersecting or skew?

A box has bottom face $ABCD$ and top face $EFGH$, with $E$ above $A$, $F$ above $B$, $G$ above $C$ and $H$ above $D$. Classify the line $AB$ against each of the lines $HG$, $BF$ and $CG$.

  1. $HG$ is directly above $DC$, and $DC \parallel AB$. So $AB \parallel HG$.

    Lines parallel to the same line are parallel; also $A, B, G, H$ lie in one slanted plane.

  2. $AB$ and $BF$ share the point $B$, so they intersect.

    Two edges from the same corner always meet there.

  3. $AB$ runs along the bottom; $CG$ goes straight up from $C$, which is not on $AB$. They are not parallel and never meet, so they are skew.

    Skew lines are the uniquely three-dimensional case.

$AB \parallel HG$; $AB$ meets $BF$; $AB$ and $CG$ are skew.

Lateral versus total surface area

A box is $3$ by $4$ at the base and $5$ tall. Find its lateral surface area and its total surface area.

  1. The four side walls are two $3 \times 5$ rectangles and two $4 \times 5$ rectangles: $2(15) + 2(20) = 70$.

    "Lateral" means everything except the top and the bottom.

  2. Top and bottom are each $3 \times 4 = 12$, adding $24$.

    The total surface area includes them.

  3. Total: $70 + 24 = 94$.

    Add the pieces of the skin.

Lateral area $70$, total surface area $94$.

A staircase of cubes

Unit cubes are glued into a staircase one cube deep: columns of heights $1, 2, 3, 4$ stand side by side, left to right. Find the volume and the surface area of the solid.

  1. Volume $= 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10$ cubic units.

    Volume counts unit cubes.

  2. Front and back: each is the staircase shape of area $10$, so $20$ in total.

    Each cube shows one unit square on the front and one on the back.

  3. From above you see $4$ unit squares of roof (at various heights), and from below $4$. From the left you see a $1 \times 4$ strip of risers ($1 + 1 + 1 + 1$), and from the right the $4$-tall wall.

    For a staircase, every outward square facing a direction is seen exactly once from that direction.

  4. Surface area $= 20 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 36$.

    Add the six directions.

Volume $10$, surface area $36$.

For solids without hidden pockets, count the visible squares from each of the six directions.

Guy wires on a flagpole

A $12$ m flagpole stands perpendicular to flat ground. Wires $13$ m long run from its top to stakes in the ground. How far from the base of the pole is each stake, and where can the stakes go?

  1. The pole is perpendicular to the ground, so it is perpendicular to the line from its base to any stake.

    That is what "perpendicular to a plane" means.

  2. Each wire is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with leg $12$: the other leg is $\sqrt{13^2 - 12^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.

    A flat right triangle lives inside the 3D picture.

  3. Every stake is $5$ m from the base, so the stakes lie on a circle of radius $5$ centered at the base.

    All points of a plane at one fixed distance from a point form a circle.

$5$ m from the base, anywhere on the circle of radius $5$ around it.

Skew edges of a cube

How many unordered pairs of edges of a cube are skew?

  1. Fix one edge. Of the other $11$ edges, $3$ are parallel to it (the cube has $4$ edges in each direction).

    Sort the other edges into the three possible relationships.

  2. $4$ edges meet it: $2$ at each of its endpoints.

    Each vertex of a cube has $3$ edges, so $2$ others besides ours.

  3. The remaining $11 - 3 - 4 = 4$ edges are skew to it.

    Everything that is neither parallel nor intersecting is skew.

  4. $12 \times 4 = 48$ counts each pair twice, so there are $48 \div 2 = 24$ pairs.

    The pair {edge 1, edge 2} is counted once from each edge.

$24$ pairs

Count from one object's point of view, then divide out the double counting.

18.2 Spheres

Hook

Slice an orange anywhere with a straight knife and the cut face is always a perfect circle, but the size depends on where you cut. Cut through the middle and you get the biggest circle possible; shave near the edge and you get a tiny one. A single right triangle predicts exactly how big.

Concept

A sphere is the set of all points in space at a fixed distance (the radius $r$) from a fixed point (the center). It is the 3D version of a circle.

$$\text{Surface area} = 4\pi r^2 \qquad\qquad \text{Volume} = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$$

A plane that meets a sphere either touches it at one point (it is tangent) or cuts it in a circle. If the plane is $d$ units from the center $O$, drop the perpendicular $OB$ to the plane and pick any point $A$ on the circle. Then $\triangle OBA$ has a right angle at $B$, $OA = r$ and $OB = d$, so the circle's radius is

$$AB = \sqrt{r^2 - d^2}.$$

The largest cross-sections, through the center ($d = 0$), are great circles, with the same radius as the sphere.

Area grows like $r^2$ and volume like $r^3$. Doubling the radius makes the surface $4$ times as big and the volume $8$ times as big.

Area and volume from the radius

Find the surface area and volume of a sphere of radius $3$.

  1. Surface area $= 4\pi(3^2) = 36\pi$.

    Apply $4\pi r^2$.

  2. Volume $= \tfrac43\pi(3^3) = \tfrac43 \cdot 27\pi = 36\pi$.

    Apply $\tfrac43\pi r^3$.

  3. The two numbers match because $4\pi r^2 = \tfrac43\pi r^3$ exactly when $r = 3$.

    A curiosity, not a rule: the units (square vs. cubic) are different.

Surface area $36\pi$, volume $36\pi$.

Diameter from the volume

A sphere has volume $972\pi$. Find its diameter.

  1. $\tfrac43\pi r^3 = 972\pi \Rightarrow r^3 = 972 \cdot \tfrac34 = 729$.

    Divide by $\pi$, then multiply by $\tfrac34$.

  2. $r = \sqrt[3]{729} = 9$, so the diameter is $18$.

    $9^3 = 729$, and the diameter is twice the radius.

$18$

Volume from the surface area

A sphere has surface area $196\pi$. Find its volume.

  1. $4\pi r^2 = 196\pi \Rightarrow r^2 = 49 \Rightarrow r = 7$.

    Go through the radius: it connects both formulas.

  2. $V = \tfrac43\pi(343) = \dfrac{1372\pi}{3}$.

    $7^3 = 343$.

$\dfrac{1372\pi}{3}$

A slice of a sphere

A plane cuts a sphere of radius $10$ at a distance $6$ from its center. Find the area of the circular cross-section.

  1. The center $O$, the foot $B$ of the perpendicular, and a point $A$ on the circle form a right triangle with $OA = 10$, $OB = 6$.

    This is the triangle in the explorer.

  2. $AB = \sqrt{10^2 - 6^2} = \sqrt{64} = 8$.

    Pythagorean Theorem.

  3. Area $= \pi \cdot 8^2 = 64\pi$.

    $AB$ is the radius of the cross-section.

$64\pi$

Scaling a balloon

A spherical balloon is inflated until its surface area is $9$ times what it was. By what factor did its volume grow?

  1. Surface area is proportional to $r^2$, so $r^2$ grew by $9$ and $r$ grew by $3$.

    $4\pi(kr)^2 = k^2 \cdot 4\pi r^2$.

  2. Volume is proportional to $r^3$, so it grew by $3^3 = 27$.

    $\tfrac43\pi(kr)^3 = k^3 \cdot \tfrac43\pi r^3$.

$27$ times

Scale lengths by $k$: areas scale by $k^2$, volumes by $k^3$.

Two parallel slices

Two parallel planes cut a sphere of radius $13$ in circles of radii $5$ and $12$. How far apart are the planes?

  1. Distance from the center to the radius-$5$ plane: $\sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = 12$.

    Same right triangle, solved for the other leg.

  2. Distance to the radius-$12$ plane: $\sqrt{13^2 - 12^2} = 5$.

    Smaller slices are farther from the center.

  3. Same side: $12 - 5 = 7$. Opposite sides: $12 + 5 = 17$.

    The problem doesn't say, so both arrangements are possible.

$7$ or $17$

A ball resting on a ring

A ball of radius $5$ rests on a thin circular ring of radius $4$ lying flat on a table. How high above the table is the top of the ball?

  1. The ring is a circle on the ball, cut by the plane of the table. So the ball's center is $\sqrt{5^2 - 4^2} = 3$ above the table.

    The plane-slice triangle again: hypotenuse $5$, leg $4$.

  2. The top of the ball is one radius above the center: $3 + 5 = 8$.

    The topmost point is directly above the center.

$8$

A box inside a sphere

All eight corners of a $2 \times 3 \times 6$ box lie on a sphere. Find the sphere's surface area.

  1. By symmetry the sphere's center is the center of the box, so opposite corners are ends of a diameter: the box's diagonal is a diameter.

    The center of the box is equally far from all eight corners.

  2. Diagonal $= \sqrt{2^2 + 3^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{49} = 7$, so $r = \tfrac72$.

    Box diagonal formula (Section 18.3).

  3. Surface area $= 4\pi\left(\tfrac72\right)^2 = 49\pi$.

    $4 \cdot \tfrac{49}{4} = 49$.

$49\pi$

A box inscribed in a sphere has its space diagonal as a diameter.

18.3 Cubes and Boxes

Hook

Will a 1-metre umbrella fit flat on the floor of a suitcase that is $80$ cm by $60$ cm? The floor's diagonal is $100$ cm, so yes, just. And if the suitcase is $30$ cm deep, the umbrella can slant from a bottom corner to the opposite top corner, an even longer stretch. Two right triangles find it.

Concept

A box (a rectangular solid or right parallelepiped) has six rectangular faces, meeting along $12$ edges at $8$ vertices. Three numbers, the length $l$, width $w$ and height $h$, describe it completely. A cube is a box with all edges equal to $s$.

$$\text{Box: } S = 2(lw + wh + hl), \quad V = lwh, \quad d = \sqrt{l^2+w^2+h^2}$$ $$\text{Cube: } S = 6s^2, \quad V = s^3, \quad d = s\sqrt3$$

The diagonal $d$ joins two opposite corners through the inside. To find it, first cross the floor along the face diagonal $\sqrt{l^2 + w^2}$, then rise $h$: the vertical edge is perpendicular to the floor, so these two make a right triangle, and $d^2 = (l^2+w^2) + h^2$.

The names $l$, $w$, $h$ are only labels: any edge can be called any of them.

The three measurements of a cube

A cube has edge $5$. Find its surface area, volume and diagonal.

  1. $S = 6 \cdot 5^2 = 150$.

    Six square faces of area $25$.

  2. $V = 5^3 = 125$.

    Base area $25$ times height $5$.

  3. $d = 5\sqrt3$.

    $\sqrt{5^2+5^2+5^2} = \sqrt{75} = 5\sqrt3$.

$S = 150$, $V = 125$, $d = 5\sqrt3$.

A whole-number diagonal

Find the surface area, volume and diagonal of a $3 \times 4 \times 12$ box.

  1. $S = 2(3\cdot4 + 4\cdot12 + 12\cdot3) = 2(12 + 48 + 36) = 192$.

    Three pairs of congruent faces.

  2. $V = 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 12 = 144$.

    $lwh$.

  3. Face diagonal of the $3 \times 4$ face: $5$. Then $d = \sqrt{5^2 + 12^2} = 13$.

    Two right triangles in a row: $3$-$4$-$5$ then $5$-$12$-$13$.

$S = 192$, $V = 144$, $d = 13$.

Working back from surface area

A cube has surface area $294$. Find its volume and diagonal.

  1. $6s^2 = 294 \Rightarrow s^2 = 49 \Rightarrow s = 7$.

    Recover the edge first; everything else follows.

  2. $V = 343$ and $d = 7\sqrt3$.

    $s^3$ and $s\sqrt3$.

$V = 343$, $d = 7\sqrt3$.

Corner to the middle of an edge

A cube has edge $2$. Corner $A$ is at the bottom front left. $M$ is the midpoint of the vertical edge at the back right (the edge above the bottom corner opposite $A$). Find $AM$.

  1. Let $C$ be the bottom corner below $M$. $AC$ is a face diagonal of the bottom: $AC = 2\sqrt2$.

    Drop to the floor to find a right triangle.

  2. $CM = 1$ (half an edge), and $CM \perp AC$ since the edge is vertical.

    A vertical edge is perpendicular to every line in the floor through its foot.

  3. $AM = \sqrt{(2\sqrt2)^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{9} = 3$.

    Pythagorean Theorem in right triangle $ACM$.

$3$

The painted cube

A $5 \times 5 \times 5$ cube made of unit cubes is painted on the outside. How many unit cubes have $3$, $2$, $1$ and $0$ painted faces?

  1. Three painted faces: the $8$ corners.

    Only a corner touches three faces of the big cube.

  2. Two: along each of the $12$ edges, the $5 - 2 = 3$ non-corner cubes: $36$.

    Remove the two corners from each edge.

  3. One: on each of the $6$ faces, the inner $3 \times 3$ square: $54$.

    Strip the border of each $5 \times 5$ face.

  4. Zero: peel off the outer layer, leaving a $3 \times 3 \times 3$ core: $27$. Check: $8 + 36 + 54 + 27 = 125 = 5^3$. ✓

    The check confirms nothing was missed or double-counted.

$8$, $36$, $54$, $27$

For an $n$-cube: $8$, $12(n-2)$, $6(n-2)^2$, $(n-2)^3$.

Volume from three face areas

Three faces of a box that meet at a corner have areas $12$, $15$ and $20$. Find the volume.

  1. With edges $x, y, z$: $xy = 12$, $xz = 15$, $yz = 20$.

    Each face is a product of two edges.

  2. Multiplying: $x^2y^2z^2 = 12 \cdot 15 \cdot 20 = 3600$.

    Each edge appears in exactly two faces.

  3. $xyz = \sqrt{3600} = 60$.

    Lengths are positive. (The edges are $3, 4, 5$, but we never needed them.)

$60$

Go straight for the quantity you want; here the product of the equations gives $(xyz)^2$.

The missing dimension

A box has diagonal $11$ and two of its edges are $2$ and $6$. Find its volume.

  1. $2^2 + 6^2 + h^2 = 11^2 \Rightarrow h^2 = 121 - 40 = 81 \Rightarrow h = 9$.

    The diagonal formula involves all three edges.

  2. $V = 2 \cdot 6 \cdot 9 = 108$.

    Reject $h = -9$: lengths are positive.

$108$

Slicing off a corner

A cube has edge $4$. A plane passes through the three vertices adjacent to corner $P$, slicing off a small pyramid. Find the area of the triangular cut and the distance from $P$ to the plane.

  1. Each side of the cut is a face diagonal, $4\sqrt2$, so the triangle is equilateral: area $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}(4\sqrt2)^2 = 8\sqrt3$.

    Three equal sides make an equilateral triangle.

  2. Viewed with a face as base, the pyramid has base a right triangle with legs $4, 4$ (area $8$) and height $4$: volume $\tfrac13 \cdot 8 \cdot 4 = \tfrac{32}{3}$.

    The three edges at $P$ are mutually perpendicular, so one can serve as the height.

  3. Now use the cut as the base: $\tfrac13 \cdot 8\sqrt3 \cdot t = \tfrac{32}{3}$, so $t = \dfrac{4}{\sqrt3} = \dfrac{4\sqrt3}{3}$.

    Computing one volume two ways reveals the unknown height.

Area $8\sqrt3$; distance $\dfrac{4\sqrt3}{3}$.

The distance is exactly one third of the cube's diagonal $4\sqrt3$.

18.4 Prisms and Cylinders

Hook

Push a neat stack of coins so it leans like a tower in Pisa. Its height hasn't changed, and every coin is still there, so its volume hasn't changed either. That simple idea, "same slices, same volume," is why one formula covers every prism and cylinder.

Concept

A prism has two congruent, parallel bases (any polygon), joined by lateral faces that are parallelograms. In a right prism the lateral edges are perpendicular to the bases and the lateral faces are rectangles; a regular prism has regular-polygon bases. The height is the perpendicular distance between the bases.

A cylinder is the same idea with circular bases. Unless told otherwise, "cylinder" means a right circular cylinder, like a can.

$$\text{Any prism or cylinder: } V = (\text{base area}) \times (\text{height})$$ $$\text{Right prism: lateral area} = (\text{base perimeter}) \times h$$ $$\text{Cylinder: } V = \pi r^2 h, \qquad S = 2\pi r h + 2\pi r^2$$

Why is the curved side of a cylinder $2\pi r h$? Slit it straight down and unroll it: it becomes a rectangle, $h$ tall and as wide as the circumference $2\pi r$.

A triangular prism

A right prism has a base that is a $3$-$4$-$5$ right triangle and height $10$. Find its volume and total surface area.

  1. Base area $= \tfrac12 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 6$, so $V = 6 \cdot 10 = 60$.

    The legs of a right triangle are a base and its height.

  2. Lateral area $= (3 + 4 + 5) \cdot 10 = 120$.

    Perimeter times height for a right prism.

  3. Total $= 120 + 2 \cdot 6 = 132$.

    Add both triangular bases.

$V = 60$, $S = 132$.

A soup can

A cylinder has radius $3$ and height $7$. Find its volume, lateral area and total surface area.

  1. $V = \pi \cdot 9 \cdot 7 = 63\pi$.

    $\pi r^2 h$.

  2. Lateral area $= 2\pi \cdot 3 \cdot 7 = 42\pi$.

    The unrolled rectangle is $2\pi r$ by $h$.

  3. Total $= 42\pi + 2 \cdot 9\pi = 60\pi$.

    Two circular ends of area $9\pi$.

$V = 63\pi$, lateral $42\pi$, total $60\pi$.

A hexagonal prism

A regular hexagonal prism has base edge $2$ and height $5$. Find its volume and lateral area.

  1. A regular hexagon splits into $6$ equilateral triangles: base area $= 6 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} \cdot 2^2 = 6\sqrt3$.

    Join the center to all six vertices.

  2. $V = 6\sqrt3 \cdot 5 = 30\sqrt3$.

    Base area times height.

  3. Lateral area $= (6 \cdot 2) \cdot 5 = 60$.

    Six $2 \times 5$ rectangles.

$V = 30\sqrt3$, lateral area $60$.

Radius from volume

A cylinder of height $4$ has volume $100\pi$. Find its total surface area.

  1. $\pi r^2 \cdot 4 = 100\pi \Rightarrow r^2 = 25 \Rightarrow r = 5$.

    Find the radius first.

  2. $S = 2\pi(5)(4) + 2\pi(25) = 40\pi + 50\pi = 90\pi$.

    Side plus two ends.

$90\pi$

A leaning prism

An oblique prism has base area $12$. Its lateral edges are $10$ long and make a $60^\circ$ angle with the base. Find its volume.

  1. The height is the perpendicular distance between the bases. A lateral edge, the height and its shadow on the base form a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle, so $h = 10 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = 5\sqrt3$.

    The leg opposite the $60^\circ$ angle is $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ of the hypotenuse.

  2. $V = 12 \cdot 5\sqrt3 = 60\sqrt3$.

    Base times perpendicular height, even when the prism leans.

$60\sqrt3$

Drop a ball in a glass

A cylindrical glass of radius $4$ holds water. A ball of radius $3$ is dropped in and sinks completely under the water. How much does the water level rise?

  1. The ball displaces its own volume: $\tfrac43\pi \cdot 27 = 36\pi$.

    The water has to go somewhere: up.

  2. A rise of $x$ adds a cylinder of volume $16\pi x$, so $16\pi x = 36\pi$ and $x = \tfrac94$.

    The extra water forms a thin cylinder with the glass's radius.

$\tfrac94 = 2.25$ units

A ribbon around a can

A can has radius $3$ and height $8\pi$. A ribbon is glued on, starting at a point on the bottom rim and winding around exactly once to the point on the top rim directly above the start. What is the shortest possible length of ribbon?

  1. Slit the can along the vertical line through the start and unroll: a rectangle $2\pi \cdot 3 = 6\pi$ wide and $8\pi$ tall.

    Unrolling turns a curved surface path into a flat one, without stretching.

  2. Winding once from bottom to top becomes the rectangle's diagonal.

    The start is one bottom corner, the finish the opposite top corner.

  3. Length $= \sqrt{(6\pi)^2 + (8\pi)^2} = \pi\sqrt{36 + 64} = 10\pi$.

    A $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle scaled by $2\pi$.

$10\pi$

Shortest paths on a surface become straight lines on its unrolled net.

Longest diagonal of a hexagonal prism

A regular hexagonal prism has base edge $2$ and height $3$. Find the longest segment joining two of its vertices.

  1. The longest diagonal of a regular hexagon of side $2$ passes through the center: it is $2 + 2 = 4$.

    It is made of two radii, and a regular hexagon's radius equals its side.

  2. Go from a bottom vertex to the top vertex above the opposite bottom vertex. With the vertical edge, this makes a right triangle with legs $4$ and $3$.

    Lateral edges are perpendicular to the base.

  3. Length $= \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5$.

    Pythagorean Theorem.

$5$

18.5 Pyramids and Cones

Hook

Fill a cone-shaped cup with water and pour it into a cylinder with the same base and the same height. You need three cupfuls to fill the cylinder. Not roughly three: exactly three, for every cone and every pyramid.

Concept

A pyramid has one polygonal base; all its other faces are triangles meeting at the apex (vertex). Its height $h$ is the perpendicular distance from the apex to the base. In a regular pyramid the base is a regular polygon and the apex sits directly above its center, so the lateral faces are congruent isosceles triangles. Their common height is the slant height $\ell$.

A cone is a pyramid with a circular base. In a right circular cone, $h$, the radius $r$ and the slant height $\ell$ form a right triangle: $h^2 + r^2 = \ell^2$.

$$V = \tfrac13(\text{base area})(h)$$ $$\text{Regular pyramid: lateral area} = \tfrac12 p\,\ell \qquad \text{Cone: lateral area} = \pi r \ell$$

Cut a cone along a slant line and unroll it: you get a sector of a circle of radius $\ell$ whose arc is the base circumference $2\pi r$. The sector is the fraction $\tfrac{2\pi r}{2\pi\ell} = \tfrac{r}{\ell}$ of the full circle, so its angle is $360^\circ \cdot \tfrac{r}{\ell}$ and its area is $\tfrac{r}{\ell} \cdot \pi\ell^2 = \pi r\ell$.

A square pyramid

A regular square pyramid has base edge $6$ and height $4$. Find its volume, slant height and total surface area.

  1. $V = \tfrac13 \cdot 36 \cdot 4 = 48$.

    One third of base area times height.

  2. From the center of the base to the midpoint of an edge is $3$. So $\ell = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5$.

    Height, apothem and slant height form a right triangle.

  3. Lateral area $= \tfrac12 \cdot 24 \cdot 5 = 60$; total $= 60 + 36 = 96$.

    $\tfrac12 p\ell$ is four triangles of area $\tfrac12 \cdot 6 \cdot 5$.

$V = 48$, $\ell = 5$, $S = 96$.

A 3-4-5 cone

A cone has radius $3$ and height $4$. Find its volume and total surface area.

  1. $V = \tfrac13\pi \cdot 9 \cdot 4 = 12\pi$.

    $\tfrac13\pi r^2 h$.

  2. $\ell = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$; lateral area $= \pi \cdot 3 \cdot 5 = 15\pi$.

    The lateral area needs the slant height, not the height.

  3. Total $= 15\pi + 9\pi = 24\pi$.

    Add the circular base.

$V = 12\pi$, $S = 24\pi$.

From volume to lateral area

A cone of height $8$ has volume $96\pi$. Find its lateral area.

  1. $\tfrac13\pi r^2 \cdot 8 = 96\pi \Rightarrow r^2 = 36 \Rightarrow r = 6$.

    Solve for the radius.

  2. $\ell = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10$; lateral area $= \pi \cdot 6 \cdot 10 = 60\pi$.

    A $6$-$8$-$10$ triangle.

$60\pi$

The unrolled sector

A cone has radius $4$ and slant height $12$. Its side is cut along a slant line and laid flat. Find the angle and area of the resulting sector.

  1. The sector has radius $12$ and arc length $2\pi \cdot 4 = 8\pi$.

    The arc was the base circle.

  2. The full circle of radius $12$ has circumference $24\pi$, so the sector is $\tfrac{8\pi}{24\pi} = \tfrac13$ of it: angle $120^\circ$.

    Angle is proportional to arc length.

  3. Area $= \tfrac13 \cdot \pi \cdot 144 = 48\pi$, matching $\pi r\ell = \pi \cdot 4 \cdot 12$.

    Two routes to the same answer: a good check.

$120^\circ$, area $48\pi$.

Rolling a sector into a cone

A $240^\circ$ sector of a circle of radius $9$ is rolled up into a cone. Find the cone's volume.

  1. Arc length $= \tfrac{240}{360} \cdot 2\pi \cdot 9 = 12\pi$. This becomes the base circumference: $2\pi r = 12\pi$, so $r = 6$.

    Rolling turns the arc into the base circle.

  2. The sector's radius becomes the slant height: $\ell = 9$, so $h = \sqrt{81 - 36} = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt5$.

    Every point of the arc is $9$ from the apex.

  3. $V = \tfrac13\pi \cdot 36 \cdot 3\sqrt5 = 36\sqrt5\,\pi$.

    $\tfrac13 \cdot 36 \cdot 3 = 36$.

$36\sqrt5\,\pi$

A pyramid known by its edges

A regular square pyramid has base edge $10$ and lateral edges $13$. Find its lateral area and volume.

  1. Each lateral face is isosceles with sides $13, 13, 10$. Its height (the slant height) is $\sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = 12$.

    The altitude of an isosceles triangle bisects the base.

  2. Lateral area $= 4 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 10 \cdot 12 = 240$.

    Four congruent triangles.

  3. Height: $h = \sqrt{\ell^2 - 5^2} = \sqrt{144 - 25} = \sqrt{119}$.

    Apex, base center and edge midpoint form a right triangle with legs $h$ and $5$.

  4. $V = \tfrac13 \cdot 100 \cdot \sqrt{119} = \dfrac{100\sqrt{119}}{3}$.

    Base area $100$.

Lateral area $240$, volume $\dfrac{100\sqrt{119}}{3}$.

Slicing a cone

A cone has radius $8$ and height $12$. A plane parallel to the base, $6$ above it, cuts off a small cone at the top. Find the volume of the remaining piece (a frustum).

  1. The small cone has height $6$, half the original, so it is a half-size copy: radius $4$.

    The cross-section of a cone through its axis shows similar triangles.

  2. Big cone: $\tfrac13\pi \cdot 64 \cdot 12 = 256\pi$. Small cone: $\tfrac13\pi \cdot 16 \cdot 6 = 32\pi$.

    Volume scales by $\left(\tfrac12\right)^3 = \tfrac18$: indeed $256\pi \div 8 = 32\pi$.

  3. Frustum: $256\pi - 32\pi = 224\pi$.

    Subtract the part removed.

$224\pi$

Next in the book: Exercise 18-10.

Half a cup of water

A cone-shaped paper cup (point down) is $12$ cm tall. How deep is the water when the cup holds exactly half its capacity?

  1. The water forms a smaller cone similar to the cup. If its depth is $k$ times the cup's height, its volume is $k^3$ times the cup's.

    All lengths of the water cone scale by $k$. (Half the depth holds only $\tfrac18$ of the cup.)

  2. $k^3 = \tfrac12 \Rightarrow k = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{2}}$.

    Set the volume ratio to one half.

  3. Depth $= \dfrac{12}{\sqrt[3]2} = 6\sqrt[3]{4} \approx 9.52$ cm.

    Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt[3]{4}$: $\sqrt[3]2 \cdot \sqrt[3]4 = 2$.

$6\sqrt[3]{4} \approx 9.52$ cm

Similar solids: volume ratio $=$ (length ratio)$^3$. A half-full cone is filled to about $79\%$ of its height.

18.6 Polyhedra

Hook

Take any solid with flat faces, a die, a pyramid, a cut gemstone, a soccer ball, and count its vertices, subtract its edges and add its faces. You'll get $2$ every single time. Shave off a corner and the counts change, but the answer stays $2$.

Concept

A polyhedron is a solid bounded by flat polygonal faces. Boxes, prisms and pyramids are polyhedra; cylinders, cones and spheres are not. A regular polyhedron has congruent regular polygons as faces with the same number meeting at each vertex. There are only five:

Euler's formula (for every convex polyhedron): $$V - E + F = 2$$

Two counting tricks go with it. Each edge borders exactly two faces, so $E$ is half the total number of sides of all the faces. Each edge has two endpoints, so $E$ is also half the sum, over all vertices, of the number of edges meeting there.

Volumes of polyhedra come from splitting them into pyramids and prisms. A regular octahedron, for example, is two square pyramids glued base to base.

Checking Euler on a prism

Count the vertices, edges and faces of a pentagonal prism and verify Euler's formula.

  1. $V = 5 + 5 = 10$ (two pentagons).

    All vertices lie on the two bases.

  2. $E = 5 + 5 + 5 = 15$ (top, bottom, lateral).

    Each base has $5$ edges, plus $5$ joining them.

  3. $F = 2 + 5 = 7$, and $10 - 15 + 7 = 2$. ✓

    Two bases plus five rectangles.

$V = 10$, $E = 15$, $F = 7$; $V - E + F = 2$.

A prism from its edge count

A prism has $18$ edges. How many faces and vertices does it have?

  1. An $n$-gon prism has $3n$ edges, so $3n = 18$ and $n = 6$.

    Generalize the previous example: $n + n + n$.

  2. $F = n + 2 = 8$ and $V = 2n = 12$. Check: $12 - 18 + 8 = 2$. ✓

    Euler's formula is a free check.

$8$ faces, $12$ vertices.

Counting edges from faces

A polyhedron has $6$ square faces and $8$ triangular faces. How many edges and vertices does it have?

  1. Total sides of all faces: $6 \cdot 4 + 8 \cdot 3 = 48$. Each edge is shared by two faces: $E = 24$.

    Every edge gets counted once from each side.

  2. $V = 2 - F + E = 2 - 14 + 24 = 12$.

    Euler's formula gives the last count.

$24$ edges, $12$ vertices (a cuboctahedron: cut a cube's corners halfway in the explorer).

Volume of a regular tetrahedron

Find the volume of a regular tetrahedron with edge $6$.

  1. Base: equilateral triangle, area $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} \cdot 36 = 9\sqrt3$.

    It is a pyramid on a triangular base.

  2. The apex is above the base's center, which is $\tfrac23$ of the way along a median of length $3\sqrt3$: distance $2\sqrt3$ from each base vertex.

    All lateral edges are equal, so the foot of the height is equidistant from the base vertices.

  3. Height: $h = \sqrt{6^2 - (2\sqrt3)^2} = \sqrt{24} = 2\sqrt6$.

    Right triangle: lateral edge, height, and center-to-vertex segment.

  4. $V = \tfrac13 \cdot 9\sqrt3 \cdot 2\sqrt6 = 6\sqrt{18} = 18\sqrt2$.

    $\sqrt3 \cdot \sqrt6 = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt2$.

$18\sqrt2$

Volume of a regular octahedron

Find the volume of a regular octahedron with edge $4$.

  1. Split it into two square pyramids with base a $4 \times 4$ square (area $16$).

    The four "equator" vertices form a square.

  2. The two apexes and two opposite equator vertices also form a square of side $4$. The pyramid's height is half its diagonal: $2\sqrt2$.

    By symmetry, any four vertices in a "belt" of the octahedron form a square.

  3. $V = 2 \cdot \tfrac13 \cdot 16 \cdot 2\sqrt2 = \dfrac{64\sqrt2}{3}$.

    Two congruent pyramids.

$\dfrac{64\sqrt2}{3}$

The truncated cube

Slicing every corner off a cube leaves a solid with $8$ triangles and $6$ octagons. How many vertices and edges does it have?

  1. $E = \tfrac12(8 \cdot 3 + 6 \cdot 8) = \tfrac12(72) = 36$.

    Half the total number of polygon sides.

  2. $V = 2 - F + E = 2 - 14 + 36 = 24$.

    Euler's formula.

  3. Check: each of the $8$ cube corners became a triangle with $3$ new vertices: $24$. ✓

    An independent count confirms the answer.

$24$ vertices, $36$ edges.

A tetrahedron inside a cube

Choose four corners of a cube of edge $2$, no two of them joined by an edge. They form a regular tetrahedron. Find its volume.

  1. Each edge of the tetrahedron is a face diagonal, $2\sqrt2$, so it is regular.

    The four chosen corners are pairwise diagonally across a face.

  2. The cube minus the tetrahedron is four corner pyramids, each with three perpendicular edges of length $2$: volume $\tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = \tfrac43$.

    Subtracting from a larger, easy solid is often simpler than direct computation.

  3. $V = 8 - 4 \cdot \tfrac43 = \tfrac83$.

    Exactly one third of the cube.

$\tfrac83$

Check with the tetrahedron formula: $V = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{12}a^3 = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{12}(2\sqrt2)^3 = \tfrac83$.

Five triangles at every vertex

A polyhedron has only triangular faces, and exactly $5$ faces meet at every vertex. Find $F$, $E$ and $V$.

  1. Counting sides of faces: $3F = 2E$, so $E = \tfrac32 F$.

    Each triangle has $3$ sides; each edge is shared by $2$ faces.

  2. Counting at vertices: $5V = 2E$ (each vertex has $5$ edges, each edge $2$ ends), so $V = \tfrac25 E = \tfrac35 F$.

    Five faces at a vertex means five edges there.

  3. Euler: $\tfrac35 F - \tfrac32 F + F = 2 \Rightarrow \tfrac{6 - 15 + 10}{10}F = 2 \Rightarrow \tfrac{F}{10} = 2$, so $F = 20$.

    Substitute both expressions into $V - E + F = 2$.

  4. $E = 30$, $V = 12$.

    Back-substitute.

$F = 20$, $E = 30$, $V = 12$: the icosahedron.

Two double-counts plus Euler's formula can determine a polyhedron's counts completely.

18.7 How to Solve 3D Problems

Hook

Slice a cube with a flat knife. You might expect a square, and you can get one, but you can also get a triangle, a rectangle, a pentagon, or even a perfect regular hexagon. Every 3D question hides flat pictures like these; the art is choosing the right slice.

Concept

Almost every 3D problem is a 2D problem in disguise. A few strategies cover most of them:

  • Find the plane. To compute a length, angle or area, find a flat figure (often a right triangle) containing it. Perpendicular edges of boxes and heights of pyramids make right angles everywhere.
  • Slice. A well-chosen cross-section through the important points turns the problem into plane geometry.
  • Dissect or subtract. Break a complicated solid into prisms and pyramids, or take a simple big solid and subtract what's missing.
  • Unfold. For shortest paths along a surface, flatten the surface into a net; the path becomes a straight segment.
  • Count volume two ways. The same pyramid can stand on different faces; equating the volumes finds unknown heights and distances.

Two right triangles make a diagonal

A $2 \times 3 \times 6$ box: find the length of its diagonal by naming the flat triangles you use.

  1. In the $2 \times 3$ floor, the diagonal is $\sqrt{4 + 9} = \sqrt{13}$.

    First triangle: two floor edges and the floor diagonal.

  2. The vertical edge $6$ is perpendicular to the floor, so the diagonal is $\sqrt{13 + 36} = 7$.

    Second triangle: floor diagonal, vertical edge, space diagonal.

$7$

Midpoints of skew edges

Put a cube of edge $4$ in coordinates with corners at $(0,0,0)$ and $(4,4,4)$. $M$ is the midpoint of the edge from $(0,0,0)$ to $(4,0,0)$, and $N$ is the midpoint of the skew edge from $(0,0,4)$ to $(0,4,4)$. Find $MN$.

  1. $M = (2, 0, 0)$ and $N = (0, 2, 4)$.

    Average the endpoints.

  2. Let $P = (0, 2, 0)$, directly below $N$. Then $MP = \sqrt{2^2 + 2^2} = 2\sqrt2$ and $PN = 4$, with $PN \perp MP$.

    Drop to the floor: the 3D distance formula is just two Pythagorean steps.

  3. $MN = \sqrt{8 + 16} = \sqrt{24} = 2\sqrt6$.

    In general, distance $= \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2}$.

$2\sqrt6$

A diagonal slice of a cube

A plane cuts a cube of edge $3$ through two opposite parallel edges (for example, the front-bottom edge and the back-top edge). Describe the cross-section and find its area.

  1. The plane contains both edges (length $3$) and crosses the left and right faces along their diagonals (length $3\sqrt2$).

    Connect the ends of the two edges.

  2. The edges are perpendicular to the left and right faces, so the cross-section is a rectangle $3$ by $3\sqrt2$.

    An edge is perpendicular to every line in a face it meets at right angles.

  3. Area $= 9\sqrt2$.

    Length times width.

A $3 \times 3\sqrt2$ rectangle, area $9\sqrt2$.

A tunnel through a cube

A $2 \times 2$ square hole is drilled straight through the center of a $6 \times 6 \times 6$ cube, from the front face to the back face. Find the volume and the total surface area (inside and out) of what remains.

  1. Volume $= 216 - (2 \cdot 2 \cdot 6) = 216 - 24 = 192$.

    Subtract the removed prism from the whole cube.

  2. Outside: $6 \cdot 36 = 216$, minus the two $2 \times 2$ openings: $216 - 8 = 208$.

    The front and back faces lose a square each.

  3. Inside: four tunnel walls, each $2 \times 6$: $48$. Total $= 208 + 48 = 256$.

    Drilling removes some surface and creates more.

Volume $192$, surface area $256$.

Corner to the main diagonal

A cube has edge $3$. $AG$ is a main diagonal and $B$ is a corner adjacent to $A$. Find the distance from $B$ to the line $AG$.

  1. In $\triangle ABG$: $AB = 3$ (edge), $BG = 3\sqrt2$ (face diagonal), $AG = 3\sqrt3$ (main diagonal).

    Find a plane containing both the point and the line.

  2. $AB \perp BG$ because edge $AB$ is perpendicular to the face containing $BG$. So the area is $\tfrac12 \cdot 3 \cdot 3\sqrt2$.

    Check: $9 + 18 = 27 = (3\sqrt3)^2$.

  3. Using $AG$ as the base: $\tfrac12 \cdot 3\sqrt3 \cdot t = \tfrac12 \cdot 3 \cdot 3\sqrt2$, so $t = \dfrac{3\sqrt2}{\sqrt3} = \sqrt6$.

    Area two ways, the 2D cousin of volume two ways.

$\sqrt6$

A spider on a box

A box is $1 \times 1 \times 2$ ($2$ tall). A spider crawls on the surface from a bottom corner to the opposite top corner. What is the shortest path?

  1. The spider must cross two faces. Unfold those two faces flat; the shortest path is the straight segment in the unfolded rectangle.

    Unfolding preserves distances along the surface.

  2. Unfold two side faces ($1 \times 2$ each) side by side: a $2 \times 2$ rectangle. Path $= \sqrt{2^2 + 2^2} = 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83$.

    The corners become opposite corners of the rectangle.

  3. Unfold the bottom with a side instead: a $1 \times 3$ rectangle. Path $= \sqrt{1^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$.

    Different unfoldings give different paths; compare all of them.

$2\sqrt2$

For an $a \times b \times c$ box with $c$ largest, the shortest corner-to-corner surface path is $\sqrt{(a+b)^2 + c^2}$.

The ball inside an octahedron

Find the radius of the largest sphere that fits inside a regular octahedron of edge $6$.

  1. The sphere touches all $8$ faces, and its center $O$ is the octahedron's center. Joining $O$ to the vertices cuts the octahedron into $8$ pyramids, each with a face as base and height $r$.

    The radius to a point of tangency is perpendicular to the face.

  2. So $V = \tfrac13 r \cdot (\text{total surface area})$.

    Add up the eight pyramid volumes.

  3. $V = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{3} \cdot 6^3 = 72\sqrt2$ and surface area $= 8 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{4} \cdot 36 = 72\sqrt3$.

    Octahedron volume from Section 18.6, scaled to edge $6$.

  4. $72\sqrt2 = \tfrac13 r \cdot 72\sqrt3 \Rightarrow r = \dfrac{3\sqrt2}{\sqrt3} = \sqrt6$.

    Solve for $r$.

$\sqrt6$

For a solid with an inscribed sphere, $V = \tfrac13 r S$.
