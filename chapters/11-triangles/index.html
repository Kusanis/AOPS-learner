Chapter 11 · Geometry

Triangles, a.k.a. Geometry

Three points, three segments, and almost all of basic geometry. Triangles can be sorted, measured, matched and scaled, and every one of those ideas becomes a tool for solving problems. This is a long chapter, so take it one section at a time and drag the vertices around until each fact feels obvious.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • The angles of a triangle add to $180^\circ$. Equal sides sit opposite equal angles.
  • Medians meet at the centroid (which splits each median $2:1$), angle bisectors at the incenter, perpendicular bisectors at the circumcenter, altitudes at the orthocenter.
  • Triangle inequality: each side is less than the sum of the other two, and more than their difference.
  • Pythagoras: $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ in a right triangle. If $a^2 + b^2 < c^2$ the angle opposite $c$ is obtuse; if $a^2 + b^2 > c^2$ it is acute. Learn the triples $3$-$4$-$5$, $5$-$12$-$13$, $8$-$15$-$17$, $7$-$24$-$25$ and their multiples.
  • Congruence (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, and HL for right triangles) proves segments and angles equal. There is no SSA.
  • Similarity (usually by AA) gives equal ratios of sides; areas scale by the square of the ratio.
  • $\sin = \dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}$, $\cos = \dfrac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}$, $\tan = \dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}$. Special triangles: $45$-$45$-$90$ is $1 : 1 : \sqrt2$ and $30$-$60$-$90$ is $1 : \sqrt3 : 2$.
  • Area $= \tfrac12 a h_a = \tfrac12 ab\sin C = rs$, where $r$ is the inradius and $s$ the semiperimeter.
  • Stuck? Add a line: a parallel for similar triangles, a perpendicular for right triangles and areas.

11.1 Classifying Triangles

Hook

Could a triangle have two right angles? Try to draw one: two sides leave a base at $90^\circ$, so they are parallel and never meet. The fact that the angles add to $180^\circ$ quietly rules out a lot of shapes, and it is the first thing to reach for whenever you meet a triangle.

Concept

The corners of a triangle are its vertices. We sort triangles in two independent ways.

By angles. If all three angles are less than $90^\circ$ the triangle is acute. If one angle is exactly $90^\circ$ it is a right triangle: the side across from the right angle is the hypotenuse, the other two are legs, and the two acute angles are complementary. If one angle is more than $90^\circ$ the triangle is obtuse. Because the three angles total $180^\circ$, a triangle has at most one right or obtuse angle.

$$\angle A + \angle B + \angle C = 180^\circ$$

By sides. All three sides equal: equilateral. At least two sides equal: isosceles. No two sides equal: scalene. In an isosceles triangle the equal sides are the legs, the third side is the base, the angle between the legs is the vertex angle, and the two angles touching the base are the base angles.

Sides and angles mirror each other: equal sides are opposite equal angles, and the longest side is opposite the largest angle. So an equilateral triangle has three equal angles, each $180^\circ / 3 = 60^\circ$, and a scalene triangle has three different angles.

Find the missing angle

Two angles of a triangle measure $35^\circ$ and $55^\circ$. Find the third angle and classify the triangle by its angles.

  1. Third angle $= 180^\circ - 35^\circ - 55^\circ = 90^\circ$.

    The three angles of any triangle add to $180^\circ$.

  2. One angle is exactly $90^\circ$, so the triangle is right. The side opposite the $90^\circ$ angle is the hypotenuse.

    Classification by angles only asks whether there is a right or obtuse angle.

$90^\circ$; it is a right triangle (and its acute angles, $35^\circ$ and $55^\circ$, are complementary).

Which angles are equal?

In $\triangle PQR$, $PQ = PR = 7$ and $QR = 10$. Classify the triangle by its sides and name the two equal angles.

  1. Two sides are equal and the third is different, so $\triangle PQR$ is isosceles (not equilateral).

    Isosceles means at least two equal sides.

  2. $PQ$ is opposite $\angle R$ and $PR$ is opposite $\angle Q$, so $\angle Q = \angle R$.

    Equal sides sit opposite equal angles. The side opposite a vertex is the one that does not touch it.

Isosceles, with base angles $\angle Q = \angle R$.

Base angles from the vertex angle

The vertex angle of an isosceles triangle is $40^\circ$. Find the base angles and classify the triangle by its angles.

  1. Let each base angle be $x$. Then $40^\circ + 2x = 180^\circ$.

    The two base angles are equal, and all three angles total $180^\circ$.

  2. $2x = 140^\circ$, so $x = 70^\circ$.

    Solve the linear equation.

  3. All angles ($40^\circ, 70^\circ, 70^\circ$) are under $90^\circ$, so it is acute.

    Check each angle against $90^\circ$.

Each base angle is $70^\circ$; the triangle is acute isosceles.

An obtuse angle must be the vertex angle

One angle of an isosceles triangle measures $100^\circ$. Find the other two angles.

  1. If $100^\circ$ were a base angle, the other base angle would also be $100^\circ$, and $100^\circ + 100^\circ > 180^\circ$. Impossible.

    Base angles come in an equal pair, and a triangle has room for only one obtuse angle.

  2. So $100^\circ$ is the vertex angle and each base angle is $\dfrac{180^\circ - 100^\circ}{2} = 40^\circ$.

    The base angles split what is left of the $180^\circ$ equally.

$40^\circ$ and $40^\circ$

When the given angle could be either

One angle of an isosceles triangle measures $50^\circ$. What could the other two angles be?

  1. Case 1: $50^\circ$ is the vertex angle. Then each base angle is $\dfrac{180^\circ - 50^\circ}{2} = 65^\circ$.

    We don't know which angle we were given, so we try each role.

  2. Case 2: $50^\circ$ is a base angle. Then the other base angle is also $50^\circ$ and the vertex angle is $180^\circ - 100^\circ = 80^\circ$.

    This time $50^\circ + 50^\circ$ leaves room for a third angle, so the case is possible.

Either $65^\circ$ and $65^\circ$, or $50^\circ$ and $80^\circ$.

In isosceles problems, ask whether the given angle is the vertex angle or a base angle.

Angles in a ratio

The angles of a triangle are in the ratio $2 : 3 : 7$. Classify the triangle by its angles and by its sides.

  1. Write the angles as $2k, 3k, 7k$. Then $12k = 180^\circ$, so $k = 15^\circ$.

    A ratio fixes the angles up to a common multiplier, and the angle sum pins down that multiplier.

  2. The angles are $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$ and $105^\circ$. Since $105^\circ > 90^\circ$, the triangle is obtuse.

    Multiply out each part of the ratio.

  3. All three angles are different, so no two sides are equal: the triangle is scalene.

    If two sides were equal, the angles opposite them would be equal too.

An obtuse scalene triangle with angles $30^\circ, 45^\circ, 105^\circ$.

Isosceles, but which pair?

A triangle has angles $x^\circ$, $2x^\circ$ and $y^\circ$, and it is isosceles. Find every possibility for its three angles.

  1. Some two angles must be equal. There are three pairs to try: $x = 2x$, $x = y$, or $2x = y$.

    Isosceles means two equal angles, but we aren't told which two.

  2. $x = 2x$ forces $x = 0$, which is not a triangle. Reject.

    Every angle of a triangle is positive.

  3. $x = y$: then $x + 2x + x = 180$, so $x = 45$. The angles are $45^\circ, 90^\circ, 45^\circ$.

    Substitute into the angle sum.

  4. $2x = y$: then $x + 2x + 2x = 180$, so $x = 36$. The angles are $36^\circ, 72^\circ, 72^\circ$.

    Same method for the last pair.

Either $45^\circ, 45^\circ, 90^\circ$ (a right isosceles triangle) or $36^\circ, 72^\circ, 72^\circ$ (an acute isosceles triangle).

Casework on "which two are equal" is the standard move for isosceles problems.

11.2 Parts of a Triangle

Hook

Cut a triangle out of cardboard and try to balance it on a pencil tip. There is exactly one point where it balances. Draw three lines, each from a corner to the middle of the opposite side, and they all pass through that point. Three lines meeting at one point is rare; a triangle has several such points, and each one has its own job.

Concept

In $\triangle ABC$ we write $a = BC$, $b = CA$, $c = AB$: each side is named by the small letter of the vertex opposite it. The perimeter is $a + b + c$, and half of it, the semiperimeter, is $s = \dfrac{a+b+c}{2}$.

Four families of lines each meet at a single point (they are concurrent):

  • A median joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. The medians meet at the centroid $G$, which is two thirds of the way along each median from the vertex: $AG : GD = 2 : 1$.
  • An angle bisector splits an angle in half. Every point on it is equally far from the two sides of the angle. The three bisectors meet at the incenter $I$, equally far ($r$, the inradius) from all three sides, so it is the center of the incircle, which touches each side once.
  • A perpendicular bisector of a side passes through its midpoint at a right angle. Its points are equally far from the two endpoints. They meet at the circumcenter $O$, equally far ($R$, the circumradius) from all three vertices, so it is the center of the circumcircle through $A$, $B$, $C$.
  • An altitude drops from a vertex perpendicular to the line containing the opposite side (you may need to extend that side). The altitudes $h_a, h_b, h_c$ meet at the orthocenter $H$.

$G$ and $I$ are always inside the triangle. $O$ and $H$ are inside an acute triangle, outside an obtuse one, and on a right triangle: $O$ at the midpoint of the hypotenuse, $H$ at the right-angle vertex.

Sides, perimeter and semiperimeter

In $\triangle ABC$, $AB = 7$, $BC = 8$ and $CA = 9$. Find $a$, $b$, $c$ and the semiperimeter $s$.

  1. $a = BC = 8$, $b = CA = 9$, $c = AB = 7$.

    Each side is named after the vertex it does not touch.

  2. $s = \dfrac{8 + 9 + 7}{2} = \dfrac{24}{2} = 12$.

    The semiperimeter is half the perimeter.

$a = 8$, $b = 9$, $c = 7$, and $s = 12$.

The centroid splits a median 2 : 1

Median $AD$ of $\triangle ABC$ has length $18$, and $G$ is the centroid. Find $AG$ and $GD$.

  1. $AG : GD = 2 : 1$, so $AG$ is $\tfrac23$ of the median and $GD$ is $\tfrac13$.

    The centroid lies two thirds of the way from the vertex to the midpoint.

  2. $AG = \tfrac23 \cdot 18 = 12$ and $GD = \tfrac13 \cdot 18 = 6$.

    Check: $12 + 6 = 18$ and $12 : 6 = 2 : 1$.

$AG = 12$, $GD = 6$

The circumcircle of a right triangle

A right triangle has legs $6$ and $8$. Where is its circumcenter, and what is its circumradius?

  1. The hypotenuse is $\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10$.

    We'll need its length; Section 11.4 covers this formula.

  2. The right angle is inscribed in the circumcircle, so it cuts off an arc of $2 \cdot 90^\circ = 180^\circ$. The hypotenuse is therefore a diameter.

    An inscribed angle is half its arc (Chapter 10), and only a diameter cuts off a semicircle.

  3. The circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse and $R = \dfrac{10}{2} = 5$.

    The center of a circle is the midpoint of any diameter.

The circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse, and $R = 5$.

Locate a centroid with coordinates

Triangle $ABC$ has vertices $A(0, 0)$, $B(6, 0)$, $C(0, 6)$. Find the centroid using the $2 : 1$ property on the median from $C$.

  1. The midpoint of $AB$ is $D = (3, 0)$, so the median from $C$ runs from $(0, 6)$ to $(3, 0)$.

    A median ends at the midpoint of the opposite side.

  2. Moving from $C$ to $D$ changes the coordinates by $(3, -6)$. Two thirds of that is $(2, -4)$.

    $G$ is two thirds of the way from the vertex to the midpoint.

  3. $G = (0 + 2,\ 6 - 4) = (2, 2)$.

    Notice that $(2, 2)$ is also the average of the three vertices, $\left(\tfrac{0+6+0}{3}, \tfrac{0+0+6}{3}\right)$. That shortcut always works.

$G = (2, 2)$

The orthocenter of a right triangle

$\triangle ABC$ has a right angle at $C$. Where is its orthocenter?

  1. The altitude from $A$ must be perpendicular to $BC$. Leg $AC$ already is, so the altitude from $A$ is $AC$ itself.

    An altitude is the perpendicular from a vertex to the opposite side's line.

  2. Likewise the altitude from $B$ is leg $BC$.

    Same reasoning with the roles swapped.

  3. These two altitudes meet at $C$, so $H = C$.

    Two of the altitudes are enough to locate their common point.

At the right-angle vertex $C$.

An angle at the incenter

In $\triangle ABC$, $\angle B = 50^\circ$ and $\angle C = 70^\circ$. The angle bisectors from $B$ and $C$ meet at $I$. Find $\angle BIC$.

  1. $\angle IBC = \tfrac12 \cdot 50^\circ = 25^\circ$ and $\angle ICB = \tfrac12 \cdot 70^\circ = 35^\circ$.

    Each bisector cuts its angle in half.

  2. In $\triangle BIC$: $\angle BIC = 180^\circ - 25^\circ - 35^\circ = 120^\circ$.

    Angle sum in the small triangle.

  3. Check with the general pattern: $\angle A = 60^\circ$ and $90^\circ + \tfrac12\angle A = 120^\circ$. ✓

    In general $\angle BIC = 180^\circ - \tfrac12(\angle B + \angle C) = 90^\circ + \tfrac12 \angle A$.

$120^\circ$

An angle at the orthocenter

In acute $\triangle ABC$, $\angle A = 70^\circ$. Altitudes $BE$ and $CF$ (with $E$ on $AC$ and $F$ on $AB$) meet at the orthocenter $H$. Find $\angle BHC$.

  1. Look at quadrilateral $AFHE$. Its angles at $F$ and $E$ are $90^\circ$ (altitudes), and its angle at $A$ is $70^\circ$.

    The angles of a quadrilateral add to $360^\circ$, since a diagonal splits it into two triangles.

  2. $\angle FHE = 360^\circ - 90^\circ - 90^\circ - 70^\circ = 110^\circ$.

    Whatever is left of $360^\circ$ belongs to the fourth angle.

  3. $\angle BHC$ and $\angle FHE$ are vertical angles, so $\angle BHC = 110^\circ$.

    $B$, $H$, $E$ are collinear and so are $C$, $H$, $F$, so the two angles are formed by the same pair of crossing lines.

$110^\circ$ (in general, $\angle BHC = 180^\circ - \angle A$ for an acute triangle).

Altitudes create right angles; look for quadrilaterals with two of them.

11.3 The Triangle Inequality

Hook

Take three sticks: $2$ cm, $3$ cm and $9$ cm. Lay the long one down and swing the two short ones up from its ends. They can't reach each other. No matter how you tilt them, $2 + 3$ falls short of $9$. Whether three lengths make a triangle comes down to one comparison.

Concept

A straight segment is the shortest path between two points. So going from $A$ to $B$ by way of $C$ is longer than going straight, unless $C$ is on the segment:

$$a + b > c, \qquad b + c > a, \qquad c + a > b.$$

This is the Triangle Inequality. If one of these becomes an equality, the "triangle" is degenerate: the three points lie on one line. In practice you only need to check one inequality: the two shorter sides must add to more than the longest.

Turned around, it bounds an unknown third side. If two sides are $p$ and $q$ with $p \ge q$, the third side $x$ must satisfy

$$p - q < x < p + q.$$

Triangle or not?

Which of these sets of lengths form a nondegenerate triangle: (a) $3, 4, 8$; (b) $5, 6, 10$?

  1. (a) The two shorter sides add to $3 + 4 = 7$, which is less than $8$. No triangle.

    The longest side must be shorter than the sum of the other two.

  2. (b) $5 + 6 = 11 > 10$. A triangle exists.

    Once the longest side passes, the other two inequalities hold automatically.

(a) No; (b) yes.

A flat triangle

What happens with lengths $4$, $6$, $10$?

  1. $4 + 6 = 10$ exactly.

    This is the boundary case between "triangle" and "no triangle".

  2. The only way to place the sides is in a straight line, with the third vertex on the long side.

    The path through the third vertex is exactly as long as the straight path, so the vertex lies on the segment.

The triangle is degenerate: the three vertices are collinear.

Range for a third side

Two sides of a triangle are $5$ and $11$. What lengths are possible for the third side $x$?

  1. $5 + 11 > x$ gives $x < 16$.

    The third side can't be as long as the other two together.

  2. $x + 5 > 11$ gives $x > 6$.

    The short side plus $x$ must beat the long side.

  3. $x + 11 > 5$ holds for every positive $x$.

    We check all three inequalities, but this one adds no information.

$6 < x < 16$

Compare with the book's Example 11-2.

Count the integer options

A triangle has sides $5$ and $11$ and an integer third side. How many values are possible?

  1. From the previous example, $6 < x < 16$.

    The integer condition just narrows down the interval we already have.

  2. The integers are $7, 8, \ldots, 15$: that is $15 - 7 + 1 = 9$ values.

    Both endpoints are excluded because the inequalities are strict.

$9$

Which side is repeated?

An isosceles triangle has sides of length $4$ and $9$ (one of them appears twice). Find its perimeter.

  1. Option 1: sides $4, 4, 9$. But $4 + 4 = 8 < 9$. Impossible.

    Test each choice of which length is repeated.

  2. Option 2: sides $9, 9, 4$. Here $4 + 9 > 9$. This works.

    The two shorter sides, $4$ and $9$, beat the longest, $9$.

  3. Perimeter $= 9 + 9 + 4 = 22$.

    Only one option survived, so the answer is unique.

$22$

Triangles with perimeter 12

How many noncongruent triangles have integer sides and perimeter $12$?

  1. List sides $a \le b \le c$ with $a + b + c = 12$ and $a + b > c$. Since $a + b = 12 - c$, the condition says $12 - c > c$, so $c < 6$.

    Sorting the sides avoids counting the same triangle twice, and the only inequality to check involves the largest side.

  2. Also $c \ge 4$, since the largest of three numbers adding to $12$ is at least $12/3$. So $c = 4$ or $c = 5$.

    The largest side is at least the average.

  3. $c = 5$: $a + b = 7$ with $a \le b \le 5$ gives $(2, 5)$ and $(3, 4)$. $c = 4$: $a + b = 8$ with $b \le 4$ gives $(4, 4)$.

    Go through the cases one at a time.

$3$ triangles: $(2, 5, 5)$, $(3, 4, 5)$, $(4, 4, 4)$.

A point inside a triangle

$P$ is any point inside $\triangle ABC$. Show that $PA + PB + PC$ is greater than the semiperimeter $s$.

  1. Apply the triangle inequality to $\triangle PAB$, $\triangle PBC$ and $\triangle PCA$: $$PA + PB > c, \quad PB + PC > a, \quad PC + PA > b.$$

    Joining $P$ to the vertices cuts $\triangle ABC$ into three triangles, each with one side of the original.

  2. Add them: $2(PA + PB + PC) > a + b + c$.

    Each of $PA$, $PB$, $PC$ appears in exactly two of the inequalities.

  3. Divide by $2$: $PA + PB + PC > \dfrac{a+b+c}{2} = s$.

    That is the definition of the semiperimeter.

$PA + PB + PC > s$, by adding three triangle inequalities.

Adding several triangle inequalities, chosen so that the terms repeat evenly, proves many length inequalities.

11.4 The Pythagorean Theorem

Hook

A TV is sold as "55 inches", but that is the diagonal, not the width. If the screen is $48$ inches wide and $27$ inches tall, is the diagonal really $55$? One formula answers it: $48^2 + 27^2 = 3033$, and $\sqrt{3033} \approx 55.07$. Close enough to sell.

Concept

In a right triangle with legs $a$, $b$ and hypotenuse $c$, the squares built on the legs have the same total area as the square on the hypotenuse:

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2.$$

Given any two sides of a right triangle, this gives the third. The comparison also works when the angle isn't right: if $c$ is the longest side, then

$$a^2 + b^2 > c^2 \Rightarrow \text{acute}, \qquad a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \Rightarrow \text{right}, \qquad a^2 + b^2 < c^2 \Rightarrow \text{obtuse}.$$

Pythagorean triples are whole numbers with $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$: $(3,4,5)$, $(5,12,13)$, $(8,15,17)$, $(7,24,25)$. Any multiple of a triple is again a right triangle, since $(ka)^2 + (kb)^2 = k^2(a^2+b^2) = (kc)^2$. So whenever two sides are given, write their ratio in whole numbers and look for a familiar triple before squaring anything.

Find the hypotenuse

The legs of a right triangle are $5$ and $12$. Find the hypotenuse.

  1. $c^2 = 5^2 + 12^2 = 25 + 144 = 169$.

    The hypotenuse squared is the sum of the squares of the legs.

  2. $c = \sqrt{169} = 13$.

    Lengths are positive, so take the positive root. This is the $5$-$12$-$13$ triple.

$13$

Find a leg

A right triangle has hypotenuse $10$ and one leg $6$. Find the other leg.

  1. $6^2 + b^2 = 10^2$, so $b^2 = 100 - 36 = 64$.

    This time the unknown is a leg, so we subtract.

  2. $b = 8$.

    It is the $3$-$4$-$5$ triple doubled.

$8$

A hypotenuse that isn't a whole number

The legs of a right triangle are $6$ and $9$. Find the hypotenuse in simplest radical form.

  1. $c^2 = 36 + 81 = 117$.

    Square and add.

  2. $c = \sqrt{117} = \sqrt{9 \cdot 13} = 3\sqrt{13}$.

    Pull out the perfect square factor (Chapter 1).

$3\sqrt{13}$

Faster: $6 : 9 = 2 : 3$, so the hypotenuse is $3\sqrt{2^2 + 3^2} = 3\sqrt{13}$.

Spot a scaled triple

The legs of a right triangle are $2.4$ and $3.2$. Find the hypotenuse without squaring decimals.

  1. $2.4 : 3.2 = 24 : 32 = 3 : 4$, so the legs are $3(0.8)$ and $4(0.8)$.

    Writing the ratio in whole numbers reveals the triple.

  2. The triangle is a $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle scaled by $0.8$, so the hypotenuse is $5(0.8) = 4$.

    Every length scales by the same factor.

$4$

The altitude of an isosceles triangle

An isosceles triangle has legs $13$ and base $10$. Find the altitude to the base.

  1. The altitude to the base cuts the base into two halves of $5$ each.

    In an isosceles triangle the two halves are congruent right triangles (Section 11.5), so the foot of the altitude is the midpoint.

  2. Each half is a right triangle with hypotenuse $13$ and leg $5$, so the altitude is $\sqrt{169 - 25} = \sqrt{144} = 12$.

    A $5$-$12$-$13$ triangle.

$12$

Acute, right or obtuse?

Classify the triangles with sides (a) $6, 7, 10$ and (b) $7, 8, 10$.

  1. (a) $6^2 + 7^2 = 85$ and $10^2 = 100$. Since $85 < 100$, the angle opposite $10$ is obtuse.

    Compare the two smaller squares with the square of the longest side.

  2. (b) $7^2 + 8^2 = 113 > 100$, so the largest angle is acute, and so are the others.

    The largest angle is opposite the longest side; if it is acute, all of them are.

(a) Obtuse; (b) acute.

The long diagonal of a box

A box measures $3 \times 4 \times 12$. Find the distance between opposite corners.

  1. The diagonal of the $3 \times 4$ bottom face is $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.

    The bottom face is a rectangle, and its diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle.

  2. The vertical edge of length $12$ is perpendicular to that diagonal, so the long diagonal is $\sqrt{5^2 + 12^2} = 13$.

    Apply Pythagoras a second time in a vertical right triangle.

$13$ (in general $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}$).

Perimeter and hypotenuse

A right triangle has perimeter $30$ and hypotenuse $13$. Find its area and its legs.

  1. The legs satisfy $a + b = 30 - 13 = 17$ and $a^2 + b^2 = 169$.

    The perimeter gives a sum; Pythagoras gives a sum of squares.

  2. $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$, so $289 = 169 + 2ab$ and $ab = 60$.

    Squaring the sum produces the sum of squares plus twice the product we want.

  3. Area $= \tfrac12 ab = 30$. The legs have sum $17$ and product $60$, so they are $5$ and $12$.

    The legs are perpendicular, so one is the height on the other. Two numbers with sum $17$ and product $60$ are $5$ and $12$.

Area $30$; legs $5$ and $12$.

$(a+b)^2 - (a^2 + b^2) = 2ab$ links perimeter, hypotenuse and area.

11.5 Congruent Triangles

Hook

A carpenter nails three boards into a triangle and it is rigid; nail four into a square and it wobbles into a rhombus. Three side lengths lock a triangle's shape completely. That rigidity is what congruence theorems are about: a few measurements force everything else.

Concept

Two figures are congruent if one is an exact copy of the other. For triangles, $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF$ means all corresponding sides and angles are equal, with the vertices listed in matching order: $A \leftrightarrow D$, $B \leftrightarrow E$, $C \leftrightarrow F$.

You don't need all six equalities. Any one of these is enough:

  • SSS: three pairs of equal sides.
  • SAS: two sides and the angle between them.
  • ASA: two angles and the side between them.
  • AAS: two angles and a side that isn't between them (the third angles match automatically).
  • For right triangles: HL (hypotenuse and a leg), LL (both legs), or an acute angle and a corresponding side.

Warning: there is no SSA. Two sides and an angle that is not between them can fit two different triangles. The explorer shows how.

The payoff: once two triangles are congruent, every corresponding part is equal. Congruent triangles are the main way to prove that two segments or two angles are equal. Mark equal parts on your diagram as you find them.

Read off corresponding parts

$\triangle PQR \cong \triangle XYZ$, with $PQ = 5$, $QR = 7$ and $\angle X = 40^\circ$. Find $XY$, $YZ$ and $\angle P$.

  1. The order of letters matches $P \leftrightarrow X$, $Q \leftrightarrow Y$, $R \leftrightarrow Z$.

    The congruence statement itself tells you which parts correspond.

  2. $XY = PQ = 5$, $YZ = QR = 7$, and $\angle P = \angle X = 40^\circ$.

    Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal.

$XY = 5$, $YZ = 7$, $\angle P = 40^\circ$.

Name the criterion

Which congruence criterion applies? (a) $AB = DE$, $BC = EF$, $\angle B = \angle E$. (b) $\angle A = \angle D$, $\angle B = \angle E$, $BC = EF$. (c) $AB = DE$, $AC = DF$, $\angle B = \angle E$.

  1. (a) $\angle B$ is between sides $AB$ and $BC$: SAS.

    The angle's vertex is the shared endpoint of the two sides.

  2. (b) Two angles, and side $BC$ is not between $A$ and $B$: AAS.

    With two angles known, the third is known too, so any matching side works.

  3. (c) $\angle B$ is not between $AB$ and $AC$ (they meet at $A$): this is SSA, which does not prove congruence.

    The given angle must be the one included between the given sides.

(a) SAS; (b) AAS; (c) no conclusion (SSA).

A kite's equal angles

In quadrilateral $ABCD$, $AB = AD$ and $CB = CD$. Prove that $\angle B = \angle D$.

  1. Draw diagonal $AC$ and compare $\triangle ABC$ with $\triangle ADC$.

    The angles we want are in two triangles that look like mirror images.

  2. $AB = AD$, $CB = CD$, and $AC = AC$. So $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle ADC$ by SSS.

    A shared side counts as a pair of equal sides.

  3. $\angle B$ and $\angle D$ correspond, so they are equal.

    Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are equal.

$\triangle ABC \cong \triangle ADC$ (SSS), so $\angle ABC = \angle ADC$.

Segments that bisect each other

Segments $AC$ and $BD$ cross at $M$, the midpoint of both. Prove that $AB = CD$.

  1. Compare $\triangle AMB$ and $\triangle CMD$: $AM = CM$ and $BM = DM$.

    $M$ is the midpoint of each segment.

  2. $\angle AMB = \angle CMD$ as vertical angles. So $\triangle AMB \cong \triangle CMD$ by SAS.

    The angle at $M$ is between the two known sides in each triangle.

  3. Therefore $AB = CD$.

    They are corresponding sides.

$AB = CD$ by SAS congruence.

The perpendicular bisector property

$P$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$, which meets $AB$ at $M$. Prove that $PA = PB$.

  1. In $\triangle PMA$ and $\triangle PMB$: $AM = BM$, $\angle PMA = \angle PMB = 90^\circ$, and $PM = PM$.

    The perpendicular bisector passes through the midpoint at a right angle.

  2. So $\triangle PMA \cong \triangle PMB$ by SAS (or LL), and $PA = PB$.

    $PA$ and $PB$ are corresponding hypotenuses.

$PA = PB$; this is why the circumcenter is equidistant from all three vertices.

Why SSA fails

Show that there are two noncongruent triangles with $AB = 10$, $\angle A = 30^\circ$ and $BC = 6$.

  1. Put $C$ on the ray from $A$ making $30^\circ$ with $AB$, at distance $t$ from $A$. Drop the perpendicular from $B$ to that ray: it has length $10 \sin 30^\circ = 5$, and its foot is $10\cos 30^\circ = 5\sqrt3$ from $A$.

    The distance from $B$ to the ray decides how many points on it are exactly $6$ from $B$.

  2. Since $6 > 5$, the circle of radius $6$ about $B$ crosses the ray twice, at distance $\sqrt{6^2 - 5^2} = \sqrt{11}$ on either side of the foot: $t = 5\sqrt3 \pm \sqrt{11}$.

    Each crossing is a right triangle with hypotenuse $6$ and leg $5$.

  3. Both values are positive ($\approx 11.98$ and $\approx 5.34$), so both points lie on the ray. The two triangles have different $AC$, so they are not congruent.

    Same $AB$, same $\angle A$, same $BC$, yet different triangles.

$AC = 5\sqrt3 + \sqrt{11}$ or $AC = 5\sqrt3 - \sqrt{11}$: two different triangles, so SSA is not a congruence test.

Two equilateral triangles

$B$ lies on segment $AC$. Equilateral triangles $ABD$ and $BCE$ are drawn on the same side of $AC$. Prove that $AE = DC$.

  1. Compare $\triangle ABE$ and $\triangle DBC$.

    $AE$ is a side of the first and $DC$ is the corresponding side of the second.

  2. $AB = DB$ (sides of equilateral $\triangle ABD$) and $BE = BC$ (sides of equilateral $\triangle BCE$).

    In an equilateral triangle all sides are equal.

  3. $\angle ABE = \angle ABD + \angle DBE = 60^\circ + \angle DBE$ and $\angle DBC = \angle DBE + \angle EBC = \angle DBE + 60^\circ$. So $\angle ABE = \angle DBC$ (both are $120^\circ$).

    Each angle is made of a $60^\circ$ angle plus the shared angle $\angle DBE$. (The angles on a line give $\angle DBE = 180^\circ - 60^\circ - 60^\circ = 60^\circ$.)

  4. By SAS, $\triangle ABE \cong \triangle DBC$, so $AE = DC$.

    The equal angles are between the equal sides.

$\triangle ABE \cong \triangle DBC$ by SAS, so $AE = DC$.

To prove two segments equal, find two triangles that contain them and prove those triangles congruent.

11.6 Similar Triangles

Hook

How tall is a tree you can't climb? Stand in the sun: if you are $1.5$ m tall and cast a $2$ m shadow while the tree casts an $18$ m shadow, the tree is $13.5$ m tall. The sun's rays make the same angle everywhere, so you and the tree form the same triangle at two different sizes.

Concept

Two triangles are similar if one is a scaled copy of the other: corresponding angles are equal and corresponding sides share one ratio $k$. We write $\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF$, again in matching order, and

$$\frac{DE}{AB} = \frac{EF}{BC} = \frac{FD}{CA} = k, \qquad \frac{[DEF]}{[ABC]} = k^2.$$

Every other length (altitudes, medians, perimeters) scales by $k$ too, and areas scale by $k^2$. Ways to prove similarity:

  • AA: two pairs of equal angles (the third follows). By far the most useful.
  • SAS: two sides in the same ratio with equal included angles.
  • SSS: all three sides in the same ratio.

Parallel lines are the usual source of equal angles. If $DE \parallel BC$ with $D$ on $AB$ and $E$ on $AC$, then $\triangle ADE \sim \triangle ABC$. Two other patterns come up constantly. In a right triangle, the altitude to the hypotenuse creates three similar triangles. And an angle bisector divides the opposite side in the ratio of the adjacent sides (the Angle Bisector Theorem): if $AD$ bisects $\angle A$, then $\dfrac{BD}{DC} = \dfrac{AB}{AC}$.

Use the scale factor

$\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF$ with $AB = 4$, $BC = 6$ and $DE = 10$. Find $EF$.

  1. $k = \dfrac{DE}{AB} = \dfrac{10}{4} = \dfrac52$.

    $AB$ and $DE$ correspond (first two letters of each name), so they give the ratio.

  2. $EF = \tfrac52 \cdot BC = \tfrac52 \cdot 6 = 15$.

    $EF$ corresponds to $BC$, and every side scales by $k$.

$15$

Shadows

A $1.5$ m post casts a $2$ m shadow. At the same moment a tree casts an $18$ m shadow. How tall is the tree?

  1. Both make right triangles (vertical object, flat ground) with the same angle of the sun's rays, so they are similar by AA.

    A right angle plus one shared angle is two pairs of equal angles.

  2. $\dfrac{h}{18} = \dfrac{1.5}{2}$, so $h = 18 \cdot 0.75 = 13.5$.

    Height over shadow is the same ratio in both triangles.

$13.5$ m

A parallel segment

In $\triangle ABC$, $D$ is on $AB$ and $E$ is on $AC$ with $DE \parallel BC$. If $AD = 3$, $DB = 6$ and $DE = 4$, find $BC$.

  1. $\angle ADE = \angle ABC$ and $\angle AED = \angle ACB$ (corresponding angles), so $\triangle ADE \sim \triangle ABC$.

    Parallel lines cut by a transversal make equal corresponding angles: AA similarity.

  2. $k = \dfrac{AB}{AD} = \dfrac{3 + 6}{3} = 3$.

    Corresponding sides are $AD$ and $AB$, the whole side, not $DB$.

  3. $BC = 3 \cdot DE = 12$.

    Scale the corresponding side.

$12$

The midpoint triangle

A triangle has sides $8$, $10$ and $14$. Find the perimeter of the triangle formed by joining the midpoints of its sides.

  1. Joining the midpoints of two sides gives a segment parallel to the third side and half as long.

    If $M$, $N$ are midpoints of $AB$, $AC$, then $\triangle AMN \sim \triangle ABC$ by SAS with ratio $\tfrac12$ (both sides halved, common angle $A$).

  2. The midpoint triangle has sides $4$, $5$, $7$ and perimeter $16$.

    Each of its sides is half of the side it is parallel to.

$16$

The altitude to the hypotenuse

In right $\triangle ABC$ with the right angle at $C$, altitude $CD$ cuts the hypotenuse into $AD = 9$ and $DB = 16$. Find $CD$, $AC$ and $BC$.

  1. $\triangle ADC \sim \triangle CDB$: both have a right angle at $D$, and $\angle ACD = 90^\circ - \angle A = \angle B$.

    AA: the two small triangles share their angles with the big one.

  2. $\dfrac{AD}{CD} = \dfrac{CD}{DB}$, so $CD^2 = 9 \cdot 16 = 144$ and $CD = 12$.

    Match the short leg to the long leg in each small triangle.

  3. $AC = \sqrt{9^2 + 12^2} = 15$ and $BC = \sqrt{16^2 + 12^2} = 20$.

    Pythagoras in each small right triangle. Check: $15^2 + 20^2 = 625 = 25^2$. ✓

The Angle Bisector Theorem

In $\triangle ABC$, $AB = 6$, $AC = 9$ and $BC = 10$. The bisector of $\angle A$ meets $BC$ at $D$. Find $BD$ and $DC$.

  1. $\dfrac{BD}{DC} = \dfrac{AB}{AC} = \dfrac{6}{9} = \dfrac23$.

    The bisector divides the opposite side in the ratio of the two sides next to the angle.

  2. Write $BD = 2t$, $DC = 3t$. Then $5t = 10$, so $t = 2$.

    The two pieces make up all of $BC$.

  3. $BD = 4$, $DC = 6$.

    The shorter piece is next to the shorter side.

$BD = 4$, $DC = 6$

Areas scale by the square

Two similar triangles have corresponding sides in the ratio $3 : 5$. The smaller has area $18$. Find the area of the larger.

  1. $k = \dfrac53$, so the area ratio is $k^2 = \dfrac{25}{9}$.

    Area is base times height over two, and both base and height scale by $k$.

  2. Larger area $= 18 \cdot \dfrac{25}{9} = 50$.

    Multiply by the area ratio, not the side ratio.

$50$

Lengths scale by $k$, areas by $k^2$.

A square in a right triangle

A right triangle has legs $3$ and $4$. A square sits in the right-angle corner, with two sides along the legs and its far vertex on the hypotenuse. Find the side of the square.

  1. Let the side be $s$. Above the square, along the leg of length $3$, is a small right triangle with vertical leg $3 - s$ and horizontal leg $s$ (the top side of the square).

    Name what we want and look at the pieces left over.

  2. The top of the square is parallel to the leg of length $4$, so the small triangle is similar to the whole triangle: $\dfrac{3 - s}{s} = \dfrac{3}{4}$.

    Parallel lines give equal corresponding angles (AA). Vertical leg over horizontal leg is the same ratio in both.

  3. $4(3 - s) = 3s$, so $12 = 7s$ and $s = \dfrac{12}{7}$.

    Cross-multiply and solve.

$\dfrac{12}{7}$

When a length is unknown, look for a parallel line that creates a smaller similar triangle.

11.7 Introduction to Trigonometry

Hook

Every right triangle with a $30^\circ$ angle is a scaled copy of every other one, by AA similarity. So the ratio "side opposite $30^\circ$ over hypotenuse" is the same number for all of them, $\tfrac12$. Trigonometry gives these fixed ratios names, so one angle and one side are enough to find the rest.

Concept

In right $\triangle ABC$ with $\angle C = 90^\circ$, relative to $\angle A$ the side $a = BC$ is opposite, $b = AC$ is adjacent, and $c = AB$ is the hypotenuse.

$$\sin A = \frac{a}{c}, \quad \cos A = \frac{b}{c}, \quad \tan A = \frac{a}{b} = \frac{\sin A}{\cos A}$$

$$\csc A = \frac{1}{\sin A}, \quad \sec A = \frac{1}{\cos A}, \quad \cot A = \frac{1}{\tan A}.$$

Because $\angle B = 90^\circ - \angle A$ and the roles of $a$ and $b$ swap, $\sin A = \cos(90^\circ - A)$. Dividing $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ by $c^2$ gives the key identity

$$\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1, \qquad \tan^2 A + 1 = \sec^2 A, \qquad 1 + \cot^2 A = \csc^2 A.$$

Special right triangles. Half a square gives the $45^\circ$-$45^\circ$-$90^\circ$ triangle with sides $1 : 1 : \sqrt2$. Half an equilateral triangle gives the $30^\circ$-$60^\circ$-$90^\circ$ triangle with sides $1 : \sqrt3 : 2$: the hypotenuse is twice the side opposite $30^\circ$.

$$\sin 30^\circ = \tfrac12,\ \ \cos 30^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ \ \tan 30^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{3}; \qquad \sin 45^\circ = \cos 45^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2},\ \ \tan 45^\circ = 1;$$ $$\sin 60^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ \ \cos 60^\circ = \tfrac12,\ \ \tan 60^\circ = \sqrt3.$$

If you forget which of $\sin 30^\circ$ and $\cos 30^\circ$ is $\tfrac12$, sketch the triangle: the side opposite the small angle is the short one.

Ratios in a 3-4-5 triangle

In right $\triangle ABC$ with $\angle C = 90^\circ$, $BC = 3$, $AC = 4$ and $AB = 5$. Find $\sin A$, $\cos A$ and $\tan A$.

  1. From $A$: opposite is $BC = 3$, adjacent is $AC = 4$, hypotenuse is $5$.

    Labels depend on which angle you stand at.

  2. $\sin A = \tfrac35$, $\cos A = \tfrac45$, $\tan A = \tfrac34$.

    Apply the definitions.

$\sin A = \tfrac35$, $\cos A = \tfrac45$, $\tan A = \tfrac34$

Special triangles

(a) A $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle has legs $7$. Find the hypotenuse. (b) A $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle has shorter leg $5$. Find the other sides.

  1. (a) Hypotenuse $= 7\sqrt2$.

    Ratio $1 : 1 : \sqrt2$.

  2. (b) Longer leg $= 5\sqrt3$ and hypotenuse $= 10$.

    Ratio $1 : \sqrt3 : 2$, with the shorter leg opposite $30^\circ$.

(a) $7\sqrt2$; (b) $5\sqrt3$ and $10$.

Start from the hypotenuse

A $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle has hypotenuse $12$. Find both legs.

  1. Shorter leg $= \tfrac12 \cdot 12 = 6$.

    The hypotenuse is twice the leg opposite $30^\circ$; equivalently $12 \sin 30^\circ = 6$.

  2. Longer leg $= 6\sqrt3$.

$6$ and $6\sqrt3$

    Any triangle with these ratios will do; this one has whole-number sides.

  2. $\sin A = \tfrac{12}{13}$, $\tan A = \tfrac{12}{5}$.

    Read them off the triangle.

  3. $\sec A = \tfrac{13}{5}$, $\csc A = \tfrac{13}{12}$, $\cot A = \tfrac{5}{12}$.

    These are reciprocals of cos, sin, tan.

Height from an angle

From a point $20$ m from the base of a building, the angle up to the top is $60^\circ$. How tall is the building?

    Tangent links the two legs, which is exactly what we have and want.

  2. $h = 20\sqrt3 \approx 34.6$ m.

Complementary angles

Find the acute angle $\theta$ such that $\sin(2\theta + 20^\circ) = \cos(\theta + 10^\circ)$, assuming both angles are acute.

  1. $\cos(\theta + 10^\circ) = \sin\big(90^\circ - (\theta + 10^\circ)\big) = \sin(80^\circ - \theta)$.

    Sine of an angle is cosine of its complement.

  2. $2\theta + 20^\circ = 80^\circ - \theta$, so $3\theta = 60^\circ$ and $\theta = 20^\circ$.

    For acute angles, equal sines mean equal angles (a bigger acute angle has a bigger opposite side).

11.8 Area of a Triangle

Hook

Slide the top vertex of a triangle along a line parallel to its base. The triangle leans, stretches and looks completely different, but its area never changes. Area depends only on the base and the height, and that one idea leads to every area formula in this section.

Concept

Write $[ABC]$ for the area of $\triangle ABC$. Two copies of a right triangle make a rectangle, so its area is half the product of the legs. Splitting any triangle by an altitude reduces it to right triangles, which gives three formulas:

$$[ABC] = \frac{a\,h_a}{2} = \frac{ab \sin C}{2} = rs.$$

  • Half base times height: the most common. Pick a side whose altitude you can find. In an obtuse triangle the altitude may fall outside, on the extended base.
  • Two sides and the included angle: the altitude from $A$ to $BC$ is $b \sin C$. For an obtuse angle use $\sin(180^\circ - \theta) = \sin\theta$.
  • Inradius times semiperimeter: join the incenter to the vertices. You get three triangles with heights $r$ and bases $a, b, c$, so $[ABC] = \tfrac12 r(a+b+c) = rs$. Use it whenever the incircle appears.

The formulas also work backwards: because area is the same however you compute it, $a h_a = b h_b = c h_c$, and the shortest altitude goes to the longest side. For similar triangles with ratio $k$, the areas have ratio $k^2$.

Base and height

A triangle has a base of $10$ and a height of $7$ to that base. Find its area.

  1. $[ABC] = \tfrac12 \cdot 10 \cdot 7 = 35$.

    Half of base times height.

$35$

Two ways to compute one area

A right triangle has legs $9$ and $12$. Find its area and the altitude to its hypotenuse.

  1. Area $= \tfrac12 \cdot 9 \cdot 12 = 54$, and the hypotenuse is $15$ (a $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle times $3$).

    Each leg is the altitude to the other.

  2. Using the hypotenuse as base: $\tfrac12 \cdot 15 \cdot h = 54$, so $h = \dfrac{108}{15} = 7.2$.

    Same area, different base, so we can solve for the new height.

Area $54$; altitude to the hypotenuse $7.2$.

Two sides and the angle between

Find the area of a triangle with sides $8$ and $10$ and included angle (a) $30^\circ$; (b) $150^\circ$.

  1. (a) $\tfrac12 \cdot 8 \cdot 10 \cdot \sin 30^\circ = 40 \cdot \tfrac12 = 20$.

    $\tfrac12 ab \sin C$ with the angle between the two sides.

  2. (b) $\sin 150^\circ = \sin 30^\circ = \tfrac12$, so the area is again $20$.

    Opening the angle to $150^\circ$ gives the same height as $30^\circ$, just on the other side.

$20$ in both cases.

Equilateral triangle

Find the area of an equilateral triangle with side $6$.

  1. $\tfrac12 \cdot 6 \cdot 6 \cdot \sin 60^\circ = 18 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = 9\sqrt3$.

    All angles are $60^\circ$.

  2. Check: the altitude is $3\sqrt3$ (a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle with hypotenuse $6$), and $\tfrac12 \cdot 6 \cdot 3\sqrt3 = 9\sqrt3$. ✓

    In general an equilateral triangle with side $s$ has area $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}s^2$.

$9\sqrt3$

Comparing altitudes

A triangle has sides $4$, $5$ and $6$. What fraction of the longest altitude is the shortest altitude?

  1. With area $K$, each altitude is $h = \dfrac{2K}{\text{side}}$, so altitudes are inversely proportional to their sides.

    $K = \tfrac12 \cdot \text{side} \cdot h$ for every side.

The 13-14-15 triangle

11.9 A Handful of Helpful Hints

Hook

Many geometry problems look impossible until you draw one extra line, and then they fall apart. Which line? There are signs to look for. Ratios ask for a parallel. Areas and distances ask for a perpendicular. A right angle asks for a circle.

Concept

Your best friends are parallel lines, perpendicular lines, congruent triangles and similar triangles. Lines show up in three ways: some are given, some you draw, and some you discover.

Proving two lines parallel: equal alternate interior angles, equal corresponding angles, supplementary same-side interior angles, or two points of one line at the same distance from the other.

Proving an angle is right:

  • the angle is inscribed in a semicircle;
  • two adjacent angles on a line are equal (each is then $90^\circ$);
  • the other two angles of its triangle are complementary;
  • the sides satisfy $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$;
  • the triangle is similar or congruent to a right triangle;
  • the median to a side is half that side.

Perpendicular without angles: a radius to a point of tangency, a radius that bisects a chord, or a line through a vertex and the orthocenter.

Rule of thumb: congruent triangles prove things equal; similar triangles find lengths and ratios; perpendiculars help with areas. If the triangles you need aren't there, draw a line that makes them.

Are the lines parallel?

A transversal crosses lines $\ell$ and $m$. (a) A pair of alternate interior angles measure $65^\circ$ and $65^\circ$. (b) In another figure, two same-side interior angles measure $110^\circ$ and $75^\circ$. Are the lines parallel?

  1. (a) Equal alternate interior angles: yes, $\ell \parallel m$.

    This is one of the standard tests for parallel lines.

  2. (b) $110^\circ + 75^\circ = 185^\circ \ne 180^\circ$: not parallel.

    Same-side interior angles of parallel lines must be supplementary.

(a) Parallel; (b) not parallel.

Test for a right triangle

Is the triangle with sides $20$, $21$, $29$ a right triangle?

  1. $20^2 + 21^2 = 400 + 441 = 841 = 29^2$.

    If the sides satisfy Pythagoras, the triangle must be right (an acute or obtuse triangle can't).

Yes; the right angle is opposite the side $29$.

A right angle from a semicircle

$AB$ is a diameter of a circle with radius $6.5$, and $C$ is on the circle with $AC = 5$. Find $BC$ and the area of $\triangle ABC$.

  1. $\angle ACB = 90^\circ$ because it is inscribed in a semicircle.

    This is a right angle we discover.

  2. $AB = 13$, so $BC = \sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = 12$.

    The diameter is the hypotenuse.

  3. Area $= \tfrac12 \cdot 5 \cdot 12 = 30$.

    The legs are perpendicular.

$BC = 12$, area $30$.

Draw an altitude

An isosceles triangle has legs $8$ and vertex angle $120^\circ$. Find its base and area.

  1. Drop the altitude from the vertex. It bisects the vertex angle and the base, making two right triangles with angles $60^\circ$ at the top and $30^\circ$ at the base.

    The base angles are $\tfrac{180^\circ - 120^\circ}{2} = 30^\circ$; the altitude produces special right triangles.

  2. Each is $30$-$60$-$90$ with hypotenuse $8$: altitude $4$, half-base $4\sqrt3$.

    The altitude is opposite the $30^\circ$ angle.

  3. Base $= 8\sqrt3$, area $= \tfrac12 \cdot 8\sqrt3 \cdot 4 = 16\sqrt3$.

    Half base times height.

Base $8\sqrt3$, area $16\sqrt3$.

Draw a parallel

In $\triangle ABC$, $D$ is the midpoint of $BC$ and $E$ is on $AC$ with $AE = 2\,EC$. Segments $AD$ and $BE$ meet at $F$. Find $AF : FD$.

  1. Through $D$, draw a line parallel to $BE$, meeting $AC$ at $G$.

    Ratios suggest similar triangles, and a parallel line creates them.

  2. In $\triangle CBE$, $D$ is the midpoint of $CB$ and $DG \parallel BE$, so $G$ is the midpoint of $CE$: $EG = GC = \tfrac12 EC$.

    $\triangle CDG \sim \triangle CBE$ with ratio $\tfrac12$.

  3. $AE = 2\,EC = 4\,EG$.

    Rewrite both lengths in the same unit, $EG$.

  4. In $\triangle ADG$, $FE \parallel DG$, so $\dfrac{AF}{FD} = \dfrac{AE}{EG} = 4$.

    $\triangle AFE \sim \triangle ADG$ again by parallel lines: the line $FE$ cuts both sides in the same ratio.

$AF : FD = 4 : 1$

One well-chosen parallel line can turn a ratio problem into two similar-triangle steps.

Compare with the book's Example 11-25.

The Angle Bisector Theorem by areas

$AD$ bisects $\angle A$ of $\triangle ABC$, with $D$ on $BC$. Prove $\dfrac{BD}{DC} = \dfrac{AB}{AC}$ using areas.

  1. $\triangle ABD$ and $\triangle ACD$ have the same altitude from $A$ to line $BC$, so $\dfrac{[ABD]}{[ACD]} = \dfrac{BD}{DC}$.

    Same height, so the areas are in the ratio of the bases.

  2. With $\theta = \angle BAD = \angle DAC$: $\dfrac{[ABD]}{[ACD]} = \dfrac{\tfrac12 \cdot AB \cdot AD \sin\theta}{\tfrac12 \cdot AC \cdot AD \sin\theta} = \dfrac{AB}{AC}$.

    Compute the same two areas with $\tfrac12 ab\sin C$, using the equal angles at $A$.

  3. Both expressions equal $\dfrac{[ABD]}{[ACD]}$, so $\dfrac{BD}{DC} = \dfrac{AB}{AC}$.

    Computing one quantity two ways gives an equation.

Both ratios equal $[ABD]/[ACD]$, so $BD/DC = AB/AC$.

Computing an area (or a ratio of areas) in two different ways is a powerful proof technique.
