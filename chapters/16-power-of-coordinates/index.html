Chapter 16 · Geometry

The Power of Coordinates

Put a grid on the plane and every point gets an address, every line gets an equation, and every circle gets a formula. Geometry questions turn into algebra questions, which you already know how to grind through. This chapter builds that dictionary between pictures and equations, and shows when it is worth using.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • A point is an ordered pair $(x, y)$: distance right, then distance up. In polar form it is $(r, \theta)$: distance from the origin and angle from the positive $x$-axis, with $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$.
  • Translations are easy in Cartesian coordinates, $(x, y) \to (x + a, y + b)$; rotations about the origin are easy in polar, $(r, \theta) \to (r, \theta + \alpha)$.
  • Slope is rise over run: $m = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$. Lines: $y = mx + b$ (slope-intercept) or $Ax + By = C$ (standard, slope $-A/B$).
  • Parallel lines have equal slopes; perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is $-1$.
  • Find intercepts by setting the other variable to $0$. Plot a line from two convenient points or by walking the slope from the $y$-intercept.
  • Distance: $d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$. Circle: $(x - p)^2 + (y - q)^2 = R^2$; complete the square to find the center and radius.
  • Intersections come from solving the equations together: substitute or eliminate.
  • Midpoint $\left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$; centroid is the average of the three vertices. For proofs, place the axes to make the coordinates as simple as possible.

16.1 Labelling the Plane

Hook

In a game of Battleship you never say "the ship near the left edge, a bit above the middle." You say "C-7." Two labels pin down one square exactly. Coordinates do the same thing for every point of the plane, with numbers instead of letters, and that is enough to turn geometry into arithmetic.

Concept

Start with a number line, the $x$-axis. Draw a second number line, the $y$-axis, perpendicular to it and crossing at $0$; the crossing point is the origin. Every point now gets an ordered pair $(x, y)$: how far right (negative means left) and how far up (negative means down). These are its Cartesian coordinates. Order matters: $(2, 5)$ and $(5, 2)$ are different points.

The axes cut the plane into four quadrants, numbered I, II, III, IV counterclockwise starting from the upper right, where both coordinates are positive. Points on an axis belong to no quadrant.

Quadrant I: $(+, +)$   II: $(-, +)$   III: $(-, -)$   IV: $(+, -)$

The grid is a choice, not a law of nature. Another very useful choice is polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$: $r$ is the distance from the origin and $\theta$ is the angle measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis. A right triangle with hypotenuse $r$ connects the two systems:

$$x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}.$$

A point has only one Cartesian name, but many polar names: adding $360^\circ$ to $\theta$ gives the same point, so $(2, 30^\circ)$ and $(2, 390^\circ)$ coincide. Unless told otherwise, "coordinates" means Cartesian.

Which quadrant?

Name the quadrant of each point: $(-3, 5)$, $(4, -2)$, $(-1, -6)$, $(0, 6)$.

  1. $(-3, 5)$: left and up, so Quadrant II. $(4, -2)$: right and down, so Quadrant IV.

    Read the signs: the first coordinate says left or right, the second says up or down.

  2. $(-1, -6)$: both negative, so Quadrant III.

    Left and down is the lower-left quadrant, the third one counterclockwise from Quadrant I.

  3. $(0, 6)$ sits on the $y$-axis, so it is in no quadrant.

    The axes are the borders between quadrants and belong to none of them.

II, IV, III, and none (on the $y$-axis).

Following directions

Start at $(3, -1)$. Walk $5$ units left, then $4$ units up. Where are you, and in which quadrant?

  1. Left $5$: $x$ goes from $3$ to $3 - 5 = -2$.

    Horizontal moves change only the first coordinate; left means subtract.

  2. Up $4$: $y$ goes from $-1$ to $-1 + 4 = 3$. You are at $(-2, 3)$.

    Vertical moves change only the second coordinate.

$(-2, 3)$, in Quadrant II.

Polar to Cartesian

Find the Cartesian coordinates of the polar point $(4, 60^\circ)$.

  1. Drop a perpendicular from the point to the $x$-axis. This makes a right triangle with hypotenuse $4$ and a $60^\circ$ angle at the origin.

    The horizontal leg is $x$ and the vertical leg is $y$.

  2. It is a $30^\circ$-$60^\circ$-$90^\circ$ triangle, so the short leg (next to the $60^\circ$ angle) is $2$ and the long leg is $2\sqrt3$.

    In a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle the sides are in ratio $1 : \sqrt3 : 2$. Equivalently, $x = 4\cos 60^\circ$ and $y = 4\sin 60^\circ$.

$(2, 2\sqrt3)$

Cartesian to polar

Give polar coordinates for $(-3, 3)$, with $0^\circ \le \theta < 360^\circ$.

  1. $r = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt2$.

    $r$ is the distance to the origin: the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $3$ and $3$.

  2. The legs are equal, so the segment to the origin makes a $45^\circ$ angle with the negative $x$-axis. Measured from the positive $x$-axis, $\theta = 180^\circ - 45^\circ = 135^\circ$.

    Always sketch the quadrant first. The triangle tells you a reference angle; the quadrant tells you where to measure it from.

$(3\sqrt2, 135^\circ)$

Many names for one point

Find the Cartesian coordinates of $(2, 270^\circ)$, and give two other polar names for the same point.

  1. $270^\circ$ is three quarter-turns from the positive $x$-axis, pointing straight down. Two units down is $(0, -2)$.

    The angles $0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ$ point right, up, left and down.

  2. Turning $90^\circ$ clockwise also points down, so $(2, -90^\circ)$ works; so does $(2, 270^\circ + 360^\circ) = (2, 630^\circ)$.

    Any two angles that differ by a multiple of $360^\circ$ point in the same direction.

$(0, -2)$; for example $(2, -90^\circ)$ and $(2, 630^\circ)$.

A square in polar coordinates

A square is centered at the origin and one vertex is the polar point $(4, 10^\circ)$. Find polar coordinates for the other three vertices and the side length of the square.

  1. All four vertices are the same distance from the center, so each has $r = 4$.

    The diagonals of a square are equal and bisect each other, so the center is equidistant from the vertices.

  2. The diagonals are perpendicular, so the vertices are $90^\circ$ apart: $(4, 100^\circ)$, $(4, 190^\circ)$, $(4, 280^\circ)$.

    Going around a square, each vertex is a quarter-turn further around the center.

  3. Two neighbouring vertices and the center form an isosceles right triangle with legs $4$, so the side is $4\sqrt2$.

    The angle between neighbouring radii is $90^\circ$; the side is the hypotenuse.

$(4, 100^\circ)$, $(4, 190^\circ)$, $(4, 280^\circ)$; side $4\sqrt2$.

Polar coordinates shine when a figure is symmetric about the origin.

Distance between polar points

Find the distance between (a) $(3, 40^\circ)$ and $(5, 220^\circ)$; (b) $(5, 20^\circ)$ and $(5, 80^\circ)$.

  1. (a) $220^\circ = 40^\circ + 180^\circ$, so the points lie on opposite rays from the origin. The origin is between them, and the distance is $3 + 5 = 8$.

    Adding $180^\circ$ reverses direction, so the two segments to the origin form one straight segment.

  2. (b) Both points are $5$ from the origin and the angle between their rays is $80^\circ - 20^\circ = 60^\circ$. The triangle with the origin is isosceles with a $60^\circ$ apex angle.

    The two radii are the equal sides; the apex angle is the difference of the angles.

  3. Its base angles are $(180^\circ - 60^\circ)/2 = 60^\circ$ each, so it is equilateral and the distance is $5$.

    An isosceles triangle with one $60^\circ$ angle at the apex has all angles $60^\circ$.

(a) $8$; (b) $5$.

Two polar points and the origin form a triangle with two known sides and the included angle.

16.2 What's it Good For?

Hook

Tell a friend "move the couch two metres toward the window" and it is easy. Tell them "spin the couch a third of a turn about the lamp" and directions in metres get awkward fast. Different coordinate systems are good at describing different motions, and picking the right one can make a problem trivial.

Concept

An equation in $x$ and $y$ describes the set of all points whose coordinates make it true. The simplest Cartesian equations give straight lines: $x = 1$ lets $y$ be anything, so it is a vertical line; $y = -5$ is a horizontal line.

The simplest polar equations give different shapes. $r = 3$ lets $\theta$ be anything while the distance stays $3$: a circle of radius $3$ about the origin. $\theta = 47^\circ$ fixes the direction and lets the distance vary: a ray from the origin.

The systems also handle motions differently:

Translation by $(a, b)$: $(x, y) \to (x + a,\ y + b)$ (simple in Cartesian).

Rotation by $\alpha$ about the origin: $(r, \theta) \to (r,\ \theta + \alpha)$ (simple in polar).

Reflection in the $x$-axis: $(x, y) \to (x, -y)$ and $(r, \theta) \to (r, -\theta)$.
Reflection in the $y$-axis: $(x, y) \to (-x, y)$ and $(r, \theta) \to (r, 180^\circ - \theta)$.

Rule of thumb: Cartesian coordinates for lines and sliding; polar coordinates for circles about the origin and turning. You will meet these motions again in Chapter 19.

Two simple lines

Describe the graphs of $x = -2$ and $y = 3$, and find where they cross.

  1. $x = -2$: every point with first coordinate $-2$, a vertical line $2$ units left of the $y$-axis.

    The equation says nothing about $y$, so $y$ is free.

  2. $y = 3$: a horizontal line $3$ units above the $x$-axis.

    Now $x$ is free and $y$ is pinned.

  3. The crossing point must satisfy both: $(-2, 3)$.

    An intersection point lies on both graphs, so it obeys both equations.

A vertical line and a horizontal line meeting at $(-2, 3)$.

A circle meets a ray

Where does the polar curve $r = 4$ meet $\theta = 45^\circ$? Give the point in both systems.

  1. $r = 4$ is the circle of radius $4$ about the origin; $\theta = 45^\circ$ is the ray along the line $y = x$ in Quadrant I. They meet at the polar point $(4, 45^\circ)$.

    The meeting point has both properties at once.

  2. In Cartesian form, $x = y$ and $x^2 + y^2 = 16$, so $2x^2 = 16$ and $x = y = 2\sqrt2$.

    A $45^\circ$-$45^\circ$-$90^\circ$ triangle with hypotenuse $4$ has legs $4/\sqrt2 = 2\sqrt2$.

$(4, 45^\circ) = (2\sqrt2, 2\sqrt2)$

Sliding a point

Translate $(3, -1)$ by $5$ units left and $2$ units up. Then translate the result by $(4, -6)$.

  1. $5$ left, $2$ up is the translation $(a, b) = (-5, 2)$: $(3 - 5, -1 + 2) = (-2, 1)$.

    A translation just adds a fixed amount to each coordinate.

  2. $(-2 + 4, 1 - 6) = (2, -5)$.

    Two translations in a row add: $(-5, 2) + (4, -6) = (-1, -4)$, and indeed $(3, -1) + (-1, -4) = (2, -5)$.

$(-2, 1)$, then $(2, -5)$.

Turning a point

Rotate the polar point $(6, 25^\circ)$ counterclockwise by $70^\circ$ about the origin, then by another $300^\circ$.

  1. After $70^\circ$: $(6, 95^\circ)$.

    Rotation about the origin keeps the distance $r$ and adds to the angle.

  2. After $300^\circ$ more: $(6, 395^\circ) = (6, 35^\circ)$.

    Subtract $360^\circ$ to bring the angle back into the usual range; it is the same point.

$(6, 95^\circ)$, then $(6, 35^\circ)$.

Reflecting in both systems

The point $P$ has polar coordinates $(2, 30^\circ)$. Reflect it in the $y$-axis. Give the image in both systems.

  1. Cartesian form of $P$: $(2\cos 30^\circ, 2\sin 30^\circ) = (\sqrt3, 1)$.

    A $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle with hypotenuse $2$ has legs $1$ and $\sqrt3$.

  2. Reflect in the $y$-axis: $(-\sqrt3, 1)$.

    The mirror swaps left and right, so only $x$ changes sign.

  3. In polar form the angle becomes $180^\circ - 30^\circ = 150^\circ$: the image is $(2, 150^\circ)$.

    The image makes a $30^\circ$ angle with the negative $x$-axis, and the distance to the origin is unchanged.

$(-\sqrt3, 1) = (2, 150^\circ)$

Rotating a Cartesian point by $90^\circ$

Rotate $(3, 4)$ counterclockwise by $90^\circ$ about the origin.

  1. Let $\theta$ be the angle of $(3, 4)$. Then $r = 5$, $\cos\theta = \tfrac35$ and $\sin\theta = \tfrac45$.

    Rotations are easy in polar form, so convert first.

  2. Turn the triangle a quarter turn: the leg of length $3$ that pointed right now points up, and the leg of length $4$ that pointed up now points left. So the image is $(-4, 3)$.

    A rigid quarter turn carries the whole right triangle with it; leg lengths are preserved.

  3. Check: $\sqrt{(-4)^2 + 3^2} = 5$, and the slopes $\tfrac43$ and $-\tfrac34$ multiply to $-1$.

    The distance to the origin is unchanged and the two radii are perpendicular, as they should be.

$(-4, 3)$. In general, a $90^\circ$ turn sends $(x, y)$ to $(-y, x)$.

Regions in polar form

Find the area of the region of points satisfying $1 \le r \le 2$ and $0^\circ \le \theta \le 90^\circ$.

  1. $1 \le r \le 2$ alone is a ring between circles of radius $1$ and $2$, with area $4\pi - \pi = 3\pi$.

    Subtract the inner disc from the outer one.

  2. $0^\circ \le \theta \le 90^\circ$ keeps only the quarter of the ring in Quadrant I, so the area is $\tfrac14 \cdot 3\pi = \tfrac{3\pi}{4}$.

    The ring is symmetric, so a $90^\circ$ wedge holds exactly a quarter of it.

$\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$

Inequalities in $r$ give rings; inequalities in $\theta$ give wedges.

Two reflections make a rotation

Show that reflecting in the $x$-axis and then in the $y$-axis is the same as a $180^\circ$ rotation about the origin. Use it to find the image of $(-2, 7)$ and of the polar point $(3, 200^\circ)$.

  1. Cartesian: $(x, y) \to (x, -y) \to (-x, -y)$.

    Apply the two reflection rules one after the other.

  2. Polar: $(r, \theta) \to (r, -\theta) \to (r, 180^\circ - (-\theta)) = (r, \theta + 180^\circ)$, which is a $180^\circ$ rotation.

    The polar description makes the rotation visible: the angle grows by $180^\circ$ and the distance stays put.

  3. $(-2, 7) \to (2, -7)$, and $(3, 200^\circ) \to (3, 380^\circ) = (3, 20^\circ)$.

    Use whichever form the point is already given in.

$(2, -7)$ and $(3, 20^\circ)$.

Seeing a motion in the right coordinate system can reveal what it really is.

16.3 Straight and Narrow

Hook

A road sign reading "8% grade" means the road climbs $8$ metres for every $100$ metres you travel horizontally. That ratio, rise over run, is all you need to know about how steep a straight road is, and it is the heart of the equation of a line.

Concept

Where a line crosses the $y$-axis is its $y$-intercept $(0, b)$; where it crosses the $x$-axis is its $x$-intercept. To find an intercept, set the other variable equal to $0$.

The slope $m$ measures steepness: move right along a non-vertical line by any amount, and you must move up by $m$ times that amount to stay on it. Between two points,

$$m = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}.$$

Positive slope climbs, negative slope falls, zero slope is horizontal, and a vertical line has no slope (the run is $0$). A line with slope $m$ and $y$-intercept $b$ has equation $y = mx + b$, the slope-intercept form. The standard form is $Ax + By = C$ with integer coefficients and $A \ge 0$; solving for $y$ shows its slope is $-A/B$.

Parallel lines: equal slopes.   Perpendicular lines: $m_1 m_2 = -1$ (neither line vertical).

Why $-1$? If one line runs $a$ and rises $b$, turning it a quarter turn makes it run $-b$ and rise $a$. The slopes are $\frac{b}{a}$ and $-\frac{a}{b}$, whose product is $-1$.

Intercepts

Find the $x$- and $y$-intercepts of $3x + 5y = 30$.

  1. Set $y = 0$: $3x = 30$, so $x = 10$. The $x$-intercept is $(10, 0)$.

    Every point on the $x$-axis has $y = 0$.

  2. Set $x = 0$: $5y = 30$, so $y = 6$. The $y$-intercept is $(0, 6)$.

    Every point on the $y$-axis has $x = 0$.

$(10, 0)$ and $(0, 6)$

Slope from two points

Find the slope of the line through $(2, -1)$ and $(6, 7)$.

  1. Run: $6 - 2 = 4$. Rise: $7 - (-1) = 8$.

    Subtract in the same order for both coordinates.

  2. $m = \dfrac{8}{4} = 2$.

    Slope is rise divided by run.

$2$

Equation through two points

Find the equation of the line through $(-2, 5)$ and $(4, 2)$ in slope-intercept and standard form.

  1. $m = \dfrac{2 - 5}{4 - (-2)} = \dfrac{-3}{6} = -\dfrac12$.

    The line falls, so a negative slope is expected.

  2. $y = -\tfrac12 x + b$. Plug in $(4, 2)$: $2 = -2 + b$, so $b = 4$.

    Any point on the line must satisfy the equation; use it to find $b$.

  3. $y = -\tfrac12 x + 4$. Multiply by $2$ and rearrange: $x + 2y = 8$.

    Clearing fractions gives the standard form. Check $(-2, 5)$: $-2 + 10 = 8$. ✓

$y = -\tfrac12 x + 4$, or $x + 2y = 8$.

Reading a standard-form line

Find the slope and both intercepts of $4x - 6y = 12$.

  1. Solve for $y$: $-6y = -4x + 12$, so $y = \tfrac23 x - 2$.

    Slope-intercept form shows the slope and $y$-intercept directly. (Check with $-A/B = -4/(-6) = \tfrac23$.)

  2. Slope $\tfrac23$, $y$-intercept $(0, -2)$. For the $x$-intercept, $4x = 12$ gives $(3, 0)$.

    Set $y = 0$ in the original equation.

Slope $\tfrac23$; intercepts $(3, 0)$ and $(0, -2)$.

A parallel line

Find the line through $(3, 4)$ parallel to $2x + y = 7$.

  1. $2x + y = 7$ means $y = -2x + 7$: slope $-2$.

    Parallel lines share a slope.

  2. $y = -2x + b$ through $(3, 4)$: $4 = -6 + b$, so $b = 10$.

    Same slope, new intercept chosen to pass through the given point.

$y = -2x + 10$, or $2x + y = 10$.

A parallel line keeps $A$ and $B$ and changes only $C$.

A perpendicular line

Find the line through $(4, -1)$ perpendicular to $3x - 2y = 6$.

  1. The given line has slope $-A/B = -3/(-2) = \tfrac32$.

    Read the slope straight from the standard form.

  2. A perpendicular slope satisfies $\tfrac32 m = -1$, so $m = -\tfrac23$.

    Take the negative reciprocal.

  3. $y = -\tfrac23 x + b$ through $(4, -1)$: $-1 = -\tfrac83 + b$, so $b = \tfrac53$. Multiply by $3$: $2x + 3y = 5$.

    Check: $2(4) + 3(-1) = 5$. ✓

$2x + 3y = 5$

Triangle cut off by a line

Find the area of the triangle bounded by $5x + 2y = 20$ and the two coordinate axes.

  1. Intercepts: $y = 0$ gives $x = 4$; $x = 0$ gives $y = 10$.

    The triangle's vertices are the origin and the two intercepts.

  2. It is a right triangle with legs $4$ and $10$: area $\tfrac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 10 = 20$.

    The axes are perpendicular, so the legs lie along them.

$20$

Three points in a row

For what value of $t$ are $(1, 2)$, $(4, 8)$ and $(t, 15)$ on one line?

  1. Slope from $(1, 2)$ to $(4, 8)$: $\tfrac{6}{3} = 2$.

    Collinear points give the same slope between any two of them.

  2. Slope from $(1, 2)$ to $(t, 15)$ must also be $2$: $\dfrac{13}{t - 1} = 2$, so $t - 1 = 6.5$ and $t = 7.5$.

    Set the slopes equal and solve.

$t = \tfrac{15}{2}$

Tuning a coefficient

For what value of $a$ is the line $ax + 4y = 7$ (a) parallel to, (b) perpendicular to the line $3x - 2y = 1$?

  1. Slopes: $ax + 4y = 7$ has slope $-\tfrac{a}{4}$; $3x - 2y = 1$ has slope $\tfrac32$.

    Use $-A/B$ for each.

  2. (a) $-\tfrac{a}{4} = \tfrac32$ gives $a = -6$. The lines $-6x + 4y = 7$ and $3x - 2y = 1$ are then distinct, since doubling the second gives $-6x + 4y = -2$.

    Equal slopes give parallel lines, but check they are not the same line.

  3. (b) $\left(-\tfrac{a}{4}\right)\left(\tfrac32\right) = -1$, so $\tfrac{3a}{8} = 1$ and $a = \tfrac83$.

    Perpendicular slopes multiply to $-1$.

(a) $a = -6$; (b) $a = \tfrac83$.

In standard form, $A_1A_2 + B_1B_2 = 0$ exactly when the lines are perpendicular: here $3a - 8 = 0$.

16.4 Plotting a Line

Hook

A builder laying a straight fence only needs two posts; a string pulled tight between them shows where every other post goes. Plotting a line is the same: find two points you trust, and the rest of the line follows.

Concept

The universal method for any equation: plug in a few $x$ values, solve for the matching $y$ values, plot the points and connect them. For a line, two points suffice, and a third is a good check.

Choose $x$ values that make $y$ come out as an integer; for $Ax + By = C$ the intercepts are often the easiest pair. For $y = mx + b$ with $m = \frac{p}{q}$, you can walk the slope: start at $(0, b)$, then repeatedly go right $q$ and up $p$ (down if $p$ is negative), or left $q$ and down $p$.

Point-slope form: the line through $(x_1, y_1)$ with slope $m$ is $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$.

Walking the slope with $\frac{p}{q}$ in lowest terms lands on every grid point of the line, which is handy for counting lattice points.

A table of values

Find three points on $y = 2x - 3$ to plot it.

  1. $x = 0$: $y = -3$. $x = 1$: $y = -1$. $x = 2$: $y = 1$.

    Small $x$ values keep the arithmetic easy.

  2. Plot $(0, -3)$, $(1, -1)$, $(2, 1)$ and draw the line through them.

    Two points determine the line; the third confirms there was no slip.

$(0, -3)$, $(1, -1)$, $(2, 1)$

Walking the slope

Plot $y = -\tfrac32 x + 4$ by walking the slope. List four points.

  1. Start at the $y$-intercept $(0, 4)$.

    The $b$ in $y = mx + b$ tells you where to begin.

  2. Slope $-\tfrac32$: right $2$, down $3$ gives $(2, 1)$, then $(4, -2)$. Left $2$, up $3$ gives $(-2, 7)$.

    The denominator is the run and the numerator is the rise; reverse both to walk backward.

$(-2, 7)$, $(0, 4)$, $(2, 1)$, $(4, -2)$

Plot by intercepts

Plot $3x - 4y = 12$.

  1. $y = 0$: $x = 4$. $x = 0$: $y = -3$. Plot $(4, 0)$ and $(0, -3)$.

    In standard form the intercepts are quick to compute.

  2. Check with a third point: $x = 8$ gives $-4y = -12$, so $y = 3$, and $(8, 3)$ lies on the same line.

    From $(4, 0)$ to $(8, 3)$ is run $4$, rise $3$, matching the slope $\frac34$.

The line through $(0, -3)$, $(4, 0)$ and $(8, 3)$.

Choosing friendly $x$ values

Find three points with integer coordinates on $2x + 5y = 7$.

  1. $y = \dfrac{7 - 2x}{5}$ is an integer when $7 - 2x$ is a multiple of $5$. Try $x = 1$: $y = 1$.

    Choosing $x$ cleverly avoids fractions.

  2. The slope is $-\tfrac25$, so walking right $5$, down $2$ gives $(6, -1)$; walking left $5$, up $2$ gives $(-4, 3)$.

    Once one lattice point is known, the slope in lowest terms gives the rest.

$(-4, 3)$, $(1, 1)$, $(6, -1)$

Point-slope form

Plot $y - 1 = -2(x + 3)$ and write it in slope-intercept form.

  1. This is point-slope form with point $(-3, 1)$ and slope $-2$.

    Compare with $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$: here $x_1 = -3$ because $x + 3 = x - (-3)$.

  2. Walk: right $1$, down $2$: $(-2, -1)$, $(-1, -3)$.

    Slope $-2 = \frac{-2}{1}$.

  3. Expand: $y = -2x - 6 + 1 = -2x - 5$.

    Check: $x = -2$ gives $y = -1$. ✓

$y = -2x - 5$, through $(-3, 1)$, $(-2, -1)$, $(-1, -3)$.

Is the point on the line?

A line passes through $(2, 1)$ with slope $\tfrac52$. Does it pass through $(14, 31)$? Through $(9, 18)$?

  1. From $(2, 1)$ to $(14, 31)$: run $12 = 6 \times 2$, so the rise must be $6 \times 5 = 30$, and $1 + 30 = 31$. Yes.

    Six slope steps of "right 2, up 5" cover the run of 12.

  2. From $(2, 1)$ to $(9, 18)$: run $7$, rise $17$, and $\tfrac{17}{7} \ne \tfrac52$. No.

    A point is on the line exactly when its slope to a known point equals the line's slope.

$(14, 31)$ is on the line; $(9, 18)$ is not.

Counting lattice points

How many points with integer coordinates lie on the segment from $(-3, 7)$ to $(9, -2)$, including the endpoints?

  1. Run $12$, rise $-9$: slope $-\tfrac{9}{12} = -\tfrac34$.

    Reduce to lowest terms; that gives the smallest grid step along the line.

  2. Walking "right $4$, down $3$" from $(-3, 7)$: $(1, 4)$, $(5, 1)$, $(9, -2)$.

    Any lattice step $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$ along the line has $\Delta y / \Delta x = -3/4$, so $\Delta x$ must be a multiple of $4$.

  3. Count: $(-3, 7)$, $(1, 4)$, $(5, 1)$, $(9, -2)$.

    In general there are $\gcd(|\text{run}|, |\text{rise}|) + 1 = \gcd(12, 9) + 1$ of them.

$4$ points

A slope in lowest terms is the smallest grid step along a line.

16.5 The Distance Formula and Circles

Hook

A city laid out on a grid has streets going east-west and avenues north-south. To get from one corner to another you walk along both, but a crow flies straight across. How far does the crow fly? The streets and the crow's path make a right triangle, and Pythagoras answers the question.

Concept

From $(x_1, y_1)$ to $(x_2, y_2)$ you go across $x_2 - x_1$ and up $y_2 - y_1$. These are the legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the distance $d$:

$$d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}.$$

A circle is the set of points at distance $R$ from a center $(p, q)$. Square the distance formula to get the standard equation of a circle:

$$(x - p)^2 + (y - q)^2 = R^2.$$

Expanding gives the general form $x^2 + y^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$, which hides the center and radius. To recover them, make the coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ equal to $1$, then complete the square in $x$ and in $y$ separately (as in Chapter 6).

If completing the square leaves $0$ on the right, the "circle" is a single point; if it leaves a negative number, no points satisfy the equation at all.

A distance

Find the distance between $(1, 2)$ and $(7, 10)$.

  1. Legs: $7 - 1 = 6$ and $10 - 2 = 8$.

    The horizontal and vertical separations form a right triangle.

  2. $d = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} = 10$.

    It is a $6$-$8$-$10$ triangle, a multiple of $3$-$4$-$5$.

$10$

Writing a circle

Write the equation of the circle with center $(3, -2)$ and radius $5$. Is $(6, 2)$ on it?

  1. $(x - 3)^2 + (y - (-2))^2 = 25$, that is, $(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25$.

    Substitute $p = 3$, $q = -2$, $R = 5$. Watch the sign: $y - (-2) = y + 2$.

  2. $(6 - 3)^2 + (2 + 2)^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$. Yes.

    A point is on the circle exactly when it satisfies the equation.

$(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25$; yes, $(6, 2)$ is on it.

A missing coordinate

Find all $y$ such that $(4, y)$ is $13$ units from $(-1, 2)$.

  1. $(4 - (-1))^2 + (y - 2)^2 = 13^2$, so $25 + (y - 2)^2 = 169$.

    Square the distance formula to avoid the square root.

  2. $(y - 2)^2 = 144$, so $y - 2 = \pm 12$ and $y = 14$ or $y = -10$.

    Both signs count: one point above and one below.

$y = 14$ or $y = -10$

Completing two squares

Find the center and radius of $x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 10y + 9 = 0$.

  1. Group: $(x^2 - 6x) + (y^2 + 10y) = -9$.

    Collect the $x$ terms and the $y$ terms, and move the constant across.

  2. Add $9$ and $25$ to both sides: $(x - 3)^2 + (y + 5)^2 = -9 + 9 + 25 = 25$.

    Half of $-6$ is $-3$, squared is $9$; half of $10$ is $5$, squared is $25$.

Center $(3, -5)$, radius $5$.

Circle on a diameter

The segment from $(-2, 1)$ to $(6, 7)$ is a diameter of a circle. Find its equation.

  1. The center is halfway between the endpoints: $\left(\frac{-2 + 6}{2}, \frac{1 + 7}{2}\right) = (2, 4)$.

    The center of a circle bisects every diameter; halfway means averaging the coordinates (proved in 16.7).

  2. Radius: distance from $(2, 4)$ to $(6, 7)$ is $\sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$.

    Any endpoint of the diameter is on the circle.

$(x - 2)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 25$

Divide first

Find the center and radius of $3x^2 + 3y^2 + 12x - 18y - 9 = 0$.

  1. Divide by $3$: $x^2 + y^2 + 4x - 6y - 3 = 0$.

    Completing the square is simplest when $x^2$ and $y^2$ have coefficient $1$.

  2. $(x^2 + 4x + 4) + (y^2 - 6y + 9) = 3 + 4 + 9$, so $(x + 2)^2 + (y - 3)^2 = 16$.

    Add the same amounts to both sides.

Center $(-2, 3)$, radius $4$.

Equidistant from three points

Find the point equidistant from $(0, 0)$, $(8, 0)$ and $(0, 6)$, and that common distance.

  1. Let the point be $(x, y)$. Equal distance to $(0, 0)$ and $(8, 0)$: $x^2 + y^2 = (x - 8)^2 + y^2$, so $0 = -16x + 64$ and $x = 4$.

    Squared distances are equal; the $x^2$ and $y^2$ terms cancel, leaving a linear equation.

  2. Equal distance to $(0, 0)$ and $(0, 6)$ gives $y = 3$ in the same way.

    The point must lie on the perpendicular bisector of each side.

  3. Distance from $(4, 3)$ to the origin: $\sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$.

    This is the center and radius of the circle through all three points.

$(4, 3)$, at distance $5$.

Setting squared distances equal cancels the squares and leaves a line.

When is it really a circle?

For which $k$ does $x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 2y + k = 0$ describe a circle? For which $k$ is the radius $3$?

  1. Complete the squares: $(x - 2)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = 4 + 1 - k = 5 - k$.

    Now the right side plays the role of $R^2$.

  2. A circle needs $5 - k > 0$, so $k < 5$. At $k = 5$ only the point $(2, -1)$ works, and for $k > 5$ nothing does.

    A sum of squares is never negative and equals $0$ only when both squares are $0$.

  3. Radius $3$: $5 - k = 9$, so $k = -4$.

    Set $R^2 = 9$.

A circle for $k < 5$; radius $3$ when $k = -4$.

16.6 Went Down to the Crossroads

Hook

Two roads drawn on a map cross somewhere. You could squint at the map, or you could notice that the crossing point lies on both roads, so its coordinates satisfy both equations. Finding where figures meet is just solving a system of equations.

Concept

To intersect two figures, write both equations and solve them simultaneously. For two lines, use substitution or elimination (Chapter 3). For a line and a circle, solve the line for one variable and substitute into the circle; that gives a quadratic.

Two distinct lines: $0$ points (parallel) or $1$ point.
Line and circle: the quadratic has $2$, $1$ or $0$ real roots: the line is a secant, a tangent, or misses.
Two circles: subtract the equations to get a line, then intersect that line with either circle.

A quick geometric check for a line and a circle: compare the distance from the center to the line with the radius. Smaller means two points, equal means tangent, larger means none.

Two lines by substitution

Where do $y = 2x + 1$ and $y = -x + 7$ meet?

  1. Set the $y$'s equal: $2x + 1 = -x + 7$, so $3x = 6$ and $x = 2$.

    At the crossing, both equations give the same $y$.

  2. $y = 2(2) + 1 = 5$.

    Check in the other: $-2 + 7 = 5$. ✓

$(2, 5)$

Two lines by elimination

Intersect $3x + 2y = 4$ and $x - 4y = 6$.

  1. Double the first: $6x + 4y = 8$. Add the second: $7x = 14$, so $x = 2$.

    Make the $y$ coefficients opposite so they cancel.

  2. $2 - 4y = 6$, so $y = -1$.

    Substitute back. Check: $6 - 2 = 4$. ✓

$(2, -1)$

Lines that never meet

Intersect $2x - 3y = 4$ and $4x - 6y = 1$.

  1. Double the first: $4x - 6y = 8$. But the second says $4x - 6y = 1$.

    The same expression cannot equal both $8$ and $1$.

  2. No solution: both lines have slope $\tfrac23$ and different intercepts, so they are parallel.

    An impossible system is the algebra's way of saying "parallel".

No intersection; the lines are parallel.

A line through a circle

Find where $y = x + 1$ meets $x^2 + y^2 = 25$.

  1. Substitute: $x^2 + (x + 1)^2 = 25$, so $2x^2 + 2x - 24 = 0$, that is, $x^2 + x - 12 = 0$.

    Replace $y$ using the line's equation to get one equation in $x$.

  2. $(x + 4)(x - 3) = 0$, so $x = 3$ or $x = -4$, giving $y = 4$ or $y = -3$.

    Use the line (not the circle) to find each $y$; the circle would also allow wrong signs.

$(3, 4)$ and $(-4, -3)$

Tangent lines

For which values of $b$ is the line $y = x + b$ tangent to $x^2 + y^2 = 8$?

  1. Substitute: $x^2 + (x + b)^2 = 8$, so $2x^2 + 2bx + b^2 - 8 = 0$.

    Tangent means exactly one intersection point.

  2. The discriminant must be $0$: $(2b)^2 - 4 \cdot 2 \cdot (b^2 - 8) = 64 - 4b^2 = 0$, so $b = \pm 4$.

    A double root means the line touches the circle at one point.

  3. Check geometrically: the line $y = x + 4$ crosses the axes at $(-4, 0)$ and $(0, 4)$. The distance from the origin to it is half the diagonal of that $4 \times 4$ corner square, $2\sqrt2 = \sqrt8$, the radius. ✓

    Tangent lines sit exactly one radius from the center.

$b = 4$ or $b = -4$

Two circles

Find the intersections of $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ and $(x - 6)^2 + y^2 = 13$.

  1. Expand the second: $x^2 - 12x + 36 + y^2 = 13$. Subtract it from the first: $12x - 36 = 12$, so $x = 4$.

    Subtracting cancels $x^2$ and $y^2$, leaving a line that contains both intersection points.

  2. $16 + y^2 = 25$, so $y = \pm 3$.

    Put $x = 4$ into either circle. Check the other: $(4 - 6)^2 + 9 = 13$. ✓

$(4, 3)$ and $(4, -3)$

Subtracting two circle equations gives the line through their crossing points.

Length of a chord

The line $x + 2y = 5$ cuts the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ in a chord. How long is the chord?

  1. Substitute $x = 5 - 2y$: $(5 - 2y)^2 + y^2 = 25$, so $5y^2 - 20y = 0$ and $y = 0$ or $y = 4$.

    Solving for $x$ avoids fractions here.

  2. The endpoints are $(5, 0)$ and $(-3, 4)$. Chord length: $\sqrt{8^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{80} = 4\sqrt5$.

    Use the line to get each $x$, then the distance formula.

  3. Check another way: the line crosses the axes at $(5, 0)$ and $(0, \tfrac52)$, and the distance from the origin to it is $\sqrt5$. Half the chord is $\sqrt{25 - 5} = 2\sqrt5$.

    The perpendicular from the center bisects a chord, making a right triangle with the radius as hypotenuse. (The distance $\sqrt5$ comes from area: $\tfrac12 \cdot 5 \cdot \tfrac52 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{5\sqrt5}{2} \cdot h$.)

$4\sqrt5$

16.7 ... Fell Down on My Knees

Hook

Coordinates are a power tool. Used well, they prove theorems in a few lines of algebra; used carelessly, they bury a two-line geometric idea under a page of symbols. The trick is to place the axes cleverly, so the algebra stays small.

Concept

Doing geometry with equations is called analytic geometry. Two workhorse formulas:

Midpoint of $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$: $\left(\dfrac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \dfrac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$.
Centroid of a triangle (where the three medians meet): $\left(\dfrac{x_1 + x_2 + x_3}{3}, \dfrac{y_1 + y_2 + y_3}{3}\right)$.

The midpoint formula follows from two congruent right triangles: halfway along the segment you have gone half the run and half the rise. More generally, the point a fraction $t$ of the way from $P$ to $Q$ is $P + t(Q - P)$, coordinate by coordinate.

For a proof about a figure (not about specific coordinates), you may choose the axes. Put a vertex at the origin, a side along an axis, use symmetry, and use $2a$ instead of $a$ when you expect to take midpoints. Every triangle can be placed with vertices $(a, 0)$, $(b, 0)$, $(0, c)$.

When in doubt, try a geometric approach first. Use coordinates when the figure has right angles, midpoints, or a natural place for the axes.

A midpoint

Find the midpoint of the segment from $(-3, 8)$ to $(7, 2)$.

  1. $\left(\frac{-3 + 7}{2}, \frac{8 + 2}{2}\right) = (2, 5)$.

    Average the $x$'s and average the $y$'s.

$(2, 5)$

The missing endpoint

The midpoint of $\overline{PQ}$ is $(1, 4)$ and $P = (-2, 9)$. Find $Q$.

  1. From $P$ to the midpoint is a move of $(+3, -5)$.

    The midpoint is halfway, so the same move again reaches $Q$.

  2. $Q = (1 + 3, 4 - 5) = (4, -1)$.

    Check: $\left(\frac{-2 + 4}{2}, \frac{9 - 1}{2}\right) = (1, 4)$. ✓

$(4, -1)$

A centroid

Find the centroid of the triangle with vertices $(1, 2)$, $(7, -4)$, $(4, 11)$.

  1. $\left(\frac{1 + 7 + 4}{3}, \frac{2 - 4 + 11}{3}\right) = (4, 3)$.

    The centroid is the average of the three vertices.

$(4, 3)$

Completing a parallelogram

$ABCD$ is a parallelogram with $A = (1, 1)$, $B = (5, 2)$, $C = (7, 6)$. Find $D$.

  1. The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, so $\overline{AC}$ and $\overline{BD}$ share a midpoint.

    Equal midpoints means $A + C = B + D$ coordinate by coordinate.

  2. $D = A + C - B = (1 + 7 - 5, 1 + 6 - 2) = (3, 5)$.

    Check: $\overrightarrow{AB} = (4, 1)$ and $\overrightarrow{DC} = (4, 1)$ match. ✓

$(3, 5)$

A third of the way

Find the point one third of the way from $(-4, 1)$ to $(8, 10)$.

  1. The whole move is $(12, 9)$; a third of it is $(4, 3)$.

    Along a straight segment, run and rise scale together.

  2. $(-4 + 4, 1 + 3) = (0, 4)$.

    Start at the first point and add the fraction of the move.

$(0, 4)$

The midpoint of the hypotenuse

Prove that the midpoint of the hypotenuse of a right triangle is the same distance from all three vertices.

  1. Place the right angle at the origin with legs on the axes: vertices $(0, 0)$, $(2a, 0)$, $(0, 2b)$.

    The right angle fits the axes perfectly, and using $2a$, $2b$ keeps the midpoint free of fractions.

  2. Midpoint of the hypotenuse: $M = (a, b)$. Its distance to $(2a, 0)$ and to $(0, 2b)$ is half the hypotenuse, $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$.

    $M$ is the midpoint, so it is halfway along a segment of length $\sqrt{4a^2 + 4b^2}$.

  3. Distance to the origin: $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, the same.

    All three distances agree, so $M$ is the center of the circle through the vertices.

All three distances equal $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$.

The midsegment theorem

Prove that the segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half as long.

  1. Place $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (2b, 0)$, $C = (2c, 2d)$.

    Side $AB$ along the $x$-axis makes "parallel to $AB$" mean "horizontal".

  2. Midpoint of $AC$: $(c, d)$. Midpoint of $BC$: $(b + c, d)$.

    Average the endpoints.

  3. Both have $y$-coordinate $d$, so the segment is horizontal, parallel to $AB$. Its length is $(b + c) - c = b$, half of $AB = 2b$.

    Equal $y$-coordinates mean slope $0$.

Parallel to $AB$, with length $\tfrac12 AB$.

The length of a median

In $\triangle ABC$, $M$ is the midpoint of $BC$. Prove $AB^2 + AC^2 = 2AM^2 + 2BM^2$. Then find the median $AM$ when $AB = 7$, $AC = 9$, $BC = 8$.

  1. Put $M$ at the origin and $BC$ on the $x$-axis: $B = (-a, 0)$, $C = (a, 0)$, $A = (p, q)$.

    Symmetry about $M$ makes the algebra cancel neatly.

  2. $AB^2 + AC^2 = (p + a)^2 + q^2 + (p - a)^2 + q^2 = 2p^2 + 2q^2 + 2a^2$.

    The cross terms $\pm 2pa$ cancel.

  3. And $2AM^2 + 2BM^2 = 2(p^2 + q^2) + 2a^2$, the same. ∎

    $AM^2 = p^2 + q^2$ and $BM = a$.

  4. Numbers: $49 + 81 = 2AM^2 + 2 \cdot 16$, so $AM^2 = 49$ and $AM = 7$.

    $BM = 4$ is half of $BC$.

$AM = 7$

Put the origin at the center of symmetry; opposite terms cancel.
