Chapter 6 · Algebra & Number

Quadratic Equations

Linear equations describe things moving at a steady rate. As soon as something speeds up, curves, or multiplies by itself, a square appears, and with it the quadratic equation. In this chapter you'll factor quadratics, derive and use the quadratic formula, untangle equations in disguise, take square roots of irrational and complex numbers, and peek at polynomials of higher degree.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • A quadratic is $ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a \ne 0$. If it factors as $a(x - r)(x - s)$, the solutions of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ are $r$ and $s$, because a product is $0$ only when a factor is $0$.
  • To factor $x^2 + bx + c$, find two numbers with product $c$ and sum $b$. Clear fractions first, and watch for $x(ax + b)$, $x^2 - k^2$ and perfect squares.
  • Completing the square turns any quadratic into $(x + m)^2 = k$, which gives the formula $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$.
  • The discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ decides the roots: positive means two real roots, zero means one repeated root, negative means two complex conjugate roots.
  • For $ax^2 + bx + c$, the roots have sum $-\dfrac{b}{a}$ and product $\dfrac{c}{a}$. With rational coefficients, irrational roots come in pairs $p \pm q\sqrt d$.
  • Clear denominators or square away radicals to reach a quadratic, then check for extraneous roots. Substitute $u$ for a repeated expression such as $x^2$ or $2^x$.
  • To find $\sqrt{a + b\sqrt c}$ or $\sqrt{a + bi}$, assume the form $x + y\sqrt c$ or $x + yi$, square, and match parts.

6.1 What's a Quadratic?

Hook

Toss a ball upward at $20$ meters per second from a $25$-meter rooftop. Gravity steals about $5t^2$ meters by time $t$, so its height is $h = 25 + 20t - 5t^2$. When does it hit the ground? You need $25 + 20t - 5t^2 = 0$, and no amount of linear-equation tricks will isolate $t$: there's a $t$ and a $t^2$ fighting each other.

Concept

A quadratic expression in $x$ has the form $ax^2 + bx + c$, where the coefficients $a, b, c$ are constants and $a \ne 0$. Its degree is $2$, the highest power of $x$. A quadratic equation is one that can be rearranged into

$$ax^2 + bx + c = 0, \qquad a \neq 0.$$

The solutions are called the roots (or zeros) of the quadratic. On a graph, $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ is a parabola, and the roots are where it crosses the $x$-axis. A parabola can cross twice, touch once, or miss entirely, which is why a quadratic can have two, one, or no real roots.

Before solving anything, it helps to recognize a quadratic in disguise: move every term to one side, expand any products, and collect like terms. If an $x^2$ survives, it's quadratic; if the $x^2$ terms cancel, it was linear all along.

Spot the quadratics

Which of these are quadratic equations? (a) $3x^2 - 5 = 0$ (b) $x(x + 2) = x^2 + 6$ (c) $(x - 1)^2 = 4x$ (d) $x^3 = x^2$

  1. (a) is already $3x^2 + 0x - 5 = 0$: quadratic.

    $b = 0$ is allowed; only $a$ must be nonzero.

  2. (b) expands to $x^2 + 2x = x^2 + 6$, so $2x = 6$: linear, not quadratic.

    The $x^2$ terms cancel. Always simplify before deciding.

  3. (c) expands to $x^2 - 2x + 1 = 4x$, so $x^2 - 6x + 1 = 0$: quadratic.

    Expand the square and collect terms on one side.

  4. (d) has an $x^3$ term: degree $3$, a cubic.

    The highest power decides the degree.

(a) and (c) are quadratic; (b) is linear; (d) is cubic.

Name the coefficients

Write $5 - 2x^2 = 3x$ in the form $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a > 0$, and name $a$, $b$, $c$.

  1. Move everything to one side: $-2x^2 - 3x + 5 = 0$.

    Standard form has $0$ on one side.

  2. Multiply by $-1$: $2x^2 + 3x - 5 = 0$.

    Multiplying an equation by a nonzero number doesn't change its solutions, and a positive $a$ is easier to work with.

$2x^2 + 3x - 5 = 0$, so $a = 2$, $b = 3$, $c = -5$.

Check candidate roots

Which of $x = 3$, $x = 1$, $x = -1$ are roots of $x^2 - 2x - 3 = 0$?

  1. $x = 3$: $9 - 6 - 3 = 0$. ✓

    A root is a value that makes the expression equal $0$.

  2. $x = 1$: $1 - 2 - 3 = -4 \ne 0$. ✗

    Substitution is the definitive test.

  3. $x = -1$: $1 + 2 - 3 = 0$. ✓

    Careful with signs: $(-1)^2 = 1$ and $-2(-1) = 2$.

$3$ and $-1$ are roots; $1$ is not.

Rearrange into standard form

Write $(2x + 1)(x - 3) = x + 5$ in standard form with the smallest possible integer coefficients.

  1. Expand: $(2x + 1)(x - 3) = 2x^2 - 6x + x - 3 = 2x^2 - 5x - 3$.

    Distribute each term of the first factor over the second.

  2. $2x^2 - 5x - 3 - x - 5 = 0$, so $2x^2 - 6x - 8 = 0$.

    Move everything to the left and combine like terms.

  3. Divide by $2$: $x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0$.

    A common factor can be divided out of an equation.

$x^2 - 3x - 4 = 0$

The rooftop ball

A ball's height in meters after $t$ seconds is $h = 25 + 20t - 5t^2$. Show that it lands at $t = 5$, and explain why the equation also has the root $t = -1$.

  1. Landing means $h = 0$: $-5t^2 + 20t + 25 = 0$, or dividing by $-5$, $t^2 - 4t - 5 = 0$.

    Set the height to zero, then simplify.

  2. $t = 5$: $25 - 20 - 5 = 0$ ✓. $t = -1$: $1 + 4 - 5 = 0$ ✓.

    Both satisfy the equation.

  3. The formula only describes the ball after it's thrown, $t \ge 0$, so $t = -1$ is rejected.

    The parabola extends backward in time as if the ball had been launched from the ground a second earlier; the math is fine, but the story isn't.

It lands at $t = 5$ seconds; $t = -1$ is a valid root of the equation but not a physical answer.

Set up from a rectangle

A rectangle has perimeter $20$ and area $21$. Write a quadratic equation for its width $x$, and check that $x = 3$ and $x = 7$ satisfy it.

  1. Width plus length is half the perimeter, so the length is $10 - x$.

    $2x + 2\ell = 20$ gives $\ell = 10 - x$.

  2. Area: $x(10 - x) = 21$, so $x^2 - 10x + 21 = 0$.

    Expand and move everything to one side.

  3. $x = 3$: $9 - 30 + 21 = 0$ ✓. $x = 7$: $49 - 70 + 21 = 0$ ✓.

    The two roots are the width and length; swapping them describes the same rectangle.

$x^2 - 10x + 21 = 0$; the rectangle is $3$ by $7$.

A known root reveals a coefficient

The equation $x^2 + kx - 10 = 0$ has $x = 2$ as a root. Find $k$ and the other root.

  1. Substitute $x = 2$: $4 + 2k - 10 = 0$, so $k = 3$.

    A root must satisfy the equation, which gives an equation for $k$.

  2. $x^2 + 3x - 10 = (x - 2)(x + 5)$.

    The factor $(x - 2)$ must appear; the other factor's constant must multiply with $-2$ to give $-10$.

  3. The other root is $x = -5$. Check: $25 - 15 - 10 = 0$. ✓

    Section 6.2 develops this factoring idea in full.

$k = 3$; the other root is $-5$.

A root plugged into the equation gives you information; a root also gives you a factor.

6.2 Factoring Quadratics

Hook

If two numbers multiply to $0$, one of them must be $0$. That tiny fact is a superpower: if you can rewrite $x^2 + 5x + 6$ as $(x + 2)(x + 3)$, then solving $x^2 + 5x + 6 = 0$ becomes two baby equations, $x + 2 = 0$ or $x + 3 = 0$.

Concept

Multiplying out two linear factors shows where the coefficients come from:

$$(x + u)(x + v) = x^2 + (u + v)x + uv.$$

So to factor $x^2 + bx + c$, look for two numbers $u, v$ with product $c$ and sum $b$. Signs help: if $c > 0$ the numbers share a sign (the sign of $b$); if $c < 0$ they have opposite signs. Then the roots are $-u$ and $-v$.

When the leading coefficient isn't $1$, first factor out any common factor (and make $a$ positive), then look for $(sx + u)(tx + v)$ with $st = a$, $uv = c$ and $sv + tu = b$. A handy shortcut: find two numbers with product $ac$ and sum $b$, split the middle term with them, and factor by grouping. If there are fractions, multiply through to clear them first.

Three patterns are worth recognizing on sight:

$$ax^2 + bx = x(ax + b), \qquad x^2 - k^2 = (x - k)(x + k), \qquad x^2 + 2kx + k^2 = (x + k)^2.$$

Never divide both sides by $x$ to "simplify" $ax^2 = bx$: you'd throw away the root $x = 0$. Factor instead.

Multiply two binomials

Expand $(x + 4)(x - 7)$.

  1. $x \cdot x + x\cdot(-7) + 4 \cdot x + 4 \cdot (-7) = x^2 - 7x + 4x - 28$.

    Every term of one factor multiplies every term of the other.

  2. $x^2 - 3x - 28$.

    The middle coefficient is $4 + (-7)$ and the constant is $4 \cdot (-7)$, just as the pattern predicts.

$x^2 - 3x - 28$

Product and sum, both positive

Solve $x^2 + 7x + 12 = 0$.

  1. Need product $12$ and sum $7$: pairs $1 \cdot 12$, $2 \cdot 6$, $3 \cdot 4$. The pair $3, 4$ works.

    Both positive since the product and sum are positive.

  2. $(x + 3)(x + 4) = 0$, so $x = -3$ or $x = -4$.

    Set each factor to zero. The roots are the negatives of $u$ and $v$.

$x = -3$ or $x = -4$

Opposite signs

Solve $x^2 - 2x - 35 = 0$.

  1. Product $-35$, so opposite signs; sum $-2$, so the negative one is bigger in size.

    Reading signs first narrows the search.

  2. $5 \cdot 7 = 35$ and $5 - 7 = -2$: use $u = 5$, $v = -7$.

    The sizes must differ by $2$.

  3. $(x + 5)(x - 7) = 0$, so $x = -5$ or $x = 7$.

    Check $x = 7$: $49 - 14 - 35 = 0$. ✓

$x = 7$ or $x = -5$

Two special forms

Solve $9x^2 - 16 = 0$ and $2x^2 = 6x$.

  1. $9x^2 - 16 = (3x)^2 - 4^2 = (3x - 4)(3x + 4)$, so $x = \pm\dfrac{4}{3}$.

    A difference of squares. $9x^2$ is the square of $3x$.

  2. $2x^2 - 6x = 0 \Rightarrow 2x(x - 3) = 0$, so $x = 0$ or $x = 3$.

    Move everything to one side and factor out $2x$. Dividing by $x$ would have lost $x = 0$.

$x = \pm\dfrac{4}{3}$; and $x = 0$ or $x = 3$.

Perfect squares

Solve $x^2 - 10x + 25 = 0$ and $4x^2 + 12x + 9 = 0$.

  1. $x^2 - 10x + 25 = (x - 5)^2$, so $x = 5$.

    $25 = 5^2$ and $-10 = 2 \cdot (-5)$: the pattern $x^2 + 2kx + k^2$ with $k = -5$.

  2. $4x^2 + 12x + 9 = (2x + 3)^2$, so $x = -\dfrac{3}{2}$.

    First and last terms are squares of $2x$ and $3$, and the middle is $2 \cdot 2x \cdot 3$.

$x = 5$ (a double root); $x = -\dfrac32$ (a double root).

Leading coefficient not 1

Solve $6x^2 + x - 2 = 0$.

  1. Product $ac = -12$ and sum $1$: the numbers $4$ and $-3$.

    These tell us how to split the middle term.

  2. $6x^2 + 4x - 3x - 2 = 2x(3x + 2) - 1(3x + 2) = (2x - 1)(3x + 2)$.

    Group in pairs and pull out the common binomial.

  3. $x = \dfrac12$ or $x = -\dfrac23$.

    Set each factor to zero.

$x = \dfrac{1}{2}$ or $x = -\dfrac{2}{3}$

Clear the fractions first

Solve $\dfrac{x^2}{2} + \dfrac{x}{6} - \dfrac{1}{3} = 0$.

  1. Multiply by $6$: $3x^2 + x - 2 = 0$.

    $6$ is the LCM of the denominators. Integer coefficients are far easier to factor.

  2. $ac = -6$, sum $1$: use $3$ and $-2$. $3x^2 + 3x - 2x - 2 = (3x - 2)(x + 1)$.

    Split the middle term and group.

  3. $x = \dfrac23$ or $x = -1$.

    Check $x = -1$: $\tfrac12 - \tfrac16 - \tfrac13 = 0$. ✓

$x = \dfrac{2}{3}$ or $x = -1$

Build a quadratic from its roots

Find a quadratic with integer coefficients whose roots are $\dfrac{3}{2}$ and $-4$.

  1. Root $\dfrac32$ comes from the factor $2x - 3$; root $-4$ from $x + 4$.

    $2x - 3 = 0$ exactly when $x = \tfrac32$, and this factor keeps the coefficients whole.

  2. $(2x - 3)(x + 4) = 2x^2 + 8x - 3x - 12 = 2x^2 + 5x - 12$.

    Expand.

$2x^2 + 5x - 12 = 0$ (or any nonzero multiple of it)

A tough split

Solve $12x^2 - x - 35 = 0$.

  1. $ac = -420$ and sum $-1$: since $20 \cdot 21 = 420$, use $20$ and $-21$.

    Numbers with sum $-1$ are consecutive in size, so look near $\sqrt{420} \approx 20.5$.

  2. $12x^2 + 20x - 21x - 35 = 4x(3x + 5) - 7(3x + 5) = (4x - 7)(3x + 5)$.

    Group and factor out the common binomial.

  3. $x = \dfrac74$ or $x = -\dfrac53$.

    Check by expanding: $12x^2 + 20x - 21x - 35$. ✓

$x = \dfrac{7}{4}$ or $x = -\dfrac{5}{3}$

For $ax^2 + bx + c$, find numbers with product $ac$ and sum $b$, then split and group.

Which middle coefficients work?

For how many integers $b$ does $x^2 + bx + 12$ factor as $(x + u)(x + v)$ with integers $u, v$?

  1. We need $uv = 12$, and then $b = u + v$.

    Every factorization comes from a pair with product $12$.

  2. Positive pairs: $(1, 12), (2, 6), (3, 4)$ with sums $13, 8, 7$.

    Order doesn't matter, since swapping gives the same sum.

  3. Negative pairs $(-1, -12), (-2, -6), (-3, -4)$ give sums $-13, -8, -7$.

    Two negatives also multiply to $+12$.

$6$ values: $b = \pm 7, \pm 8, \pm 13$

6.3 The Quadratic Formula

Hook

Try to factor $x^2 + 2x - 1$. Product $-1$, sum $2$... nothing works, because the roots are $-1 \pm \sqrt2$. Factoring is fast when it works, but we need a method that always works. The trick is to make the quadratic into a perfect square, since square roots undo squares.

Concept

Completing the square. Because $(x + m)^2 = x^2 + 2mx + m^2$, the expression $x^2 + bx$ becomes a perfect square when you add $\left(\tfrac{b}{2}\right)^2$: half the $x$-coefficient, squared. To solve $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$:

$$x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a} \;\Longrightarrow\; \left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.$$

That last expression is the quadratic formula. The $\pm$ gives the two roots. The quantity under the root, the discriminant $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$, tells you what kind of roots to expect when $a, b, c$ are real:

$\Delta > 0$: two different real roots. $\quad\Delta = 0$: one repeated real root $-\dfrac{b}{2a}$. $\quad\Delta < 0$: two complex roots, which are conjugates $p \pm qi$.

If $a, b, c$ are rational and $\Delta$ is a perfect square, the roots are rational and the quadratic factors nicely. If the coefficients are rational and $p + q\sqrt d$ is a root, then $p - q\sqrt d$ is the other. Adding and multiplying the formula's two roots gives

$$r + s = -\frac{b}{a}, \qquad rs = \frac{c}{a}.$$

Complete the square

Solve $x^2 + 6x - 7 = 0$ by completing the square.

  1. $x^2 + 6x = 7$. Add $\left(\tfrac62\right)^2 = 9$ to both sides: $x^2 + 6x + 9 = 16$.

    Half of $6$ is $3$, and $3^2 = 9$ completes the square.

  2. $(x + 3)^2 = 16$, so $x + 3 = \pm 4$.

    Both $4$ and $-4$ square to $16$.

  3. $x = 1$ or $x = -7$.

    Solve the two linear equations.

$x = 1$ or $x = -7$

Use the formula

Solve $x^2 - 4x + 1 = 0$.

  1. $a = 1$, $b = -4$, $c = 1$: $\Delta = 16 - 4 = 12$.

    Compute the discriminant first; it's the messiest part.

  2. $x = \dfrac{4 \pm \sqrt{12}}{2} = \dfrac{4 \pm 2\sqrt3}{2} = 2 \pm \sqrt3$.

    $-b = 4$. Simplify $\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt3$, then divide each term by $2$.

$x = 2 \pm \sqrt{3}$

Formula with $a \neq 1$

Solve $2x^2 + 3x - 4 = 0$.

  1. $\Delta = 3^2 - 4(2)(-4) = 9 + 32 = 41$.

    $-4ac$ with $c$ negative becomes a positive contribution.

  2. $x = \dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{41}}{4}$.

    $2a = 4$. Since $41$ is prime, $\sqrt{41}$ doesn't simplify.

$x = \dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{41}}{4}$

Read the discriminant

Without solving, describe the roots of (a) $x^2 - 6x + 9$ (b) $2x^2 + x + 5$ (c) $3x^2 - 5x - 2$.

  1. (a) $\Delta = 36 - 36 = 0$: one repeated real root.

    The parabola just touches the axis.

  2. (b) $\Delta = 1 - 40 = -39 < 0$: two complex conjugate roots.

    The square root of a negative is imaginary.

  3. (c) $\Delta = 25 + 24 = 49 = 7^2$: two different rational roots.

    A perfect-square discriminant means the formula gives rational answers, so it factors: $(3x + 1)(x - 2)$.

(a) one repeated real root; (b) two non-real complex roots; (c) two rational roots.

Complex roots

Solve $x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0$.

  1. $\Delta = 4 - 20 = -16$, and $\sqrt{-16} = 4i$.

    Chapter 2: $\sqrt{-16} = \sqrt{16}\, i$.

  2. $x = \dfrac{-2 \pm 4i}{2} = -1 \pm 2i$.

    The roots are complex conjugates, as they must be with real coefficients.

$x = -1 \pm 2i$

Completing the square when $a \neq 1$

Solve $3x^2 - 12x + 5 = 0$ by completing the square.

  1. Divide by $3$: $x^2 - 4x = -\dfrac53$.

    Completing the square needs a leading coefficient of $1$.

  2. Add $4$: $(x - 2)^2 = 4 - \dfrac53 = \dfrac73$.

    Half of $-4$ is $-2$, squared is $4$.

  3. $x = 2 \pm \sqrt{\dfrac73} = 2 \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{21}}{3}$.

    Rationalize: $\sqrt{\tfrac73} = \tfrac{\sqrt7}{\sqrt3} = \tfrac{\sqrt{21}}{3}$. The formula gives the same: $\tfrac{12 \pm \sqrt{84}}{6}$.

$x = 2 \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{21}}{3}$

Conjugate roots

One root of $x^2 + bx + c = 0$, where $b$ and $c$ are rational, is $2 - \sqrt5$. Find $b$ and $c$.

  1. The other root is $2 + \sqrt5$.

    With rational coefficients, irrational roots come in conjugate pairs.

  2. Sum: $4$. Product: $(2 - \sqrt5)(2 + \sqrt5) = 4 - 5 = -1$.

    The difference of squares clears the radical.

  3. Sum $= -b$ and product $= c$, so $b = -4$ and $c = -1$.

    For $x^2 + bx + c$, $a = 1$, so $r + s = -b$ and $rs = c$.

$b = -4$, $c = -1$

Force a double root

For which values of $k$ does $x^2 + kx + 16 = 0$ have exactly one (repeated) root?

  1. A repeated root means $\Delta = k^2 - 64 = 0$.

    That's when the $\pm\sqrt\Delta$ part vanishes.

  2. $k = 8$ or $k = -8$.

    Then the quadratic is $(x + 4)^2$ or $(x - 4)^2$.

$k = \pm 8$

Sum of squares of the roots

Let $r$ and $s$ be the roots of $2x^2 - 7x + 3 = 0$. Find $r^2 + s^2$ without finding $r$ and $s$.

  1. $r + s = \dfrac72$ and $rs = \dfrac32$.

    Sum $= -\tfrac{b}{a}$, product $= \tfrac{c}{a}$.

  2. $r^2 + s^2 = (r + s)^2 - 2rs = \dfrac{49}{4} - 3 = \dfrac{37}{4}$.

    $(r + s)^2 = r^2 + 2rs + s^2$, so subtract the $2rs$.

  3. Check: the roots are $3$ and $\tfrac12$, and $9 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac{37}{4}$. ✓

    Here the quadratic happens to factor as $(2x - 1)(x - 3)$, so we can verify.

$\dfrac{37}{4}$

Symmetric expressions in the roots can be built from the sum and product alone.

Two real roots, but watch $a$

For which real $k$ does $kx^2 + 4x + 1 = 0$ have two different real roots?

  1. Need $\Delta = 16 - 4k > 0$, so $k < 4$.

    Two different real roots require a positive discriminant.

  2. But if $k = 0$ the equation is $4x + 1 = 0$, which is linear with only one root.

    The formula assumes $a \ne 0$; the case $a = 0$ must be checked separately.

  3. So $k < 4$ and $k \neq 0$.

    Combine both conditions.

$k < 4$, $k \ne 0$

A coefficient that can be zero can change the type of equation.

6.4 Variations on a Theme

Hook

$4^x - 6 \cdot 2^x + 8 = 0$ doesn't look like a quadratic. Neither does $\sqrt{x + 7} = x + 1$. But call $2^x$ "$u$" in the first and it becomes $u^2 - 6u + 8 = 0$; square both sides of the second and an honest quadratic appears. Many equations are quadratics wearing a costume.

Concept

Rearrangements. If the variable is in a denominator, multiply both sides by every denominator. If it's under a square root, isolate the root and square both sides (repeat if there are two roots). Either way you reach a polynomial equation you can solve.

Always check your answers in the original equation. Multiplying by an expression that might be $0$, or squaring both sides, can introduce extraneous roots: values that solve the new equation but not the original. A value that makes a denominator $0$ is out. And since $\sqrt{\ }$ means the nonnegative root, squaring $A = B$ also accepts solutions of $-A = B$.

Substitutions. If an expression and its square both appear, such as $x^2$ and $x^4$, or $3^x$ and $9^x = (3^x)^2$, let $u$ be that expression. Solve the quadratic in $u$, then translate back, discarding impossible values (for example $2^x$ can never be negative).

Sum and product: if $x + y = S$ and $xy = P$, then $x$ and $y$ are the two roots of $t^2 - St + P = 0$.

A variable in the denominator

Solve $x + \dfrac{6}{x} = 5$.

  1. Multiply by $x$ (note $x \ne 0$): $x^2 + 6 = 5x$.

    Clearing the denominator gives a polynomial equation.

  2. $x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3) = 0$, so $x = 2$ or $x = 3$.

    Neither is $0$, so both are valid. Check: $2 + 3 = 5$ and $3 + 2 = 5$. ✓

$x = 2$ or $x = 3$

A quadratic in $x^2$

Find all real solutions of $x^4 - 13x^2 + 36 = 0$.

  1. Let $u = x^2$: $u^2 - 13u + 36 = 0$, so $(u - 4)(u - 9) = 0$.

    $x^4 = (x^2)^2$, so the equation is quadratic in $x^2$.

  2. $x^2 = 4$ or $x^2 = 9$, so $x = \pm 2$ or $x = \pm 3$.

    Each positive value of $u$ gives two values of $x$.

$x = -3, -2, 2, 3$

Two denominators

Solve $\dfrac{3}{x - 1} + \dfrac{2}{x + 1} = 2$.

  1. Multiply by $(x - 1)(x + 1)$: $3(x + 1) + 2(x - 1) = 2(x^2 - 1)$.

    Multiply by every denominator at once; note $x \ne \pm 1$.

  2. $5x + 1 = 2x^2 - 2$, so $2x^2 - 5x - 3 = 0$, which factors as $(2x + 1)(x - 3) = 0$.

    Collect terms; then $ac = -6$, sum $-5$: split as $-6x + x$.

  3. $x = 3$ or $x = -\dfrac12$. Neither is $\pm 1$. Check $x = 3$: $\tfrac32 + \tfrac12 = 2$ ✓.

    Both survive the check. Check $x = -\tfrac12$: $-2 + 4 = 2$ ✓.

$x = 3$ or $x = -\dfrac{1}{2}$

Next in the book: Example 6-8.

Reject an impossible value

Solve $9^x - 2 \cdot 3^{x+1} - 27 = 0$.

  1. $9^x = (3^x)^2$ and $3^{x+1} = 3 \cdot 3^x$. Let $u = 3^x$: $u^2 - 6u - 27 = 0$.

    Rewrite everything in terms of $3^x$.

  2. $(u - 9)(u + 3) = 0$, so $u = 9$ or $u = -3$.

    Product $-27$, sum $-6$.

  3. $3^x = -3$ is impossible, since powers of $3$ are positive. $3^x = 9$ gives $x = 2$.

    Translating back can rule out values of $u$.

$x = 2$

Given the sum and product

Find all pairs $(x, y)$ with $x + y = 4$ and $xy = 5$.

  1. $x$ and $y$ are the roots of $t^2 - 4t + 5 = 0$.

    $(t - x)(t - y) = t^2 - (x + y)t + xy$.

  2. $t = \dfrac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 20}}{2} = \dfrac{4 \pm 2i}{2} = 2 \pm i$.

    The discriminant is negative, so the solutions are complex.

  3. Pairs: $(2 + i, 2 - i)$ and $(2 - i, 2 + i)$.

    Either root can be $x$. Check: $(2 + i)(2 - i) = 4 + 1 = 5$ ✓.

$(x, y) = (2 + i,\ 2 - i)$ or $(2 - i,\ 2 + i)$

Next in the book: Examples 6-10 and 6-11.

Two radicals

Solve $\sqrt{2x + 3} - \sqrt{x + 1} = 1$.

  1. Isolate one radical: $\sqrt{2x + 3} = 1 + \sqrt{x + 1}$. Square: $2x + 3 = 1 + 2\sqrt{x + 1} + x + 1$.

    Squaring with both radicals on one side would leave a messy product; isolate one first.

  2. Simplify: $x + 1 = 2\sqrt{x + 1}$.

    Collect everything except the remaining radical.

  3. Let $u = \sqrt{x + 1} \ge 0$: $u^2 = 2u$, so $u = 0$ or $u = 2$, giving $x = -1$ or $x = 3$.

    Factor $u(u - 2) = 0$ rather than dividing by $u$.

  4. Check: $x = -1$: $\sqrt1 - \sqrt0 = 1$ ✓. $x = 3$: $\sqrt9 - \sqrt4 = 1$ ✓.

    We squared, so checking is mandatory. This time both survive.

$x = -1$ or $x = 3$

Isolate, square, repeat; then check every candidate.

Substitute a whole expression

Solve $(x^2 + x)^2 - 8(x^2 + x) + 12 = 0$.

  1. Let $u = x^2 + x$: $u^2 - 8u + 12 = (u - 2)(u - 6) = 0$.

    The block $x^2 + x$ and its square both appear.

  2. $x^2 + x = 2$: $(x + 2)(x - 1) = 0$, so $x = -2, 1$.

    Each value of $u$ gives a new quadratic in $x$.

  3. $x^2 + x = 6$: $(x + 3)(x - 2) = 0$, so $x = -3, 2$.

    Expanding would have given a quartic; substitution kept it to quadratics.

$x = -3, -2, 1, 2$

Look for a repeated block, not just a repeated variable.

6.5 Square Roots of Irrationals and Imaginaries

Hook

Squaring $1 + \sqrt3$ is easy: $1 + 2\sqrt3 + 3 = 4 + 2\sqrt3$. Now go backwards: what is $\sqrt{4 + 2\sqrt3}$? A calculator says $2.732\ldots$, which hides the beautiful exact answer $1 + \sqrt3$. This section is about undoing squares like that.

Concept

To find $\sqrt{a + b\sqrt c}$ (with $c$ prime, say), guess the form $x + y\sqrt c$ and square it:

$$(x + y\sqrt c)^2 = (x^2 + c y^2) + 2xy\sqrt c, \quad\text{so}\quad x^2 + cy^2 = a \text{ and } 2xy = b.$$

Small cases yield to trial: list pairs with $2xy = b$ and test them in the other equation. Otherwise substitute $y = \tfrac{b}{2x}$ to get a quadratic in $x^2$. If $c$ is not prime, the answer may instead look like $\sqrt p + \sqrt q$ with $pq = c$.

$\sqrt{\ }$ of a real number means the nonnegative root. Both $\pm(x + y\sqrt c)$ square to the same thing, so choose the sign that makes the answer positive.

Complex numbers work the same way. Guess $\sqrt{a + bi} = x + yi$:

$$(x + yi)^2 = (x^2 - y^2) + 2xyi, \quad\text{so}\quad x^2 - y^2 = a \text{ and } 2xy = b.$$

Every nonzero complex number has two square roots, $\pm(x + yi)$, and neither is preferred. Geometrically, squaring doubles a point's angle from the positive real axis and squares its distance from $0$, so a square root halves the angle and takes the root of the distance.

Squaring first

Compute $(2 + \sqrt3)^2$ and $(3 - 2i)^2$.

  1. $(2 + \sqrt3)^2 = 4 + 4\sqrt3 + 3 = 7 + 4\sqrt3$.

    $(x + y)^2 = x^2 + 2xy + y^2$ with $y = \sqrt3$.

  2. $(3 - 2i)^2 = 9 - 12i + 4i^2 = 9 - 12i - 4 = 5 - 12i$.

    Remember $i^2 = -1$.

$7 + 4\sqrt3$ and $5 - 12i$

An easy denesting

Simplify $\sqrt{6 + 2\sqrt5}$.

  1. Try $x + y\sqrt5$: need $x^2 + 5y^2 = 6$ and $2xy = 2$, so $xy = 1$.

    Match the rational and irrational parts.

  2. $x = y = 1$ works: $1 + 5 = 6$. So the answer is $1 + \sqrt5$.

    It's positive, so it's the right square root.

$1 + \sqrt{5}$

A minus sign inside

Simplify $\sqrt{11 - 6\sqrt2}$.

  1. Try $x + y\sqrt2$: $x^2 + 2y^2 = 11$ and $xy = -3$.

    $2xy = -6$.

  2. Pairs with $xy = -3$: $(1, -3)$ gives $1 + 18 = 19$ ✗; $(3, -1)$ gives $9 + 2 = 11$ ✓.

    Trial works quickly because $3$ has few factors.

  3. Candidates $\pm(3 - \sqrt2)$. Since $3 > \sqrt2$, take $3 - \sqrt2$.

    The square root symbol asks for the positive one.

$3 - \sqrt{2}$

A complex square root

Find the square roots of $-3 + 4i$.

  1. Let $(x + yi)^2 = -3 + 4i$: $x^2 - y^2 = -3$ and $xy = 2$.

    Real parts and imaginary parts must match separately.

  2. Try $(1, 2)$: $1 - 4 = -3$ ✓.

    Pairs with $xy = 2$ are few: $(1,2), (2,1)$ and their negatives.

  3. Check: $(1 + 2i)^2 = 1 + 4i - 4 = -3 + 4i$ ✓.

    The other root is the negative.

$\pm(1 + 2i)$

Next in the book: Example 6-15.

When $c$ is not prime

Simplify $\sqrt{8 + 2\sqrt{15}}$.

  1. $15 = 3 \cdot 5$, so try $\sqrt p + \sqrt q$: $(\sqrt p + \sqrt q)^2 = p + q + 2\sqrt{pq}$.

    With a composite radicand, the two pieces may be different radicals.

  2. Need $p + q = 8$ and $pq = 15$: $p = 3$, $q = 5$.

    Two numbers with sum $8$ and product $15$, a factoring problem in disguise.

$\sqrt{3} + \sqrt{5}$

Choosing the positive root

Simplify $\sqrt{22 - 12\sqrt2}$.

  1. $x^2 + 2y^2 = 22$ and $xy = -6$.

    Set up the matching equations.

  2. $(1,-6)$: $73$ ✗. $(2,-3)$: $4 + 18 = 22$ ✓.

    Test the factor pairs of $-6$.

  3. $3\sqrt2 = \sqrt{18} > \sqrt{4} = 2$, so $2 - 3\sqrt2 < 0$. Take its negative: $3\sqrt2 - 2$.

    Compare by squaring: $18 > 4$. A square root must be nonnegative.

$3\sqrt{2} - 2$

Next in the book: Example 6-14.

Solve the system directly

Find the square roots of $8 - 6i$ without guessing.

  1. $x^2 - y^2 = 8$ and $xy = -3$, so $y = -\dfrac3x$.

    Solve one equation for $y$.

  2. $x^2 - \dfrac{9}{x^2} = 8 \Rightarrow x^4 - 8x^2 - 9 = 0 \Rightarrow (x^2 - 9)(x^2 + 1) = 0$.

    A quadratic in $x^2$, a substitution from Section 6.4.

  3. $x$ is real, so $x^2 = 9$, $x = \pm 3$, and $y = \mp 1$.

    $x^2 = -1$ would make $x$ non-real, but we assumed $x$ and $y$ are real.

$\pm(3 - i)$

Next in the book: Exercise 6-11.

A root with no integer form

Simplify $\sqrt{2 + \sqrt3}$.

  1. Write $2 + \sqrt3 = \dfrac{4 + 2\sqrt3}{2}$.

    The form $a + 2\sqrt{pq}$ fits the pattern $(\sqrt p + \sqrt q)^2$.

  2. $4 + 2\sqrt3 = (1 + \sqrt3)^2$, so $\sqrt{2 + \sqrt3} = \dfrac{1 + \sqrt3}{\sqrt2}$.

    From the hook: $1 + 3 = 4$ and $1 \cdot 3 = 3$.

  3. Rationalize: $\dfrac{1 + \sqrt3}{\sqrt2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2} = \dfrac{\sqrt2 + \sqrt6}{2}$.

    Numerically both sides are about $1.932$.

$\dfrac{\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{6}}{2}$

Multiply and divide by $2$ to create the $2\sqrt{pq}$ shape.

A quadratic with a complex discriminant

Solve $z^2 - 3z + (3 - i) = 0$.

  1. $\Delta = 9 - 4(3 - i) = -3 + 4i$.

    The quadratic formula works with complex coefficients too.

  2. $\sqrt{-3 + 4i} = \pm(1 + 2i)$.

    Found in an earlier example.

  3. $z = \dfrac{3 \pm (1 + 2i)}{2}$, so $z = 2 + i$ or $z = 1 - i$.

    Check: sum $3$ and product $(2 + i)(1 - i) = 2 - 2i + i + 1 = 3 - i$ ✓.

$z = 2 + i$ or $z = 1 - i$

Complex square roots let the quadratic formula handle any quadratic at all.

6.6 Beyond Quadratics

Hook

Degree $1$: a line, one root. Degree $2$: a parabola, up to two roots. What about $x^3$, $x^4$, or $x^{17}$? The pattern continues, and much of what you learned about quadratics, especially "roots give factors", carries over.

Concept

A polynomial is a sum of constants times nonnegative integer powers of a variable, like $4x^5 - x^2 + 7$. Its degree is the highest power with a nonzero coefficient, and that coefficient is the leading coefficient. Degree $3$ is a cubic; degree $4$ is a quartic.

As with quadratics, the roots are the values that make the polynomial $0$, and each root $r$ corresponds to a factor $(x - r)$. A polynomial of degree $n$ with roots $r_1, \ldots, r_n$ factors as

$$a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)\cdots(x - r_n).$$

Expanding shows the same sum and product patterns as before. For a monic cubic, $(x - r)(x - s)(x - t) = x^3 - (r + s + t)x^2 + (rs + st + tr)x - rst$.

Solving higher-degree equations is harder in general, but the tools you have often suffice: factor out common factors, group terms, spot patterns like $(x + 1)^3$, substitute, or divide out a known root.

Degree of a product

What are the degree and leading coefficient of $(2x^2 + 1)(3x^3 - x + 4)$?

  1. The highest-power term comes from $2x^2 \cdot 3x^3 = 6x^5$.

    Every other product of terms has a smaller power.

Degree $5$, leading coefficient $6$.

Roots from factors

Solve $(x - 1)(x + 2)(x - 5) = 0$.

  1. A product is $0$ when some factor is $0$: $x = 1$, $x = -2$ or $x = 5$.

    The zero-product idea works for any number of factors.

$x = 1, -2, 5$

Expand a cubic

Expand $(x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)$.

  1. $(x - 1)(x - 2) = x^2 - 3x + 2$.

    Multiply two factors first.

  2. $(x^2 - 3x + 2)(x - 3) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 3x^2 + 9x + 2x - 6 = x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6$.

    Distribute and collect like terms.

  3. Notice $6 = 1 + 2 + 3$, $11 = 1\cdot2 + 2\cdot3 + 3\cdot1$, and $6 = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3$.

    The coefficients follow the sum and product pattern for cubics.

$x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6$

Factor out a common $x$

Solve $x^3 - 4x = 0$.

  1. $x(x^2 - 4) = x(x - 2)(x + 2) = 0$.

    Factor out $x$, then use the difference of squares.

  2. $x = 0$, $x = 2$ or $x = -2$.

    Dividing by $x$ at the start would have lost $x = 0$.

$x = 0, 2, -2$

Build a cubic from its roots

Find the cubic with leading coefficient $1$ and roots $1$, $2$ and $-3$.

  1. $(x - 1)(x - 2)(x + 3)$.

    Each root $r$ gives a factor $(x - r)$.

  2. $(x^2 - 3x + 2)(x + 3) = x^3 + 3x^2 - 3x^2 - 9x + 2x + 6 = x^3 - 7x + 6$.

    The $x^2$ term vanishes because the roots sum to $0$.

$x^3 - 7x + 6$

Factor by grouping

Solve $x^3 + 2x^2 - 9x - 18 = 0$.

  1. $x^2(x + 2) - 9(x + 2) = (x + 2)(x^2 - 9)$.

    Each pair has the factor $(x + 2)$ once you pull out $x^2$ and $-9$.

  2. $(x + 2)(x - 3)(x + 3) = 0$, so $x = -2, 3, -3$.

    Finish with the difference of squares.

$x = -3, -2, 3$

Recognize a cube

Find all real $x$ with $x^3 + 3x^2 + 3x = 7$.

  1. $(x + 1)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2 + 3x + 1$, so add $1$ to both sides: $(x + 1)^3 = 8$.

    Completing the cube, just like completing the square.

  2. $x + 1 = 2$, so $x = 1$.

    $8$ has exactly one real cube root.

$x = 1$

Next in the book: Exercise 6-8.

Divide out a known root

Given that $x = 2$ is a root of $x^3 - 5x^2 + 2x + 8 = 0$, find the other roots.

  1. Check: $8 - 20 + 4 + 8 = 0$ ✓. So $(x - 2)$ is a factor: $x^3 - 5x^2 + 2x + 8 = (x - 2)(x^2 + px + q)$.

    A root gives a factor; the other factor must be a quadratic with leading coefficient $1$.

  2. Expanding gives $x^3 + (p - 2)x^2 + (q - 2p)x - 2q$. Match: $p - 2 = -5$ and $-2q = 8$, so $p = -3$, $q = -4$.

    Check the middle coefficient: $q - 2p = -4 + 6 = 2$ ✓.

  3. $x^2 - 3x - 4 = (x - 4)(x + 1)$, so the other roots are $4$ and $-1$.

    Back to a familiar quadratic. The roots sum to $2 + 4 - 1 = 5$, matching the $-5x^2$ term.

$x = 4$ and $x = -1$

Each known root lowers the degree by one.

Irrational roots in a cubic

Find a cubic with integer coefficients and leading coefficient $1$ that has roots $1 + \sqrt2$ and $3$.

  1. To have integer coefficients, include the conjugate $1 - \sqrt2$ as a root too.

    Pairing conjugates removes the radical.

  2. $(x - 1 - \sqrt2)(x - 1 + \sqrt2) = (x - 1)^2 - 2 = x^2 - 2x - 1$.

    Difference of squares with $(x - 1)$ as one term.

  3. $(x^2 - 2x - 1)(x - 3) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 2x^2 + 6x - x + 3 = x^3 - 5x^2 + 5x + 3$.

    Multiply in the factor for the root $3$.

$x^3 - 5x^2 + 5x + 3$
