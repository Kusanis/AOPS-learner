Quadrilaterals

Add one more side to a triangle and the shapes multiply: trapezoids, parallelograms, rhombuses, rectangles, squares. Each special shape is defined by one simple property, and each one quietly hands you several more. In this chapter you'll learn what every family inherits, how to find areas by cutting quadrilaterals into triangles, and how to prove which family a mystery shape belongs to.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • A diagonal cuts any quadrilateral into two triangles, so the angles always add to $360^\circ$.
  • Trapezoid: the median (joining the midpoints of the legs) has length $\dfrac{a+b}{2}$, and the area is median $\times$ height $= \dfrac{(a+b)h}{2}$.
  • Parallelogram: opposite sides and opposite angles are equal, neighbouring angles are supplementary, and the diagonals bisect each other. Area $= bh = ab\sin\theta = \tfrac12 d_1 d_2 \sin\varphi$.
  • Rhombus: a parallelogram with perpendicular diagonals, so the area is $\tfrac12 d_1 d_2$ and each side is $\sqrt{(d_1/2)^2 + (d_2/2)^2}$.
  • Rectangle: equal diagonals $\sqrt{l^2+w^2}$. Use $(l+w)^2 = l^2 + w^2 + 2lw$ to link perimeter, area and diagonal.
  • Drop perpendiculars to create right triangles and rectangles; that is the standard first move.
  • A converse needs its own proof. Diagonals that bisect each other make a parallelogram; add "perpendicular" for a rhombus, "equal" for a rectangle.

12.1 The Fundamentals

Hook

Cut a paper quadrilateral of any shape, tear off its four corners and fit them together point to point. No matter how lopsided the original was, the four corners always close up into one full turn. Why should four random angles be so well behaved?

Concept

A quadrilateral is a closed figure with four straight sides. We always name it by listing its vertices in order around the figure: $ABCD$ and $BCDA$ name the same quadrilateral, but $ACBD$ does not, because $A$ and $C$ are not neighbours.

A segment joining two vertices that are not neighbours is a diagonal. Every quadrilateral has exactly two: $AC$ and $BD$. If the diagonals are perpendicular, the quadrilateral is called orthodiagonal.

The diagonal $AC$ splits $ABCD$ into triangles $ABC$ and $ACD$. The angles of those two triangles together make up exactly the four angles of the quadrilateral, so

$$\angle A + \angle B + \angle C + \angle D = 180^\circ + 180^\circ = 360^\circ.$$

A quadrilateral is convex if every angle is less than $180^\circ$. If one angle is a reflex angle (more than $180^\circ$), the quadrilateral is concave, shaped like an arrowhead. The angle sum is still $360^\circ$; just split it along the diagonal from the reflex vertex, which lies inside. The perimeter is the sum of the four sides.

Most contest problems use convex quadrilaterals. Assume convex unless a figure says otherwise.

The missing angle

Three angles of a quadrilateral measure $75^\circ$, $110^\circ$ and $95^\circ$. Find the fourth angle.

  1. The known angles add to $75^\circ + 110^\circ + 95^\circ = 280^\circ$.

    Collect what we know before using the angle sum.

  2. The fourth angle is $360^\circ - 280^\circ = 80^\circ$.

    All four angles of a quadrilateral add to $360^\circ$.

$80^\circ$

Names and diagonals

Quadrilateral $PQRS$ is drawn. Which of $QRSP$, $PRQS$ and $SRQP$ are also correct names for it, and what are its diagonals?

  1. $QRSP$ is valid: it walks around the same figure, starting at $Q$.

    Any starting vertex is fine, as long as we keep going around in order.

  2. $SRQP$ is valid: it walks around in the opposite direction.

    Reversing direction still lists neighbours next to each other.

  3. $PRQS$ is not valid: $P$ and $R$ are not neighbours in $PQRS$.

    Writing $PR$ first would make a diagonal into a side.

  4. The diagonals join non-neighbours: $PR$ and $QS$.

    Each vertex has exactly one vertex it is not adjacent to.

$QRSP$ and $SRQP$ are valid names; the diagonals are $PR$ and $QS$.

Angles in a ratio

The angles of a quadrilateral are in the ratio $2 : 3 : 4 : 6$. Find the largest angle.

  1. Call the angles $2x$, $3x$, $4x$ and $6x$.

    A ratio tells us the angles are multiples of one unknown.

  2. $2x + 3x + 4x + 6x = 15x = 360^\circ$, so $x = 24^\circ$.

    Use the angle sum of a quadrilateral.

  3. The angles are $48^\circ, 72^\circ, 96^\circ, 144^\circ$; the largest is $6x = 144^\circ$.

    Checking: $48 + 72 + 96 + 144 = 360$.

$144^\circ$

Perimeter with an unknown

The sides of a quadrilateral are $x$, $x + 3$, $2x$ and $2x - 1$, and its perimeter is $44$. Find the longest side.

  1. $x + (x+3) + 2x + (2x-1) = 6x + 2 = 44$, so $x = 7$.

    The perimeter is the sum of all four sides.

  2. The sides are $7, 10, 14, 13$.

    Substitute $x = 7$ into each expression. They add to $44$, as required.

$14$

The arrowhead

Concave quadrilateral $ABCD$ has a reflex angle at $D$. The other three angles are $\angle A = 30^\circ$, $\angle B = 40^\circ$, $\angle C = 50^\circ$. Find the reflex angle at $D$, and the ordinary angle you see at $D$ from outside the dent.

  1. The reflex angle is $360^\circ - (30^\circ + 40^\circ + 50^\circ) = 240^\circ$.

    The angle sum is $360^\circ$ for concave quadrilaterals too: split along diagonal $BD$, which lies inside.

  2. The angle on the other side of $D$ is $360^\circ - 240^\circ = 120^\circ$.

    The two angles at a point make a full turn.

  3. Notice $120^\circ = 30^\circ + 40^\circ + 50^\circ$.

    In general this outside angle at the dent is $360^\circ - (360^\circ - (A+B+C)) = A + B + C$.

Reflex angle $240^\circ$; the angle outside the dent is $120^\circ$.

In an arrowhead, the angle at the dent equals the sum of the other three angles.

Angles in arithmetic progression

The angles of a quadrilateral form an arithmetic sequence, and the largest angle is twice the smallest. Find all four angles.

  1. Write the angles as $a$, $a + d$, $a + 2d$, $a + 3d$.

    An arithmetic sequence adds the same amount each time.

  2. Largest is twice smallest: $a + 3d = 2a$, so $a = 3d$.

    Translate the second condition into an equation.

  3. Sum: $4a + 6d = 12d + 6d = 18d = 360^\circ$, so $d = 20^\circ$ and $a = 60^\circ$.

    Substitute $a = 3d$ into the angle sum.

$60^\circ, 80^\circ, 100^\circ, 120^\circ$

Where two angle bisectors meet

In convex quadrilateral $ABCD$, $\angle C = 100^\circ$ and $\angle D = 70^\circ$. The bisectors of $\angle A$ and $\angle B$ meet at $P$. Find $\angle APB$, and find a formula for it in general.

  1. In triangle $APB$: $\angle APB = 180^\circ - \tfrac12\angle A - \tfrac12\angle B$.

    The bisectors cut $\angle A$ and $\angle B$ in half, and those halves are two angles of triangle $APB$.

  2. $\angle A + \angle B = 360^\circ - \angle C - \angle D$.

    The quadrilateral angle sum gives the sum we need without finding each angle.

  3. $\angle APB = 180^\circ - \tfrac12(360^\circ - \angle C - \angle D) = \tfrac12(\angle C + \angle D)$.

    Substitute and simplify; the $180^\circ$ terms cancel.

  4. Here $\angle APB = \tfrac12(100^\circ + 70^\circ) = 85^\circ$.

    Plug in the given angles.

$\angle APB = 85^\circ$; in general $\angle APB = \tfrac12(\angle C + \angle D)$.

When you can't find angles one at a time, the angle sum often gives exactly the combination you need.

12.2 Trapezoids

Hook

A staircase handrail, a lampshade seen from the side, a slice of a pyramid: shapes with one pair of parallel sides are everywhere. Their area looks awkward because the top and bottom differ, yet there is a single "average" length that makes the area as easy as a rectangle's.

Concept

A trapezoid has one pair of parallel sides, the bases (lengths $a$ and $b$). The other two sides are the legs. The distance between the bases is the height $h$. Because the bases are parallel, the two angles along each leg are co-interior angles, so they are supplementary: if $AB \parallel CD$, then $\angle A + \angle D = \angle B + \angle C = 180^\circ$.

The median joins the midpoints of the two legs. It is parallel to the bases and its length is their average. Rotating a copy of the trapezoid half a turn about the midpoint of a leg makes a parallelogram with base $a + b$ and height $h$, which explains the area formula:

$$\text{median} = \frac{a+b}{2}, \qquad \text{Area} = \frac{(a+b)\,h}{2} = \text{median} \times h.$$

If the legs are equal, the trapezoid is isosceles. Dropping altitudes from the ends of the shorter base creates two congruent right triangles, so an isosceles trapezoid has equal base angles and equal diagonals (and either fact implies it is isosceles).

Altitudes from the short base split the long base into the short base plus two overhangs. In an isosceles trapezoid each overhang is $\tfrac{b-a}{2}$.

Area from the bases

A trapezoid has bases $7$ and $13$ and height $5$. Find its median and its area.

  1. Median $= \dfrac{7 + 13}{2} = 10$.

    The median is the average of the bases.

  2. Area $= 10 \times 5 = 50$.

    Area is median times height, the same as $\tfrac{(a+b)h}{2}$.

Median $10$, area $50$.

Supplementary angles along a leg

In trapezoid $ABCD$ with $AB \parallel CD$, $\angle A = 65^\circ$ and $\angle C = 110^\circ$. Find $\angle B$ and $\angle D$.

  1. $\angle D = 180^\circ - \angle A = 115^\circ$.

    $A$ and $D$ are the two ends of leg $AD$, which crosses the parallel bases, so they are supplementary.

  2. $\angle B = 180^\circ - \angle C = 70^\circ$.

    Same reason along leg $BC$. Check: $65 + 70 + 110 + 115 = 360$.

$\angle B = 70^\circ$, $\angle D = 115^\circ$

Working backward from the median

A trapezoid has median $12$ and one base $9$. Find the other base. If its area is $84$, find its height.

  1. $\dfrac{9 + b}{2} = 12 \Rightarrow b = 15$.

    The median is the average of the bases, so the bases add to twice the median.

  2. $12h = 84 \Rightarrow h = 7$.

    Area is median times height.

Other base $15$, height $7$.

Isosceles trapezoid: find the height

An isosceles trapezoid has bases $6$ and $14$ and legs of length $5$. Find its area.

  1. Drop altitudes from the ends of the top base. They cut the bottom base into $4 + 6 + 4$.

    The middle piece equals the top base (it's a rectangle), and by symmetry the two overhangs share the remaining $14 - 6 = 8$ equally.

  2. $h = \sqrt{5^2 - 4^2} = 3$.

    Each overhang, altitude and leg form a right triangle with hypotenuse $5$.

  3. Area $= \dfrac{(6 + 14)\cdot 3}{2} = 30$.

    Now the trapezoid formula applies.

$30$

The diagonal of the same trapezoid

For the isosceles trapezoid with bases $6$ and $14$ and legs $5$, find the length of a diagonal.

  1. Let the long base be $DC$ and the top be $AB$, with $A$ above the left end. The altitude from $A$ lands $4$ units from $D$, hence $14 - 4 = 10$ units from $C$.

    The diagonal $AC$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the height and this horizontal distance.

  2. $AC = \sqrt{10^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{109}$.

    Pythagorean Theorem with legs $10$ and $h = 3$.

$\sqrt{109}$

Altitudes turn trapezoid questions into right-triangle questions.

Different base angles

Trapezoid $ABCD$ has $AB \parallel DC$, $AB = 4$, height $6$, $\angle D = 60^\circ$ and $\angle C = 45^\circ$. Find $DC$ and the area.

  1. Drop altitudes $AE$ and $BF$ to $DC$. Then $EF = AB = 4$.

    $ABFE$ is a rectangle, so its opposite sides are equal.

  2. In $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle $AED$, the long leg is $AE = 6$, so $DE = \dfrac{6}{\sqrt3} = 2\sqrt3$.

    The side opposite $60^\circ$ is $\sqrt3$ times the side opposite $30^\circ$.

  3. In $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle $BFC$, $FC = BF = 6$.

    The legs of an isosceles right triangle are equal.

  4. $DC = 2\sqrt3 + 4 + 6 = 10 + 2\sqrt3$, and the area is $\dfrac{(4 + 10 + 2\sqrt3)\cdot 6}{2} = 42 + 6\sqrt3$.

    Add the three pieces of the long base, then use the area formula.

$DC = 10 + 2\sqrt3$, area $= 42 + 6\sqrt3$.

How the diagonals divide each other

In trapezoid $ABCD$, $AB \parallel CD$, $AB = 6$ and $CD = 15$. The diagonals meet at $E$, and $AC = 14$. Find $AE$ and $EC$.

  1. $\triangle ABE \sim \triangle CDE$.

    $\angle BAE = \angle DCE$ and $\angle ABE = \angle CDE$ are alternate angles for the parallel bases, so AA similarity applies.

  2. The ratio of similarity is $AB : CD = 6 : 15 = 2 : 5$, so $AE : EC = 2 : 5$.

    $AE$ and $EC$ are corresponding sides.

  3. $AE = \tfrac{2}{7}\cdot 14 = 4$ and $EC = \tfrac{5}{7}\cdot 14 = 10$.

    Split $14$ in the ratio $2 : 5$.

$AE = 4$, $EC = 10$

The diagonals of a trapezoid cut each other in the ratio of the bases.

Midpoints of the diagonals

A trapezoid has bases $8$ and $18$. Find the distance between the midpoints of its two diagonals.

  1. Let $AB = 8$ (top) and $DC = 18$. Let $X$ be the midpoint of leg $AD$, and let $M$, $N$ be the midpoints of diagonals $AC$ and $BD$.

    Naming the points lets us use midpoint facts in triangles.

  2. In $\triangle ADC$, $XM$ joins midpoints of $AD$ and $AC$, so $XM \parallel DC$ and $XM = \tfrac12 DC = 9$.

    The segment joining midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and half as long.

  3. In $\triangle DAB$, $XN$ joins midpoints of $DA$ and $DB$, so $XN \parallel AB$ and $XN = \tfrac12 AB = 4$.

    Same midpoint fact in a different triangle.

  4. Both $M$ and $N$ lie on the line through $X$ parallel to the bases (the median), on the same side of $X$, so $MN = 9 - 4 = 5$.

    Two segments from $X$ along the same line in the same direction differ by $MN$.

$5$, which is $\dfrac{18 - 8}{2}$.

The median carries the midpoints of the diagonals, which sit $\tfrac{b-a}{2}$ apart.

12.3 Parallelograms

Hook

Push sideways on a rectangular picture frame with loose joints and it leans into a slanted shape. The sides keep their lengths and stay parallel, but the area shrinks as it leans. What exactly controls that area?

Concept

A parallelogram has both pairs of opposite sides parallel. Draw diagonal $AC$: alternate angles give $\angle BAC = \angle DCA$ and $\angle BCA = \angle DAC$, so $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle CDA$ by ASA. That one congruence delivers a list of facts:

  • Opposite sides are equal: $AB = CD$, $BC = DA$.
  • Opposite angles are equal, and neighbouring angles are supplementary: $\angle A + \angle B = 180^\circ$.
  • The diagonals bisect each other: they meet at their common midpoint.

For area, take any side as the base $b$ and measure the perpendicular height $h$ to the opposite side. Two more formulas come from splitting along diagonals into triangles:

$$[ABCD] = b\,h = (AB)(AD)\sin A = \tfrac12\, d_1 d_2 \sin\varphi,$$

where $\varphi$ is either angle between the diagonals (both have the same sine, since $\sin(180^\circ - \varphi) = \sin\varphi$).

Notation: $[ABCD]$ means the area of $ABCD$.

One angle gives all four

One angle of a parallelogram is $68^\circ$. Find the other three.

  1. The opposite angle is also $68^\circ$.

    Opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal.

  2. The two neighbouring angles are $180^\circ - 68^\circ = 112^\circ$.

    Neighbouring angles are co-interior angles between parallel sides.

$68^\circ$, $112^\circ$, $112^\circ$

Two bases, two heights

A parallelogram has sides $12$ and $8$. The height to the side of length $12$ is $5$. Find the area and the height to the side of length $8$.

  1. Area $= 12 \times 5 = 60$.

    Base times the height drawn to that base.

  2. Using the other base: $8 \times h' = 60$, so $h' = 7.5$.

    The area doesn't depend on which side we call the base.

Area $60$; the other height is $7.5$.

Algebra with opposite angles

Two opposite angles of a parallelogram measure $(4x + 10)^\circ$ and $(7x - 35)^\circ$. Find all the angles.

  1. $4x + 10 = 7x - 35 \Rightarrow 3x = 45 \Rightarrow x = 15$.

    Opposite angles are equal.

  2. Those angles are $4(15) + 10 = 70^\circ$; the other two are $180^\circ - 70^\circ = 110^\circ$.

    Neighbours are supplementary.

$70^\circ, 110^\circ, 70^\circ, 110^\circ$

Diagonals that bisect

The diagonals of parallelogram $ABCD$ meet at $E$, with $AE = 2y + 1$, $EC = 3y - 4$ and $BE = y + 3$. Find $AC$ and $BD$.

  1. $2y + 1 = 3y - 4 \Rightarrow y = 5$.

    $E$ is the midpoint of $AC$, so $AE = EC$.

  2. $AC = 2 \cdot 11 = 22$; $BE = 8$ so $BD = 16$.

    Each diagonal is twice the half from $E$.

$AC = 22$, $BD = 16$

Area from two sides and an angle

A parallelogram has sides $8$ and $10$, and one of its angles is $150^\circ$. Find its area.

  1. $[ABCD] = (8)(10)\sin 150^\circ$.

    The diagonal splits it into two congruent triangles, each with area $\tfrac12 (8)(10)\sin 150^\circ$.

  2. $\sin 150^\circ = \sin 30^\circ = \tfrac12$, so the area is $40$.

    Supplementary angles have the same sine, so the $30^\circ$ angle works just as well.

$40$

Area from the diagonals

The diagonals of a parallelogram have lengths $12$ and $10$ and meet at a $30^\circ$ angle. Find the area.

  1. The diagonals cut the parallelogram into four triangles, each with two sides $6$ and $5$ (half-diagonals) meeting at $30^\circ$ or $150^\circ$.

    The diagonals bisect each other, so the half-lengths are $6$ and $5$.

  2. Each triangle has area $\tfrac12(6)(5)\sin 30^\circ = 7.5$, so the total is $4 \times 7.5 = 30$.

    $\sin 150^\circ = \sin 30^\circ$, so all four triangles have the same area.

  3. Check with the formula: $\tfrac12 (12)(10)\sin 30^\circ = 30$.

    This is where $\tfrac12 d_1 d_2 \sin\varphi$ comes from.

$30$

From the centre to a side

Parallelogram $PQRS$ has area $72$ and $PQ = 9$. Its diagonals meet at $X$. How far is $X$ from side $PQ$?

  1. The height between $PQ$ and $SR$ is $72 / 9 = 8$.

    Area is base times height.

  2. $X$ is the midpoint of diagonal $PR$, so it is halfway between the lines $PQ$ and $SR$: distance $4$.

    The midpoint of a segment joining two parallel lines is equidistant from them.

$4$

The parallelogram law

A parallelogram has sides $5$ and $7$, and one diagonal has length $8$. Find the other diagonal.

  1. Place $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (a, 0)$, $D = (p, q)$, so $C = (a + p, q)$, with $AB = a$ and $AD^2 = p^2 + q^2$.

    Coordinates make the diagonal lengths easy to write down.

  2. $AC^2 + BD^2 = (a + p)^2 + q^2 + (p - a)^2 + q^2 = 2a^2 + 2(p^2 + q^2)$.

    The $\pm 2ap$ terms cancel, leaving $2AB^2 + 2AD^2$: the sum of the squares of all four sides.

  3. $8^2 + d^2 = 2(5^2) + 2(7^2) = 148$, so $d^2 = 84$ and $d = 2\sqrt{21}$.

    Apply the identity to our numbers.

$2\sqrt{21}$

In a parallelogram, $d_1^2 + d_2^2$ equals the sum of the squares of the four sides.

12.4 Rhombuses (Rhombi?)

Hook

A kite made from two sticks crossed at right angles, with the string pulled tight around the ends, has equal sides when the sticks cross at their middles. Its area turns out to depend only on the lengths of the two sticks, not on the sides at all.

Concept

A rhombus has four equal sides. Diagonal $BD$ splits it into two triangles that are congruent by SSS, which gives alternate angles and shows that opposite sides are parallel. So every rhombus is a parallelogram and inherits all the parallelogram facts.

Since the diagonals bisect each other, $\triangle ABE \cong \triangle CBE$ by SSS (where $E$ is the centre), so the two angles at $E$ along $AC$ are equal and add to $180^\circ$: the diagonals are perpendicular. They also bisect the angles of the rhombus.

The diagonals cut the rhombus into four congruent right triangles with legs $\tfrac{d_1}{2}$ and $\tfrac{d_2}{2}$. That gives the side and the area:

$$s = \sqrt{\left(\tfrac{d_1}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{d_2}{2}\right)^2}, \qquad [ABCD] = \frac{d_1 d_2}{2}.$$

A rhombus is also a parallelogram, so $s^2 \sin\theta$ and base $\times$ height work too.

Area from diagonals

A rhombus has diagonals $6$ and $9$. Find its area.

  1. Area $= \dfrac{6 \times 9}{2} = 27$.

    The rhombus is half of the $6 \times 9$ rectangle around it.

$27$

Side from diagonals

A rhombus has diagonals $10$ and $24$. Find its perimeter.

  1. The half-diagonals are $5$ and $12$.

    The diagonals bisect each other.

  2. Side $= \sqrt{5^2 + 12^2} = 13$, so the perimeter is $4 \times 13 = 52$.

    The diagonals are perpendicular, so the half-diagonals and a side form a right triangle.

$52$

Equal sides, solved

Two sides of a rhombus have lengths $4x - 1$ and $x + 11$. Find the perimeter.

  1. $4x - 1 = x + 11 \Rightarrow x = 4$.

    All sides of a rhombus are equal.

  2. Each side is $15$, so the perimeter is $60$.

    Four equal sides.

$60$

The missing diagonal

A rhombus has side $25$ and one diagonal $14$. Find the other diagonal and the area.

  1. Half of the known diagonal is $7$; the other half-diagonal is $\sqrt{25^2 - 7^2} = \sqrt{576} = 24$.

    Right triangle with hypotenuse $25$ (a side) and one leg $7$.

  2. The other diagonal is $48$, and the area is $\dfrac{14 \times 48}{2} = 336$.

    Double the half-diagonal, then use $\tfrac12 d_1 d_2$.

Diagonal $48$, area $336$.

A rhombus with a $60^\circ$ angle

A rhombus has side $6$ and an angle of $60^\circ$. Find both diagonals and the area.

  1. The short diagonal cuts off a triangle with two sides $6$ and a $60^\circ$ angle between them; it is equilateral, so the short diagonal is $6$.

    An isosceles triangle with a $60^\circ$ apex angle has all angles $60^\circ$.

  2. Half the long diagonal is $\sqrt{6^2 - 3^2} = 3\sqrt3$, so the long diagonal is $6\sqrt3$.

    Right triangle with hypotenuse $6$ and short leg $3$.

  3. Area $= \dfrac{6 \cdot 6\sqrt3}{2} = 18\sqrt3$.

    Check: $s^2\sin 60^\circ = 36 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = 18\sqrt3$.

Diagonals $6$ and $6\sqrt3$; area $18\sqrt3$.

The height of a rhombus

A rhombus has diagonals $16$ and $12$. Find the distance between two opposite sides.

  1. Area $= \dfrac{16 \times 12}{2} = 96$ and side $= \sqrt{8^2 + 6^2} = 10$.

    The diagonals give the area; the half-diagonals give the side.

  2. As a parallelogram, area $=$ side $\times$ height: $10h = 96$, so $h = 9.6$.

    The distance between opposite sides is the height to a side.

$9.6$

Computing one area in two ways is a quick route to a hidden length.

Diagonals from perimeter and area

A rhombus has perimeter $40$ and area $96$. Find its diagonals.

  1. Let the half-diagonals be $p$ and $q$. The side is $10$, so $p^2 + q^2 = 100$.

    Perimeter $40$ means side $10$, the hypotenuse of the right triangle with legs $p$, $q$.

  2. Area $= \dfrac{(2p)(2q)}{2} = 2pq = 96$, so $pq = 48$.

    Write the area in terms of the half-diagonals.

  3. $(p + q)^2 = p^2 + q^2 + 2pq = 196$, so $p + q = 14$; and $(p - q)^2 = 100 - 96 = 4$, so $p - q = 2$.

    Combining the two equations into perfect squares avoids solving a quadratic directly.

  4. $p = 8$ and $q = 6$, so the diagonals are $16$ and $12$.

    Add and subtract the two equations. Check: $\tfrac12(16)(12) = 96$ and $\sqrt{64 + 36} = 10$.

$16$ and $12$

$(p+q)^2 = p^2 + q^2 + 2pq$ links a side (via Pythagoras) to an area.

12.5 Rectangles and Squares

Hook

A TV screen is sold by the length of its diagonal. Two screens can share the same "size" yet have very different areas. Which shape of rectangle gives the most screen for a given diagonal?

Concept

A rectangle has four equal angles. Since they add to $360^\circ$, each is $90^\circ$, and opposite sides are parallel, so every rectangle is a parallelogram. With length $l$ and width $w$:

$$\text{Perimeter} = 2l + 2w, \qquad \text{Area} = lw, \qquad \text{diagonal} = \sqrt{l^2 + w^2}.$$

Both diagonals have that length (Pythagoras on either half), so a rectangle's diagonals are equal and bisect each other. Problems often give two of perimeter, area and diagonal; connect them with

$$(l + w)^2 = l^2 + w^2 + 2lw = d^2 + 2\,(\text{Area}).$$

A square has equal sides and equal angles, so it is both a rectangle and a rhombus and inherits everything from both. With side $s$: area $s^2$, perimeter $4s$, diagonal $s\sqrt2$, and area $= \tfrac12 d^2$.

In rectangles and squares, drawing extra perpendicular lines almost always helps: everything is already lined up with right angles.

The basic three

A rectangle is $12$ by $9$. Find its perimeter, area and diagonal.

  1. Perimeter $= 2(12) + 2(9) = 42$; area $= 12 \times 9 = 108$.

    Direct use of the formulas.

  2. Diagonal $= \sqrt{144 + 81} = \sqrt{225} = 15$.

    The diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $12$ and $9$.

Perimeter $42$, area $108$, diagonal $15$.

A square from its diagonal

A square has diagonal $10$. Find its side and area.

  1. $s\sqrt2 = 10 \Rightarrow s = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt2} = 5\sqrt2$.

    The diagonal of a square is $\sqrt2$ times the side.

  2. Area $= s^2 = 50$.

    Shortcut: area $= \tfrac12 d^2 = \tfrac12(100)$, because a square is a rhombus with equal diagonals.

Side $5\sqrt2$, area $50$.

Perimeter and diagonal give the sides

A rectangle has perimeter $34$ and diagonal $13$. Find its area and dimensions.

  1. $l + w = 17$ and $l^2 + w^2 = 169$.

    Half the perimeter, and Pythagoras for the diagonal.

  2. $2lw = (l + w)^2 - (l^2 + w^2) = 289 - 169 = 120$, so the area is $lw = 60$.

    The identity $(l + w)^2 = l^2 + w^2 + 2lw$ hands us the area directly.

  3. Two numbers with sum $17$ and product $60$: $12$ and $5$.

    Check: $12^2 + 5^2 = 169$.

Area $60$; the rectangle is $12$ by $5$.

A diagonal at $30^\circ$

A diagonal of a rectangle has length $12$ and makes a $30^\circ$ angle with the longer side. Find the area.

  1. The diagonal and two sides form a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle with hypotenuse $12$.

    The rectangle's corner is a right angle.

  2. Short side $= 6$, long side $= 6\sqrt3$.

    Opposite $30^\circ$ is half the hypotenuse; opposite $60^\circ$ is $\sqrt3$ times that.

  3. Area $= 6 \cdot 6\sqrt3 = 36\sqrt3$.

    Length times width.

$36\sqrt3$

Area and diagonal give the perimeter

A rectangle has area $24$ and diagonal $8$. Find its perimeter.

  1. $lw = 24$ and $l^2 + w^2 = 64$.

    Translate the given facts.

  2. $(l + w)^2 = 64 + 2(24) = 112$, so $l + w = \sqrt{112} = 4\sqrt7$.

    We never need the individual sides, which here are not nice numbers.

  3. Perimeter $= 2(l + w) = 8\sqrt7$.

    The perimeter only depends on the sum.

$8\sqrt7$

Aim for the combination the question asks for ($l + w$), not the pieces.

A segment across a square

Square $ABCD$ has side $12$, with $AB$ on top and $DC$ on the bottom. Point $E$ on $AB$ has $AE = 3$, and point $F$ on $CD$ has $CF = 4$. Find $EF$.

  1. Drop a perpendicular from $E$ to $DC$, meeting it at $G$. Then $EG = 12$ and $DG = AE = 3$.

    $AEGD$ is a rectangle, so the perpendicular makes a right triangle $EGF$.

  2. $DF = 12 - 4 = 8$, so $GF = 8 - 3 = 5$.

    Measure both points from $D$ along the bottom side.

  3. $EF = \sqrt{12^2 + 5^2} = 13$.

    Pythagoras in right triangle $EGF$.

$13$

A picture frame

A picture is twice as long as it is wide. A frame $3$ inches wide surrounds it, and the outside perimeter of the frame is $84$ inches. Find the area of the frame itself.

  1. Let the picture be $2w$ by $w$. The outside of the frame is $(2w + 6)$ by $(w + 6)$.

    The frame adds $3$ inches on each side, so $6$ to each dimension.

  2. $2(2w + 6) + 2(w + 6) = 6w + 24 = 84$, so $w = 10$.

    Outside perimeter.

  3. Frame area $= 26 \times 16 - 20 \times 10 = 416 - 200 = 216$ square inches.

    Outer rectangle minus the picture.

$216$ square inches

A tilted square inside a square

Square $EFGH$ has one vertex on each side of square $ABCD$, which has side $7$. Each vertex of $EFGH$ cuts a side of $ABCD$ into pieces $3$ and $4$ (going around in the same direction). Show $EFGH$ is a square and find its area.

  1. The four corner triangles each have legs $3$ and $4$ and a right angle, so they are congruent (LL), and each hypotenuse is $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.

    So all four sides of $EFGH$ equal $5$.

  2. At $E$, the two acute angles of neighbouring corner triangles are different angles of congruent triangles, so they add to $90^\circ$. The angle of $EFGH$ at $E$ is $180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$.

    The three angles at $E$ lie along a straight side of $ABCD$.

  3. Area of $EFGH$ $= 5^2 = 25$. Check: $7^2 - 4 \cdot \tfrac12 (3)(4) = 49 - 24 = 25$.

    Big square minus four triangles must agree.

$EFGH$ is a square of area $25$.

Doing this with legs $a$, $b$ gives $(a + b)^2 - 2ab = c^2$: a proof of the Pythagorean Theorem.

12.6 Hints and Problems

Hook

"Every square has four equal sides." True. "Every shape with four equal sides is a square." False: a squashed rhombus is a counterexample. Flipping a statement around gives its converse, and the converse needs its own proof. Which converses about quadrilaterals survive?

Concept

To show a quadrilateral belongs to a family, prove the definition (or a theorem you have already proved). The most useful tests look at the diagonals, meeting at $E$:

Bisect each other $\Rightarrow$ parallelogram.   Bisect + perpendicular $\Rightarrow$ rhombus.
Bisect + equal $\Rightarrow$ rectangle.   All three $\Rightarrow$ square.

Other parallelogram tests: both pairs of opposite sides equal; both pairs of opposite angles equal; or one pair of opposite sides both parallel and equal.

Be careful: perpendicular diagonals alone do not force a rhombus (think of a kite), and one pair of parallel sides plus one pair of equal sides does not force a parallelogram (think of an isosceles trapezoid). When a statement is false, a single counterexample settles it.

Proof toolkit: congruent triangles (SSS, SAS, ASA, HL), alternate angles for parallel lines, and the midpoint segment of a triangle (parallel to the third side, half as long).

A counterexample: perpendicular is not enough

Is every quadrilateral with perpendicular diagonals a rhombus?

  1. Take $A = (-1, 0)$, $C = (4, 0)$, $B = (0, 2)$, $D = (0, -2)$. The diagonals lie along the axes, so they are perpendicular.

    To disprove "every", we just need one example that fails.

  2. $AB = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt5$ but $BC = \sqrt{16 + 4} = 2\sqrt5$.

    The sides are not all equal, so it is not a rhombus. (It is a kite.)

No. A kite with perpendicular diagonals need not be a rhombus.

Parallel plus equal, but not a parallelogram

A quadrilateral has one pair of parallel sides and the other pair of sides equal. Must it be a parallelogram?

  1. An isosceles trapezoid has bases parallel and legs equal.

    Look for a familiar shape meeting both conditions.

  2. Its legs are not parallel (they lean toward each other), so it is not a parallelogram.

    For example, bases $6$ and $14$ with legs $5$, as in §12.2.

No; an isosceles trapezoid is a counterexample.

Diagonals that bisect each other

Prove that if the diagonals of $ABCD$ bisect each other at $E$, then $ABCD$ is a parallelogram.

  1. $AE = EC$, $BE = ED$, and $\angle AEB = \angle CED$ (vertical angles). So $\triangle AEB \cong \triangle CED$ by SAS.

    Two triangles across from each other at $E$ share all the given equalities.

  2. Hence $\angle EAB = \angle ECD$, i.e. $\angle CAB = \angle ACD$.

    Corresponding parts of congruent triangles.

  3. These are alternate angles for lines $AB$ and $CD$ cut by $AC$, so $AB \parallel CD$. In the same way, $\triangle AED \cong \triangle CEB$ gives $AD \parallel BC$.

    Equal alternate angles force parallel lines; with both pairs parallel, $ABCD$ is a parallelogram.

Both pairs of opposite sides are parallel, so $ABCD$ is a parallelogram.

One pair both parallel and equal

In quadrilateral $ABCD$, $AB \parallel CD$ and $AB = CD$. Prove $ABCD$ is a parallelogram.

  1. Draw diagonal $AC$. Then $\angle BAC = \angle DCA$.

    Alternate angles, because $AB \parallel CD$.

  2. $\triangle BAC \cong \triangle DCA$ by SAS: $AB = CD$, the angles above, and the shared side $AC$.

    The diagonal is common to both triangles.

  3. So $\angle BCA = \angle DAC$, which are alternate angles for $BC$ and $AD$; hence $BC \parallel AD$.

    Now both pairs of opposite sides are parallel.

$ABCD$ is a parallelogram.

A parallelogram with perpendicular diagonals

Prove that a parallelogram whose diagonals are perpendicular is a rhombus.

  1. Let the diagonals meet at $E$. In a parallelogram they bisect each other, so $AE = EC$.

    This is the parallelogram fact we already proved.

  2. $\triangle AEB \cong \triangle CEB$ by SAS: $AE = CE$, right angles at $E$, and shared $BE$.

    Perpendicular diagonals give the right angles.

  3. So $AB = CB$. Opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal, so $AB = BC = CD = DA$.

    One pair of neighbouring sides equal is enough once opposite sides match.

All four sides are equal, so it is a rhombus.

The midpoint quadrilateral

A quadrilateral has diagonals of lengths $10$ and $14$. Find the perimeter of the quadrilateral formed by joining the midpoints of its sides in order.

  1. The midpoints of $AB$ and $BC$ are joined by a segment parallel to $AC$ and half as long: $\tfrac12 AC$.

    Midpoint segment in triangle $ABC$.

  2. Likewise the midpoints of $CD$ and $DA$ give $\tfrac12 AC$; the other two sides are each $\tfrac12 BD$.

    Use triangles $CDA$, $BCD$ and $DAB$ in the same way.

  3. Perimeter $= 2 \cdot \tfrac12(10) + 2 \cdot \tfrac12(14) = 24$.

    The perimeter equals the sum of the diagonals. (Opposite sides are parallel and equal, so it's always a parallelogram.)

$24$

A median, via a parallelogram

Triangle $ABC$ has $AB = 4$, $BC = 6$ and $AC = 8$. Find the length of median $BM$, where $M$ is the midpoint of $AC$.

  1. Extend $BM$ past $M$ to $P$ with $MP = BM$. Then $ABCP$ has diagonals $AC$ and $BP$ bisecting each other at $M$, so it is a parallelogram.

    Doubling the median creates a parallelogram with sides $4$ and $6$.

  2. Parallelogram law: $AC^2 + BP^2 = 2(4^2) + 2(6^2) = 104$.

    The sum of the squares of the diagonals equals the sum of the squares of the four sides.

  3. $64 + BP^2 = 104 \Rightarrow BP^2 = 40$, so $BP = 2\sqrt{10}$ and $BM = \sqrt{10}$.

    $BM$ is half of $BP$.

$BM = \sqrt{10}$

Doubling a median turns a triangle into a parallelogram.
