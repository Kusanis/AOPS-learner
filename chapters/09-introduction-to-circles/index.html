An Introduction to Circles

Geometry starts with the simplest curve there is: all the points at one fixed distance from a center. From that single idea come chords, tangents, arcs and sectors, the number $\pi$ that links a circle's size to its distance around, and the formulas you need for wheels, gears and anything else that spins.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • A circle is every point at distance $r$ (the radius) from its center. The diameter is $d = 2r$, the longest chord.
  • A line at distance $t$ from the center is a secant if $t < r$, a tangent if $t = r$, and misses if $t > r$. The perpendicular from the center bisects any chord.
  • Circumference $C = \pi d = 2\pi r$; area $A = \pi r^2$. To move between them, find $r$ first.
  • Scaling the radius by $k$ multiplies the circumference by $k$ and the area by $k^2$.
  • Arcs and sectors are fractions of the whole: same fraction of the circumference, same fraction of the area.
  • Tangent circles: centers are $r_1 + r_2$ apart (externally) or $|r_1 - r_2|$ apart (internally), and the point of tangency lies on the line of centers.
  • Speed of a point on a spinning circle $=$ (revolutions per unit time) $\times\, 2\pi r$. A wheel rolling without slipping moves $2\pi r$ per turn.

9.1 Parts of a Circle

Hook

Tie a goat to a post with a 5-metre rope. The edge of the grass it can reach is a circle: every point exactly 5 metres from the post. Pull a straight fence across that patch and you've drawn a chord; nudge the fence outward until it just grazes the edge and it becomes a tangent. Every word in this section describes something you could build in that field.

Concept

A circle is the set of all points at a fixed distance, the radius $r$, from a fixed point, the center. We name a circle by its center: circle $O$. Circles with the same center are concentric.

  • A chord is a segment with both endpoints on the circle. A diameter is a chord through the center; its length is $d = 2r$, and no chord is longer.
  • A secant is a line that crosses the circle at two points. A tangent touches it at exactly one point.
  • An arc is a piece of the circle itself. Two points $A$ and $B$ cut the circle into a shorter minor arc, written $\overset{\frown}{AB}$, and a longer major arc, named with a third point on it, such as $\overset{\frown}{ACB}$.
  • A sector is the region between two radii and their arc (a pizza slice). A circular segment is the region between a chord and its arc.

Whether a line is a secant or a tangent depends only on its distance $t$ from the center:

$t < r$: secant (two points) $\qquad t = r$: tangent (one point) $\qquad t > r$: no intersection.

The perpendicular from the center to a chord meets it at its midpoint. With the Pythagorean theorem (Chapter 11) this gives the chord length: a chord at distance $t$ from the center has half-length $\sqrt{r^2 - t^2}$.

The longest chord

Circle $O$ has radius $7$. What is its diameter? Could it have a chord of length $3$? Of length $15$?

  1. $d = 2r = 14$.

    A diameter is made of two radii end to end through the center.

  2. A chord of length $3$ is possible; one of length $15$ is not.

    The diameter is the longest chord, so every chord has length between $0$ and $14$.

Diameter $14$; a chord of length $3$ exists, but none of length $15$.

Naming arcs

Points $A$, $B$ and $C$ lie on a circle, with $C$ on the longer of the two arcs between $A$ and $B$. Which arc does $\overset{\frown}{AB}$ name, and how do you name the other?

  1. $\overset{\frown}{AB}$ is the minor arc (the short, highlighted one).

    With only two letters, the shorter arc is meant.

  2. The major arc is $\overset{\frown}{ACB}$.

    A third point on the arc says which way around the circle to go.

$\overset{\frown}{AB}$ is the minor arc; the major arc is $\overset{\frown}{ACB}$.

Secant, tangent, or neither?

A circle has radius $6$. Three lines are at distances $4$, $6$ and $9$ from its center. Describe how each meets the circle.

  1. Distance $4 < 6$: a secant, meeting the circle at two points.

    The closest point of the line is inside the circle, so the line must cross the circle going in and coming out.

  2. Distance $6 = 6$: a tangent, touching at one point.

    The closest point is exactly on the circle and every other point of the line is farther away.

  3. Distance $9 > 6$: no intersection.

    Even the closest point of the line is outside the circle.

Secant, tangent, and a line that misses the circle.

Length of a chord

In a circle of radius $5$, a chord is $3$ units from the center. How long is the chord?

  1. Drop the perpendicular from the center $O$ to the chord, meeting it at its midpoint $M$. Then $OM = 3$.

    The perpendicular from the center bisects the chord, splitting it into two equal halves.

  2. Join $O$ to an endpoint $A$: $OA = 5$ is a radius, and triangle $OMA$ has a right angle at $M$.

    A radius to the endpoint completes a right triangle.

  3. $AM = \sqrt{5^2 - 3^2} = \sqrt{16} = 4$, so the chord is $2 \cdot 4 = 8$.

    Pythagorean theorem (Chapter 11), then double the half-chord.

$8$

How far is the chord?

A chord of length $24$ lies in a circle of radius $13$. How far is it from the center?

  1. Half the chord is $12$.

    The perpendicular from the center hits the chord's midpoint.

  2. Distance $= \sqrt{13^2 - 12^2} = \sqrt{169 - 144} = 5$.

    Same right triangle as before, now solving for the other leg.

$5$

Radius, half-chord and distance to the center form a right triangle.

A chord that avoids an inner circle

Two concentric circles have radii $3$ and $5$. What is the longest chord of the larger circle that does not pass through the interior of the smaller circle?

  1. The chord must stay at distance at least $3$ from the center, and closer chords are longer. So the longest one is at distance exactly $3$: it is tangent to the small circle.

    Half-chord length $\sqrt{r^2 - t^2}$ grows as $t$ shrinks, so we want the smallest allowed $t$.

  2. Half-length $= \sqrt{5^2 - 3^2} = 4$, so the chord has length $8$.

    The radius of the small circle to the point of tangency is the perpendicular distance.

$8$

"Longest" or "shortest" questions about chords become questions about distance from the center.

9.2 Circumference, Area, and $\pi$

Hook

Wrap a string once around a can, then lay it along the can's top, across the middle. However big or small the can, the string covers the width a little more than three times. That "a little more than three" is the same for every circle in the universe, and it is called $\pi$.

Concept

The circumference $C$ is the distance around a circle. Every circle has the same ratio $C/d$; this constant is $\pi \approx 3.14159$. So

$$C = \pi d = 2\pi r \qquad\qquad A = \pi r^2.$$

Why $\pi r^2$? Cut a circle into many thin wedges and lay them alternately point-up and point-down. They form a shape that is nearly a parallelogram: its height is $r$ and its length is half the circumference, $\pi r$. Its area is $\pi r \cdot r = \pi r^2$, and the more wedges, the better the fit.

To go between $C$ and $A$, always pass through the radius: from $A = 49\pi$ get $r = 7$, then $C = 14\pi$. Keep answers exact in terms of $\pi$ unless asked to approximate.

An arc or sector that is a fraction $f$ of the whole circle has length $f \cdot 2\pi r$ and area $f \cdot \pi r^2$. And because area grows with $r^2$, doubling the radius doubles the circumference but quadruples the area.

Among all shapes with a given perimeter, the circle encloses the most area. Proving it is hard, but you can use the fact.

The two formulas

Find the circumference and area of a circle with radius $5$.

  1. $C = 2\pi r = 10\pi$.

    Circumference uses the radius once.

  2. $A = \pi r^2 = 25\pi$.

    Area uses the radius squared.

$C = 10\pi \approx 31.4$, $A = 25\pi \approx 78.5$

From area to circumference

A circle has area $49\pi$. Find its circumference.

  1. $\pi r^2 = 49\pi$, so $r^2 = 49$ and $r = 7$.

    Find the radius first; it unlocks everything else.

  2. $C = 2\pi(7) = 14\pi$.

    Now use the circumference formula.

$14\pi$

From circumference to area, without a nice radius

A circle has circumference $10$ (not $10\pi$). Find its area.

  1. $2\pi r = 10$, so $r = \dfrac{5}{\pi}$.

    The radius isn't a whole number this time; keep it exact.

  2. $A = \pi\left(\dfrac5\pi\right)^2 = \pi \cdot \dfrac{25}{\pi^2} = \dfrac{25}{\pi}$.

    One factor of $\pi$ cancels.

$\dfrac{25}{\pi} \approx 7.96$

When area equals circumference

For what radius is a circle's area numerically equal to its circumference?

  1. $\pi r^2 = 2\pi r$.

    Set the two formulas equal.

  2. Divide by $\pi r$ (with $r > 0$): $r = 2$.

    A real circle has positive radius, so dividing by $r$ is safe.

$r = 2$

Doubling the radius

A pizza's radius is doubled. By what factor do its crust length and its area change?

  1. Circumference: $2\pi(2r) = 2 \cdot (2\pi r)$, so it doubles.

    Circumference is proportional to $r$.

  2. Area: $\pi(2r)^2 = 4\pi r^2$, so it is multiplied by $4$.

    Area is proportional to $r^2$, and $2^2 = 4$.

Crust $\times 2$, area $\times 4$.

A circle in a square

A circle is drawn inside a square of side $10$, touching all four sides. Find the area inside the square but outside the circle.

  1. The circle's diameter equals the side, so $r = 5$ and the circle's area is $25\pi$.

    The circle touches opposite sides, so its diameter spans the square.

  2. Region area $= 10^2 - 25\pi = 100 - 25\pi$.

    Subtract the inner shape from the outer one.

$100 - 25\pi \approx 21.5$

Arcs and sectors as fractions

Points $A$ and $B$ split a circle of radius $12$ into two arcs whose lengths are in the ratio $1 : 3$. Find both arc lengths and the area of the smaller sector $AOB$.

  1. The minor arc is $\frac14$ of the circle and the major arc is $\frac34$.

    Ratio $1:3$ means $4$ equal parts in all.

  2. $C = 24\pi$, so the arcs are $6\pi$ and $18\pi$.

    Take each fraction of the circumference.

  3. Sector area $= \frac14 \cdot \pi (12)^2 = 36\pi$.

    The sector is the same fraction of the disk as its arc is of the circle.

Arcs $6\pi$ and $18\pi$; sector area $36\pi$.

The best shape for a fence

With $20$ metres of fencing, how much more area can you enclose with a circle than with a square?

  1. Square: side $5$, area $25$.

    The perimeter is shared equally by four sides.

  2. Circle: $2\pi r = 20$, so $r = \dfrac{10}{\pi}$ and $A = \pi \cdot \dfrac{100}{\pi^2} = \dfrac{100}{\pi} \approx 31.8$.

    Same route as before: perimeter, then radius, then area.

  3. Difference: $\dfrac{100}{\pi} - 25 \approx 6.8$ square metres.

    The circle encloses the most area of any shape with that perimeter.

$\dfrac{100}{\pi} - 25 \approx 6.8$ m² more

One wire or two?

A $30$ cm wire is cut into pieces of $10$ cm and $20$ cm, and each piece is bent into a circle. Find the total area of the two circles, and compare it with one circle made from the whole wire.

  1. A circle of circumference $L$ has $r = \dfrac{L}{2\pi}$ and area $\pi\left(\dfrac{L}{2\pi}\right)^2 = \dfrac{L^2}{4\pi}$.

    Work out the general formula once instead of three times.

  2. Two circles: $\dfrac{10^2}{4\pi} + \dfrac{20^2}{4\pi} = \dfrac{100 + 400}{4\pi} = \dfrac{125}{\pi} \approx 39.8$ cm².

    Substitute $L = 10$ and $L = 20$.

  3. One circle: $\dfrac{30^2}{4\pi} = \dfrac{225}{\pi} \approx 71.6$ cm².

    Since area grows like $L^2$, and $30^2 > 10^2 + 20^2$, keeping the wire whole wins.

Two circles: $\dfrac{125}{\pi}$ cm²; one circle: $\dfrac{225}{\pi}$ cm², much more.

Area from a perimeter $L$ is $\frac{L^2}{4\pi}$: squares of lengths add up to less than the square of their sum.

The area of a ring

Two concentric circles form a ring. A chord of the outer circle is tangent to the inner circle and has length $10$. Find the area of the ring.

  1. Let the radii be $R$ (outer) and $r$ (inner). The ring's area is $\pi R^2 - \pi r^2 = \pi(R^2 - r^2)$.

    Outer disk minus inner disk.

  2. The inner radius to the point of tangency meets the chord at its midpoint, at a right angle. So $r^2 + 5^2 = R^2$.

    Center, point of tangency and chord endpoint form a right triangle with legs $r$ and $5$ and hypotenuse $R$.

  3. $R^2 - r^2 = 25$, so the ring's area is $25\pi$.

    We never needed $R$ or $r$ separately, only their squared difference.

$25\pi$

Look for the combination the answer needs ($R^2 - r^2$) instead of each unknown.

9.3 Tangent Circles

Hook

Push two coins together on a table until they just touch. Where exactly do they touch? On the straight line joining their centers, and the centers are as far apart as the two radii put together. That one observation solves a surprising number of puzzles about coins, bubbles and gears.

Concept

Two circles are tangent if they touch at exactly one point. The point of tangency always lies on the line through the two centers.

Externally tangent (side by side): distance between centers $= r_1 + r_2$.
Internally tangent (one inside the other): distance between centers $= |r_1 - r_2|$.

More generally, if $d$ is the distance between the centers: $d > r_1 + r_2$ means the circles are separate; $|r_1 - r_2| < d < r_1 + r_2$ means they cross at two points; $d < |r_1 - r_2|$ means one lies inside the other without touching.

When several circles are tangent, join their centers. The segments between centers have lengths that are sums (or differences) of radii, which turns a picture of circles into a polygon you can calculate with.

Externally tangent

Circles of radii $3$ and $5$ are externally tangent. How far apart are their centers?

  1. The segment between centers passes through the point of tangency.

    The point of tangency lies on the line of centers.

  2. It is one radius of each circle: $3 + 5 = 8$.

    From one center to the touching point is $3$; from there to the other center is $5$.

$8$

Internally tangent

A circle of radius $3$ sits inside a circle of radius $8$ and is tangent to it. How far apart are the centers?

  1. From the big center to the touching point is $8$; the small center lies on that same segment, $3$ from the touching point.

    Again the point of tangency is on the line of centers.

  2. Distance $= 8 - 3 = 5$.

    For internal tangency the radii overlap, so we subtract.

$5$

Three coins in a triangle

Circles of radii $1$, $2$ and $3$ are each externally tangent to the other two. Find the side lengths and perimeter of the triangle formed by their centers.

  1. Sides: $1 + 2 = 3$, $1 + 3 = 4$ and $2 + 3 = 5$.

    Each pair of tangent circles has centers $r_i + r_j$ apart.

  2. Perimeter $= 3 + 4 + 5 = 12$.

    Each radius is used twice: $2(1 + 2 + 3) = 12$. (It's a $3$-$4$-$5$ right triangle, too.)

Sides $3, 4, 5$; perimeter $12$.

Find the hidden radius

Circle $B$ lies inside circle $A$ and is internally tangent to it. Circle $A$ has radius $10$ and the centers are $4$ apart. Find the radius of circle $B$.

  1. Internal tangency: $AB = 10 - r_B$.

    The distance between centers is the difference of the radii.

  2. $4 = 10 - r_B$, so $r_B = 6$.

    Solve the linear equation.

$6$

Radii from the distances

Three circles are mutually externally tangent. Their centers are $5$, $6$ and $7$ apart (for the pairs $PQ$, $QR$, $PR$). Find the three radii.

  1. Let the radii be $p, q, r$: $p + q = 5$, $q + r = 6$, $p + r = 7$.

    Each center distance is a sum of two radii.

  2. Add all three: $2(p + q + r) = 18$, so $p + q + r = 9$.

    Every radius appears in exactly two of the equations.

  3. $r = 9 - 5 = 4$, $p = 9 - 6 = 3$, $q = 9 - 7 = 2$.

    Subtract each pair-sum from the total.

$p = 3$, $q = 2$, $r = 4$

A symmetric system: add everything, then subtract each equation.

A circle nestled among three

A circle of radius $6$ contains two circles of radius $3$ whose centers lie on one diameter; each small circle is internally tangent to the big one, and they are tangent to each other at the big circle's center. Find the radius of a circle that is internally tangent to the big circle and externally tangent to both small ones.

  1. Put the big center at $O$ and the small centers at $3$ units on either side. By symmetry the new circle's center $X$ lies on the perpendicular to the diameter at $O$. Let its radius be $x$.

    The setup is symmetric, so the new circle must be centered on the axis of symmetry.

  2. Internal tangency to the big circle: $OX = 6 - x$. External tangency to a small circle: distance from its center to $X$ is $3 + x$.

    Translate each tangency into a distance between centers.

  3. The right triangle with legs $3$ and $OX$ gives $3^2 + (6 - x)^2 = (3 + x)^2$.

    The segment from $O$ to a small center is perpendicular to $OX$. Pythagorean theorem (Chapter 11).

  4. $9 + 36 - 12x + x^2 = 9 + 6x + x^2$, so $36 = 18x$ and $x = 2$.

    The $x^2$ terms cancel, leaving a linear equation. Check: $OX = 4$ and $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5 = 3 + 2$. ✓

$2$

Tangency gives distances between centers; then look for a right triangle.

9.4 Spinning and Rolling

Hook

On a merry-go-round, the horses on the outside and the ones near the middle both go around once per turn. Yet the outer riders feel the wind much more. They travel a bigger circle in the same time, so they move faster. Turning "how fast it spins" into "how fast it moves" is just circumference times turns.

Concept

The angular speed of a spinning object says how fast it turns, for example in revolutions per minute (rpm). Every point on a rigid spinning disk has the same angular speed. The linear speed of a point is how fast it actually moves along its path.

In one revolution a point at distance $r$ from the center travels one circumference, $2\pi r$. So

$$\text{linear speed} = (\text{revolutions per unit time}) \times 2\pi r.$$

A wheel that rolls without slipping lays its rim along the ground, so it moves forward $2\pi r$ per revolution. Two pulleys joined by a belt (or two gears in contact) share the same rim speed, so their revolution rates are inversely proportional to their radii.

Watch the units: keep track of minutes vs. hours and centimetres vs. metres, and convert at the end (Chapter 4's conversion factors).

One turn of a wheel

A wheel of radius $30$ cm rolls without slipping through one revolution. How far does it move?

  1. Distance $= 2\pi r = 60\pi$ cm.

    Rolling lays the whole rim along the ground once.

  2. $60\pi \approx 188.5$ cm.

    Approximate only at the end.

$60\pi \approx 188.5$ cm

A carousel horse

A carousel makes $4$ revolutions per minute. How fast does a horse $5$ m from the center move?

  1. One revolution: $2\pi(5) = 10\pi$ m.

    The horse travels one circumference per turn.

  2. Speed $= 4 \times 10\pi = 40\pi \approx 126$ m per minute.

    Multiply the distance per turn by the turns per minute.

$40\pi \approx 126$ m/min

How many turns?

A bicycle wheel has diameter $70$ cm. About how many revolutions does it make in $1$ km?

  1. Distance per revolution: $\pi d = 70\pi$ cm.

    Here the diameter is given, so use $C = \pi d$.

  2. $1$ km $= 100\,000$ cm, so the number of turns is $\dfrac{100\,000}{70\pi} \approx 454.7$.

    Convert to the same units before dividing.

About $455$ revolutions

Same spin, different speeds

Two ants sit on a spinning disk, $2$ cm and $6$ cm from the center. How do their speeds compare?

  1. Both complete each revolution at the same time.

    The disk is rigid, so all of it has the same angular speed.

  2. Per revolution they travel $4\pi$ and $12\pi$ cm, so the outer ant is $3$ times as fast.

    Linear speed is proportional to the distance from the center.

The outer ant is $3$ times as fast.

From rpm to km/h

A car's wheels have radius $0.3$ m and turn at $1000$ rpm. How fast is the car going, in km/h?

  1. Per minute: $1000 \times 2\pi(0.3) = 600\pi$ m.

    Rolling without slipping: the car moves one circumference per wheel turn.

  2. Per hour: $600\pi \times 60 = 36\,000\pi$ m $= 36\pi$ km.

    Multiply by $60$ minutes per hour, then divide by $1000$ metres per kilometre.

  3. $36\pi \approx 113$ km/h.

    Approximate at the end.

$36\pi \approx 113$ km/h

A belt between pulleys

A belt joins a pulley of radius $4$ cm to one of radius $10$ cm. The small pulley turns at $150$ rpm. How fast does the large one turn?

  1. The belt moves at one speed, so both rims have the same linear speed.

    The belt doesn't stretch or slip, so every point of it moves at the same speed.

  2. Small rim: $150 \times 2\pi(4) = 1200\pi$ cm/min.

    Linear speed = turns per minute $\times$ circumference.

  3. Large pulley: $N \times 2\pi(10) = 1200\pi$, so $N = 60$ rpm.

    Equivalently, rpm is inversely proportional to radius: $150 \cdot \frac{4}{10} = 60$.

$60$ rpm

Connected rims share linear speed; rates of turning scale inversely with radius.

The coin rotation puzzle

A coin rolls without slipping once around the outside of an identical coin that is held still. How many times does the moving coin turn around its own center?

  1. Let each radius be $r$. The moving coin's center stays $2r$ from the fixed center, so it travels a circle of radius $2r$, a distance of $2\pi(2r) = 4\pi r$.

    Externally tangent circles have centers $r + r$ apart.

  2. The moving coin's center advances $2\pi r$ along its path for each full turn of the coin.

    For a wheel rolling without slipping, one turn moves its center one circumference along its path. (The path is curved, but the count of turns comes from the center's travel.)

  3. Turns $= \dfrac{4\pi r}{2\pi r} = 2$.

    Try it with two coins: the head on the moving coin is upright twice.

$2$ turns

For rolling, follow the center: turns = (distance the center travels) ÷ (circumference).
