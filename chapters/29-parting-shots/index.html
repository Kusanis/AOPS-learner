Chapter 29 · Advanced Topics

Parting Shots

The book ends with a mixed bag of problems and no new theory: you have to recognize which tool each problem needs. This page groups that skill into three habits: translate words into equations, look for hidden structure before computing, and count or bound carefully when the answer must be guaranteed.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • Name the unknown, write each sentence as an equation, and check the answer against the story.
  • Rates add when things work together: (work rate) $\times$ (time) $=$ work, and distance $=$ rate $\times$ time, with relative speed for objects moving toward each other.
  • Clock hands: the minute hand moves $6^\circ$ per minute, the hour hand $\tfrac12^\circ$ per minute.
  • Before computing, look for structure: difference of squares, symmetric pairs, telescoping sums, geometric series, powers of $10$.
  • For "how many," organize cases; for "guarantee," find the worst case; for a maximum, give a bound and an example.
  • In take-away games, work backward from the end to find the positions you want to leave your opponent.

29.1 Translate, Then Solve

Hook

Look at a clock at 3:00 and the hands make a right angle. When is the next time they make one? The minute hand will need to "catch up" past the hour hand, which keeps creeping forward. The question is really a race between two runners, and once you see it that way, it is one line of algebra.

Concept

Most word problems in this book yield to the same routine:

  1. Name the unknown(s), with units.
  2. Translate each fact into an equation. Useful templates: total $=$ count $\times$ average; amount of a substance $=$ concentration $\times$ volume; distance $=$ rate $\times$ time; work $=$ rate $\times$ time.
  3. Solve, then check the answer in the original story (not just in your equations).

$$\text{rate}_{\text{together}} = \text{rate}_1 + \text{rate}_2, \qquad \text{minute hand: } 6^\circ/\text{min}, \quad \text{hour hand: } 0.5^\circ/\text{min}.$$

For the clock, measure both hands in degrees clockwise from 12. At $h$ hours and $m$ minutes the minute hand is at $6m$ degrees and the hour hand at $30h + 0.5m$ degrees. The angle between them is the difference, or $360^\circ$ minus it if that is smaller.

Average speed for a whole trip is total distance divided by total time, not the average of the speeds.

Three test scores

Lena's second test score was $3$ more than her first, and her third was $6$ more than her second. Her average was $80$. What were her scores?

  1. Let the first score be $x$. Then the scores are $x$, $x + 3$, $x + 9$.

    Name one unknown and express the others with it.

  2. $x + (x + 3) + (x + 9) = 3 \times 80 = 240$, so $3x + 12 = 240$ and $x = 76$.

    Total $=$ count $\times$ average.

  3. Scores: $76, 79, 85$. Check: $\frac{76 + 79 + 85}{3} = 80$. ✓

    Check in the story.

$76$, $79$ and $85$

A linear cost

Making $10$ items costs 55 dollars and making $30$ costs 115 dollars. If cost is a linear function of the number made, what do $50$ items cost?

  1. Each extra item costs $\frac{115 - 55}{30 - 10} = 3$ dollars.

    The slope of a line through the two data points.

  2. Fixed cost: $55 - 3 \cdot 10 = 25$ dollars, so $C(n) = 25 + 3n$.

    Find the intercept from either point.

  3. $C(50) = 25 + 150 = 175$ dollars.

    Evaluate.

175 dollars

Next in the book: Problem 550.

Strengthening a mixture

A jug holds $8$ litres of drink that is $25\%$ juice. How much pure juice must be added to make it $40\%$ juice?

  1. Juice now: $0.25 \times 8 = 2$ litres. Add $x$ litres of pure juice.

    Track the amount of juice, which is what changes in a known way.

  2. $2 + x = 0.40(8 + x)$.

    Juice after $=$ $40\%$ of the new total volume.

  3. $2 + x = 3.2 + 0.4x \Rightarrow 0.6x = 1.2 \Rightarrow x = 2$.

    Check: $4$ litres of juice in $10$ litres is $40\%$. ✓

$2$ litres

Next in the book: Problem 545.

The angle at 4:40

What angle do the hands of a clock make at 4:40?

  1. Minute hand: $6 \times 40 = 240^\circ$.

    $6^\circ$ per minute from the 12.

  2. Hour hand: $30 \times 4 + 0.5 \times 40 = 140^\circ$.

    The hour hand has moved two-thirds of the way from 4 to 5.

  3. Angle: $240 - 140 = 100^\circ$.

    Since $100 < 180$, this is the smaller angle.

$100^\circ$

Next in the book: Problem 561.

Trains passing

A $600$-foot train going $30$ mph meets a train going $45$ mph in the opposite direction on a parallel track. From the moment their fronts meet until their backs separate takes $12$ seconds. How long is the second train?

  1. Relative speed: $30 + 45 = 75$ mph $= \dfrac{75 \times 5280}{3600} = 110$ ft/s.

    Moving toward each other, the speeds add. Convert to feet per second to match the other units.

  2. To pass completely, together they cover the sum of their lengths: $600 + L = 110 \times 12 = 1320$.

    Picture one train standing still and the other sliding past at $110$ ft/s.

  3. $L = 720$ feet.

    Solve.

$720$ feet

Next in the book: Problem 547.

Two taps and a drain

Tap A fills a tank in $6$ hours and tap B in $4$ hours. A drain empties a full tank in $12$ hours. With all three open, how long does it take to fill the empty tank?

  1. Rates in tanks per hour: A $= \frac16$, B $= \frac14$, drain $= -\frac1{12}$.

    A job done in $t$ hours has rate $\frac1t$; the drain works against the taps.

  2. Combined: $\frac{2}{12} + \frac{3}{12} - \frac{1}{12} = \frac{4}{12} = \frac13$ tank per hour.

    Rates add.

  3. Time $= 1 \div \frac13 = 3$ hours.

    Work $=$ rate $\times$ time.

$3$ hours

Average speed is not the average of speeds

A cyclist rides to a town at $30$ km/h and back along the same road at $60$ km/h. What is the average speed for the round trip?

  1. Let each leg be $d$ km. Time: $\frac{d}{30} + \frac{d}{60} = \frac{3d}{60} = \frac{d}{20}$ hours.

    The distance is unknown, but it will cancel.

  2. Average speed $= \dfrac{2d}{d/20} = 40$ km/h.

    Total distance over total time. The slow leg takes twice as long, so it counts twice as much.

$40$ km/h

A right angle between 2 and 3

At what time strictly between 2:00 and 3:00 are the clock hands perpendicular?

  1. At $t$ minutes after 2:00: minute hand at $6t$, hour hand at $60 + 0.5t$ degrees.

    Set up both positions as functions of one variable.

  2. The minute hand starts $60^\circ$ behind and gains $5.5^\circ$ per minute, so it can't fall to $90^\circ$ behind. It must be $90^\circ$ ahead: $6t - (60 + 0.5t) = 90$.

    Decide which hand is ahead before writing the equation; the other possibility is impossible here.

  3. $5.5t = 150 \Rightarrow t = \frac{300}{11} = 27\tfrac{3}{11}$ minutes.

    The next solution, $270^\circ$ ahead, gives $t = 60$: exactly 3:00, which is excluded.

At $2{:}27\tfrac{3}{11}$, about 2:27:16.

Clock problems are chase problems: the minute hand gains $5.5^\circ$ per minute on the hour hand.

29.2 Look for Structure

Hook

Compute $1001^2 - 999^2$. You could square two four-digit numbers and subtract. Or you could notice it's a difference of squares and finish in your head. Contest problems are built so that the brute-force route is long and the clever route is short. Training yourself to pause and look first is the single most valuable habit in this book.

Concept

Before you start crunching, scan for these patterns:

  • Factor forms: $a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)$; perfect squares like $(a + b)^2$.
  • Symmetry and pairing: terms that cancel in pairs, like $-k$ and $k$, or that sum to a constant.
  • Telescoping: write each term as a difference so that neighbours cancel, as in $\dfrac{1}{k(k+2)} = \dfrac12\left(\dfrac1k - \dfrac1{k+2}\right)$.
  • Geometric series: $a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \dfrac{a}{1 - r}$ for $|r| < 1$.
  • Powers of $10$ and cycles: combine $2$s with $5$s; last digits of powers repeat.

$$\text{Ask first: what does this expression } \textit{look like}\text{?}$$

Use the factorization

If $x + y = 9$ and $x^2 - y^2 = 27$, find $3x - y$.

  1. $x^2 - y^2 = (x + y)(x - y) = 9(x - y) = 27$, so $x - y = 3$.

    The second equation contains the first as a factor.

  2. Adding and subtracting: $x = 6$, $y = 3$, so $3x - y = 15$.

    Two linear equations in two unknowns.

$15$

Next in the book: Problem 549.

Mental squaring

Compute $1001^2 - 999^2$.

  1. $(1001 - 999)(1001 + 999) = 2 \times 2000$.

    Difference of squares.

  2. $= 4000$.

    No large multiplication needed.

$4000$

Digits on the pages

How many digits are used to number the pages of a book from $1$ to $150$?

  1. Pages $1$–$9$: $9$ one-digit numbers, $9$ digits.

    Group pages by how many digits they have.

  2. Pages $10$–$99$: $90$ numbers, $180$ digits.

    $99 - 10 + 1 = 90$.

  3. Pages $100$–$150$: $51$ numbers, $153$ digits. Total $9 + 180 + 153 = 342$.

    $150 - 100 + 1 = 51$; don't forget the $+1$.

$342$

Pairs that cancel

Find the mean of all integers from $-50$ to $51$ inclusive.

  1. The numbers $-50, \dots, 50$ cancel in pairs ($-k$ with $k$), leaving a sum of $51$.

    Symmetry does the adding for us.

  2. There are $51 - (-50) + 1 = 102$ numbers, so the mean is $\frac{51}{102} = \frac12$.

    Mean $=$ sum $\div$ count. (Also: the mean of an evenly spaced list is the average of its ends, $\frac{-50 + 51}{2}$.)

$\tfrac12$

Next in the book: Problem 543.

A telescoping sum

Evaluate $\dfrac{1}{1 \cdot 3} + \dfrac{1}{3 \cdot 5} + \dfrac{1}{5 \cdot 7} + \cdots + \dfrac{1}{19 \cdot 21}$.

  1. $\dfrac{1}{k(k+2)} = \dfrac12\left(\dfrac1k - \dfrac1{k+2}\right)$.

    Check: $\frac1k - \frac1{k+2} = \frac{2}{k(k+2)}$.

  2. The sum is

    $$\frac12\left[\left(1 - \tfrac13\right) + \left(\tfrac13 - \tfrac15\right) + \cdots + \left(\tfrac1{19} - \tfrac1{21}\right)\right].$$

    Every middle fraction appears once with $+$ and once with $-$.

  3. $= \dfrac12\left(1 - \dfrac1{21}\right) = \dfrac{10}{21}$.

    Only the first and last pieces survive.

$\tfrac{10}{21}$

Squares forever

A square has side $4$. A second square joins the midpoints of its sides, a third joins the midpoints of the second's sides, and so on forever. What is the total area of all the squares?

  1. Each new square has half the area of the previous one.

    The midpoint square's corners cut off four triangles that together make up half the old square.

  2. Areas: $16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + \cdots$.

    A geometric series with first term $16$ and ratio $\frac12$.

  3. Sum $= \dfrac{16}{1 - \frac12} = 32$.

    The infinite geometric series formula.

$32$

Next in the book: Problems 532 and 542.

A hidden perfect square

Simplify $\sqrt{7 + 4\sqrt3}$.

  1. Look for $(a + b\sqrt3)^2 = a^2 + 3b^2 + 2ab\sqrt3$ with $a^2 + 3b^2 = 7$ and $2ab = 4$.

    Guess that the inside is a perfect square and match the rational and radical parts.

  2. $a = 2$, $b = 1$ works: $(2 + \sqrt3)^2 = 4 + 3 + 4\sqrt3$. ✓

    $ab = 2$ suggests trying small integer pairs.

$2 + \sqrt3$

Counting digits without multiplying

How many digits does $2^{20} \cdot 5^{15}$ have?

  1. $2^{20} \cdot 5^{15} = 2^5 \cdot (2 \cdot 5)^{15} = 32 \times 10^{15}$.

    Pair each $5$ with a $2$ to make a $10$.

  2. $32$ followed by $15$ zeros has $2 + 15 = 17$ digits.

    Multiplying by $10^{15}$ appends fifteen zeros.

$17$

Next in the book: Problem 569.

The last two digits

Find the last two digits of $7^{2026}$.

  1. $7^2 = 49$, $7^4 = 2401$. So $7^4$ ends in $01$.

    Compute small powers, keeping only the last two digits, until a pattern appears.

  2. $2026 = 4 \times 506 + 2$, so $7^{2026} = (7^4)^{506} \cdot 7^2$.

    Split the exponent around the cycle length.

  3. $(\ldots 01)^{506}$ ends in $01$, and $01 \times 49$ ends in $49$.

    A number ending in $01$ times another ending in $01$ still ends in $01$ (Chapter 5's modular arithmetic).

$49$

Huge exponents usually hide a short repeating cycle.

29.3 Count, Bound and Play

Hook

Two players face a pile of $21$ sticks. Each turn, a player removes $1$, $2$ or $3$; whoever takes the last stick loses. One of the players can force a win no matter what the other does, and figuring out which one needs no luck, only careful counting backward from the end. Try it in the explorer below.

Concept

The last group of problems asks you to count exactly, to guarantee something, or to find a winning strategy. The tools all come from Chapters 25–28:

  • Count by cases that don't overlap, or by complements.
  • Bound and construct: a maximum needs an upper bound and an example that reaches it.
  • Invariants and parity: a quantity that can't change (or always stays even) rules out whole families of outcomes.
  • Work backward in games. A position is losing for the player about to move if every move leads to a winning position for the opponent; it is winning if some move leads to a losing one.

$$\text{take } 1 \text{ to } k \text{ each turn: positions repeat with period } k + 1.$$

If the player who takes the last stick loses, the positions you want to leave for your opponent are $1, k + 2, 2k + 3, \dots$, the numbers that are $1$ more than a multiple of $k + 1$. If taking the last stick wins, they are the multiples of $k + 1$.

Forming a committee

A club has $5$ seniors and $3$ juniors. How many $3$-person committees have at least $2$ seniors?

  1. Exactly $2$ seniors: $\dbinom52\dbinom31 = 10 \times 3 = 30$.

    Split into non-overlapping cases by the number of seniors.

  2. Exactly $3$ seniors: $\dbinom53 = 10$.

    The other case.

  3. Total: $30 + 10 = 40$.

    Add the cases.

$40$

Next in the book: Problem 541.

Fence posts around a field

Posts are placed every $3$ feet around a $24$ ft by $15$ ft rectangular field, with a post at each corner. How many posts are needed?

  1. Perimeter: $2(24 + 15) = 78$ feet, so there are $78 \div 3 = 26$ gaps.

    Both side lengths are multiples of $3$, so the corners land on posts.

  2. Around a closed loop, posts and gaps alternate and come back to the start, so there are $26$ posts.

    The "one extra post" of a straight fence disappears when the ends meet.

$26$

Next in the book: Problem 564.

Twenty sticks

From a pile of $20$ sticks, players alternately remove $1$, $2$ or $3$. Whoever takes the last stick loses. Should you go first, and if so, what is your first move?

  1. Facing $1$ stick, you must take it and lose. So leaving $1$ for your opponent wins.

    Start from the end of the game.

  2. Facing $5$: any move leaves $2$, $3$ or $4$, and the opponent can then leave you $1$. So $5$ is losing to face. Likewise $9, 13, 17$.

    Whatever you take ($t$), the opponent takes $4 - t$, keeping the pattern every $4$.

  3. Go first and take $3$, leaving $17$. Then always answer a move of $t$ with $4 - t$.

    $20$ is not of the form $4m + 1$, so it is a winning position to face.

Go first and take $3$ sticks.

Next in the book: Problem 563.

Lines in the plane

Six lines are drawn in a plane. What is the greatest possible number of intersection points? Into how many regions can they cut the plane, at most?

  1. Each pair of lines meets at most once: $\dbinom62 = 15$ points, reached when no two are parallel and no three meet at a point.

    Bound by counting pairs; the "general position" arrangement reaches it.

  2. Adding the $k$th line, crossing the $k - 1$ earlier ones, splits $k$ regions in two. Regions: $1 + (1 + 2 + \cdots + 6) = 1 + 21 = 22$.

    The new line is cut into $k$ pieces by the $k - 1$ crossings, and each piece divides one region.

$15$ intersection points; $22$ regions.

When the last stick wins

Now the player who takes the last stick wins. Players take $1$ to $4$ sticks from a pile of $37$. What should the first player do?

  1. Facing $0$ sticks means your opponent just took the last one: you've lost. Facing $1$–$4$, take them all and win.

    Work backward from the end again, with the new rule.

  2. Facing $5$: every move leaves $1$–$4$ for the opponent, who wins. So multiples of $5$ are losing to face.

    Answering $t$ with $5 - t$ keeps the opponent on multiples of $5$.

  3. $37 = 35 + 2$: take $2$, leaving $35$.

    Leave a multiple of $5$, and keep doing so.

Take $2$ sticks, then always answer $t$ with $5 - t$.

A parity invariant

At a party, some pairs of guests shake hands. Prove that the number of guests who shook an odd number of hands is even.

  1. Add up every guest's handshake count. Each handshake involves two people, so the total is $2H$, where $H$ is the number of handshakes.

    Count the same thing (hand-in-handshake participations) two ways.

  2. The total is even. The even counts add to an even number, so the odd counts must also add to an even number.

    Subtracting an even number from an even number leaves an even number.

  3. A sum of odd numbers is even only when there is an even number of them.

    Odd $+$ odd $=$ even, so odd numbers pair off.

The number of guests with an odd count is even.

Peaceful kings

What is the largest number of kings that can be placed on an $8 \times 8$ chessboard so that no two attack each other? (A king attacks all squares touching its own, including diagonally.)

  1. Bound: split the board into sixteen $2 \times 2$ blocks. The four squares of a block all touch each other, so each block holds at most one king: at most $16$.

    Choose regions where two kings would always attack: pigeonhole in reverse.

  2. Example: put a king in the top-left square of every block (rows and columns $1, 3, 5, 7$). Any two are at least $2$ squares apart in some direction, so none attack.

    The bound is reached, so it is the maximum.

$16$

Bound with a partition, then match the bound with a construction.

Next in the book: Problem 557.

Two groups with equal sums

Ten different numbers are chosen from $1, 2, \dots, 100$. Prove that two non-empty groups of them, with no number in common, have the same sum.

  1. The ten numbers have $2^{10} = 1024$ subsets.

    These will be the pigeons.

  2. A subset's sum is between $0$ and $91 + 92 + \cdots + 100 = 955$: only $956$ possible values.

    These are the holes. The largest sum uses the ten biggest possible numbers.

  3. $1024 > 956$, so two different subsets $A$ and $B$ have the same sum.

    Pigeonhole.

  4. Remove the numbers they share from both. The sums stay equal, the groups no longer overlap, and neither is empty.

    Removing the same numbers from both lowers both sums equally. If one became empty, its sum would be $0$, forcing the other to be empty too, which would mean $A = B$.

Two disjoint non-empty groups with equal sums always exist.

Subsets make excellent pigeons: there are exponentially many of them.
