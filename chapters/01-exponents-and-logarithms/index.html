Chapter 1 · Algebra & Number

Exponents and Logarithms

Exponents are shorthand for repeated multiplication, but the shorthand turns out to be smarter than the thing it abbreviates. In this chapter you'll stretch exponents to zero, negatives and fractions, tidy up radicals, and meet the logarithm: the tool that asks "what exponent?"

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • Same base: multiply → add exponents, divide → subtract, power of a power → multiply: $a^m a^n = a^{m+n}$, $\dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$, $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$.
  • $a^0 = 1$ and $a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}$ (for $a \ne 0$) keep those rules working everywhere.
  • $a^{p/q} = \left(\sqrt[q]{a}\right)^p$: take the root first to keep numbers small.
  • Simplify $\sqrt{n}$ by pulling out perfect-square factors; prime factorization finds them all.
  • Rationalize with a matching root, or with a conjugate: $(a+\sqrt b)(a-\sqrt b) = a^2 - b$.
  • $\log_b x = y \iff b^y = x$. Logs turn products into sums, powers into multiples, and change base with $\log_b x = \dfrac{\log_c x}{\log_c b}$.
  • To compare or solve, rewrite everything with a common base or a common exponent.

1.1 Integer Exponents

Hook

A sheet of paper is about $0.1$ mm thick. Fold it in half, then in half again, and keep going. After 42 folds the stack would be about $0.1 \times 2^{42}$ mm thick, which is farther than the distance to the Moon. Doubling 42 times is far stronger than it sounds, and exponents let us write it in four characters.

Concept

Just as multiplication is shorthand for repeated addition, an exponent is shorthand for repeated multiplication: $a^n$ means $n$ copies of $a$ multiplied together. In $a^n$, $a$ is the base and $n$ is the exponent.

Counting copies explains every rule. $a^3 \cdot a^4$ is three $a$'s times four $a$'s, which is seven $a$'s, so $a^3a^4 = a^7$. Dividing cancels copies: $\dfrac{a^5}{a^2} = a^3$. And $(a^2)^3$ is three groups of two $a$'s, or $a^6$.

$$a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n} \qquad \frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n} \qquad (a^m)^n = a^{mn} \qquad (ab)^n = a^n b^n$$

What should $a^0$ mean? Use the division rule: $\dfrac{a^3}{a^3} = a^{3-3} = a^0$, but anything divided by itself is $1$. So for $a \ne 0$ we define $a^0 = 1$. In the same way, $\dfrac{a^0}{a^n} = a^{-n}$, which forces

$$a^0 = 1, \qquad a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n} \qquad (a \neq 0).$$

Two traps to watch for. First, $-3^2$ means $-(3^2) = -9$, while $(-3)^2 = 9$: the exponent binds tighter than the minus sign. Second, towers are read from the top down: $2^{3^2} = 2^{9}$, not $(2^3)^2 = 2^6$.

Combine powers of the same base

Evaluate $\dfrac{3^4 \cdot 3^2}{3^5}$.

  1. Multiply the top: $3^4 \cdot 3^2 = 3^{4+2} = 3^6$.

    Four 3's times two 3's is six 3's, so the exponents add.

  2. Divide: $\dfrac{3^6}{3^5} = 3^{6-5} = 3^1 = 3$.

    Five of the six 3's on top cancel with the five on the bottom.

$3$

Where does the minus sign go?

Evaluate $(-2)^5$ and $-2^4$.

  1. $(-2)^5 = (-2)(-2)(-2)(-2)(-2) = -32$.

    The parentheses make $-2$ the base. An odd number of negative factors leaves a negative product.

  2. $-2^4 = -(2^4) = -16$.

    Without parentheses the exponent applies only to the $2$; the minus sign is applied afterward.

$(-2)^5 = -32$ and $-2^4 = -16$.

Zero and negative exponents

Evaluate $5^{-2} + 2^{-3} \cdot 7^0$.

  1. $5^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{5^2} = \dfrac{1}{25}$ and $2^{-3} = \dfrac{1}{8}$.

    A negative exponent means "reciprocal of the positive power."

  2. $7^0 = 1$, so the sum is $\dfrac{1}{25} + \dfrac{1}{8}$.

    Any nonzero number to the zero power is $1$.

  3. $\dfrac{1}{25} + \dfrac{1}{8} = \dfrac{8}{200} + \dfrac{25}{200} = \dfrac{33}{200}$.

    Use the common denominator $200 = 25 \cdot 8$.

$\dfrac{33}{200}$

Power of a power vs. a tower

Compare $(2^3)^4$ and $2^{3^4}$.

  1. $(2^3)^4 = 2^{3 \cdot 4} = 2^{12} = 4096$.

    Four groups of three 2's is twelve 2's.

  2. $2^{3^4} = 2^{81}$, since $3^4 = 81$.

    Towers are evaluated from the top down, so the exponent is $3^4$.

  3. $2^{81}$ is vastly larger: it equals $2^{12}\cdot 2^{69}$.

    Writing it this way shows it is $4096$ multiplied by an enormous number.

$(2^3)^4 = 2^{12}$, but $2^{3^4} = 2^{81}$, which is much larger.

Simplify with variables

Simplify $\dfrac{(x^{-2}y^{3})^{-2}}{x^{3}y^{-1}}$, writing the answer with positive exponents. Assume $x, y \ne 0$.

  1. $(x^{-2}y^3)^{-2} = x^{(-2)(-2)}y^{3(-2)} = x^4y^{-6}$.

    Raise each factor to the power, multiplying exponents.

  2. $\dfrac{x^4y^{-6}}{x^3y^{-1}} = x^{4-3}y^{-6-(-1)} = x^1y^{-5}$.

    Subtract exponents base by base. Be careful: $-6-(-1) = -5$.

  3. $x y^{-5} = \dfrac{x}{y^5}$.

    Move the negative power to the denominator to make the exponent positive.

$\dfrac{x}{y^5}$

Compare with a common exponent

Which is larger, $2^{60}$ or $3^{40}$?

  1. Both exponents are multiples of $20$: $2^{60} = (2^3)^{20} = 8^{20}$ and $3^{40} = (3^2)^{20} = 9^{20}$.

    Rewriting with the same exponent turns a hard comparison into an easy one.

  2. Since $9 > 8$, we get $9^{20} > 8^{20}$.

    For positive bases and the same positive exponent, the bigger base gives the bigger power.

$3^{40}$ is larger.

If the bases differ, try to make the exponents match (or vice versa).

Factor out the smallest power

Evaluate $\dfrac{2^{2024} + 2^{2022}}{2^{2023} - 2^{2021}}$.

  1. Factor $2^{2021}$ from every term: the numerator is $2^{2021}(2^3 + 2^1)$ and the denominator is $2^{2021}(2^2 - 1)$.

    $2^{2024} = 2^{2021}\cdot 2^3$, and so on. Factoring out the smallest power leaves small numbers behind.

  2. Cancel $2^{2021}$: $\dfrac{8 + 2}{4 - 1} = \dfrac{10}{3}$.

    The huge common factor divides out completely.

$\dfrac{10}{3}$

Sums of powers don't combine, but you can always factor out the smallest power.

1.2 Fractional Exponents

Hook

What could $9^{1/2}$ possibly mean? You can't multiply $9$ by itself "half a time." But if the exponent rules are going to keep working, $9^{1/2} \cdot 9^{1/2}$ must equal $9^{1/2+1/2} = 9^1 = 9$. So $9^{1/2}$ has to be a number that, multiplied by itself, gives $9$.

Concept

That reasoning defines fractional exponents. $a^{1/2} = \sqrt{a}$ is the nonnegative number whose square is $a$. In general $a^{1/q} = \sqrt[q]{a}$, the $q$th root. Since $(a^{1/q})^p = a^{p/q}$,

$$a^{p/q} = \left(\sqrt[q]{a}\right)^{p} = \sqrt[q]{a^{p}}.$$

Both forms are equal, but taking the root first keeps the numbers small: $8^{2/3} = (\sqrt[3]{8})^2 = 2^2 = 4$ is easier than $\sqrt[3]{64}$.

Be careful with signs. The equation $x^2 = 9$ has two solutions, $x = \pm 3$, but the symbol $\sqrt{9}$ means only the nonnegative one, $3$. Odd roots of negative numbers are fine ($\sqrt[3]{-8} = -2$), but even roots of negative numbers are not real; that's where Chapter 2's imaginary numbers come in.

To estimate a root, trap the number between perfect powers: $49 < 55 < 64$, so $7 < \sqrt{55} < 8$.

Unit fraction exponents

Evaluate $27^{1/3}$ and $16^{1/4}$.

  1. $27^{1/3} = \sqrt[3]{27} = 3$, since $3^3 = 27$.

    An exponent of $1/3$ asks for the number whose cube is $27$.

  2. $16^{1/4} = \sqrt[4]{16} = 2$, since $2^4 = 16$.

    An exponent of $1/4$ asks for the positive number whose fourth power is $16$.

$3$ and $2$

Root first, then power

Evaluate $8^{2/3}$.

  1. $8^{2/3} = \left(8^{1/3}\right)^2 = 2^2$.

    Split $\tfrac{2}{3}$ as $\tfrac13 \cdot 2$ and take the cube root first.

  2. $2^2 = 4$.

    Doing the root first means we never had to handle $8^2 = 64$.

$4$

A negative fractional exponent

Evaluate $32^{-3/5}$.

  1. $32^{-3/5} = \dfrac{1}{32^{3/5}}$.

    Deal with the minus sign first: it just means reciprocal.

  2. $32^{3/5} = \left(\sqrt[5]{32}\right)^3 = 2^3 = 8$.

    $2^5 = 32$, so the fifth root is $2$.

  3. So $32^{-3/5} = \dfrac18$.

    Combine the two pieces.

$\dfrac{1}{8}$

Trap a root between integers

Between which two consecutive integers does $\sqrt{90}$ lie? What about $\sqrt[3]{100}$?

  1. $81 < 90 < 100$, so $9 < \sqrt{90} < 10$.

    Find the perfect squares on either side; square roots preserve order.

  2. $64 < 100 < 125$, so $4 < \sqrt[3]{100} < 5$.

    Same idea with perfect cubes: $4^3 = 64$ and $5^3 = 125$.

$\sqrt{90}$ is between $9$ and $10$; $\sqrt[3]{100}$ is between $4$ and $5$.

Equations vs. root symbols

Find all real $x$ with $x^2 = 49$. Then evaluate $\sqrt{49}$, $\sqrt[3]{-64}$, and decide whether $(-16)^{1/2}$ is real.

  1. $x^2 = 49$ gives $x = 7$ or $x = -7$.

    Both $7^2$ and $(-7)^2$ equal $49$, and an equation asks for every solution.

  2. $\sqrt{49} = 7$ only.

    The radical symbol names one number, the nonnegative root.

  3. $\sqrt[3]{-64} = -4$, because $(-4)^3 = -64$.

    Odd powers keep the sign, so odd roots of negatives are real.

  4. $(-16)^{1/2}$ is not real: no real number squares to a negative.

    Every real square is $\ge 0$.

$x = \pm 7$; $\sqrt{49} = 7$; $\sqrt[3]{-64} = -4$; $(-16)^{1/2}$ is not a real number.

A mixed product

Evaluate $\dfrac{4^{3/2}\cdot 9^{-1/2}}{8^{-2/3}}$.

  1. $4^{3/2} = (\sqrt4)^3 = 8$ and $9^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{3}$.

    Root first, then power, then reciprocal for the negative.

  2. $8^{-2/3} = \dfrac{1}{(\sqrt[3]{8})^2} = \dfrac14$.

    The cube root of $8$ is $2$, and $2^2 = 4$.

  3. $\dfrac{8 \cdot \frac13}{\frac14} = \dfrac{8}{3}\cdot 4 = \dfrac{32}{3}$.

    Dividing by $\tfrac14$ is multiplying by $4$.

$\dfrac{32}{3}$

Solve by matching bases

Solve $4^{x+1} = 8^{x}$.

  1. Write both sides as powers of $2$: $(2^2)^{x+1} = (2^3)^x$, so $2^{2x+2} = 2^{3x}$.

    With a common base, the equation compares exponents only.

  2. $2x + 2 = 3x$, so $x = 2$.

    Equal powers of $2$ must have equal exponents.

  3. Check: $4^3 = 64$ and $8^2 = 64$. ✓

    A quick substitution confirms the answer.

$x = 2$

Don't lose a solution

Find all real $x$ such that $x^{2/3} = 16$.

  1. Write $x^{2/3} = \left(x^{1/3}\right)^2 = 16$.

    Taking the cube root first gives us a square equation we know how to handle.

  2. So $x^{1/3} = 4$ or $x^{1/3} = -4$.

    An equation $u^2 = 16$ has two solutions, $u = \pm 4$.

  3. Cube: $x = 64$ or $x = -64$.

    Cube roots of negatives are real, so both values are valid.

  4. Check $x = -64$: $\left((-64)^{1/3}\right)^2 = (-4)^2 = 16$. ✓

    The negative solution is easy to miss, so verify it directly.

$x = 64$ or $x = -64$

When an even power appears, ask whether a negative value could also work.

1.3 Simplifying Radical Expressions

Hook

$\sqrt{72}$ and $6\sqrt2$ are exactly the same number. But only one of them tells you at a glance that it's a bit more than $8$, and that it combines neatly with $\sqrt{8}$. Simplifying radicals is about choosing the most useful name for a number.

Concept

The key fact is that roots split over products: for $a, b \ge 0$,

$$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b}, \qquad \sqrt[q]{ab} = \sqrt[q]{a}\,\sqrt[q]{b}.$$

So to simplify $\sqrt{n}$, factor out the largest perfect square: $\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \cdot 2} = 6\sqrt2$. A radical is in simplest form when the number under the root has no perfect-square factor other than $1$ (for cube roots, no perfect-cube factor, and so on).

When the largest square isn't obvious, use the prime factorization. Each pair of equal primes escapes a square root as a single prime; for a cube root you need a triple. For example, $1440 = 2^5 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$, so $\sqrt{1440} = 2^2 \cdot 3\sqrt{2 \cdot 5} = 12\sqrt{10}$.

Simplified radicals with the same radicand combine like terms: $5\sqrt2 + 3\sqrt2 = 8\sqrt2$. But $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$ does not simplify, and $\sqrt{a+b} \ne \sqrt a + \sqrt b$.

Pull out a perfect square

Simplify $\sqrt{72}$.

  1. The largest perfect square dividing $72$ is $36$: $72 = 36 \cdot 2$.

    We want the biggest square so only a square-free number is left inside.

  2. $\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36}\sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt2$.

    The root of a product is the product of the roots.

$6\sqrt{2}$

Combine like radicals

Simplify $\sqrt{50} + \sqrt{18}$.

  1. $\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \cdot 2} = 5\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt{18} = \sqrt{9 \cdot 2} = 3\sqrt2$.

    Simplifying first reveals a common radical.

  2. $5\sqrt2 + 3\sqrt2 = 8\sqrt2$.

    Like terms add just as $5x + 3x = 8x$.

$8\sqrt{2}$

A cube root

Simplify $\sqrt[3]{54}$.

  1. $54 = 27 \cdot 2$, and $27 = 3^3$ is a perfect cube.

    For a cube root we look for perfect cube factors.

  2. $\sqrt[3]{54} = \sqrt[3]{27}\,\sqrt[3]{2} = 3\sqrt[3]{2}$.

    Roots split over products for any index.

$3\sqrt[3]{2}$

Use the prime factorization

Simplify $\sqrt{2400}$.

  1. $2400 = 24 \cdot 100 = 2^5 \cdot 3 \cdot 5^2$.

    The prime factorization shows every square factor at once.

  2. Group pairs: $2^5 = (2^2)^2 \cdot 2$ and $5^2$ is one pair. Out come $2^2 \cdot 5 = 20$; left inside are $2 \cdot 3 = 6$.

    Each pair of equal primes leaves the root as one copy.

  3. $\sqrt{2400} = 20\sqrt6$.

    $6$ has no square factor, so we're done.

$20\sqrt{6}$

Multiply first, then simplify

Simplify $\sqrt{12}\cdot\sqrt{27}$.

  1. $\sqrt{12}\cdot\sqrt{27} = \sqrt{12 \cdot 27} = \sqrt{324}$.

    The product rule works in reverse too.

  2. $324 = 18^2$, so the result is $18$.

    Alternatively $2\sqrt3 \cdot 3\sqrt3 = 6 \cdot 3 = 18$, which is the same answer.

$18$

Radicals with variables

Simplify $\sqrt[4]{48x^5y^8}$, where $x, y > 0$.

  1. Find fourth powers: $48 = 16 \cdot 3 = 2^4 \cdot 3$, $x^5 = x^4 \cdot x$, and $y^8 = (y^2)^4$.

    A fourth root needs groups of four.

  2. $\sqrt[4]{2^4 x^4 (y^2)^4 \cdot 3x} = 2xy^2\sqrt[4]{3x}$.

    Each complete fourth power leaves the root; $3x$ stays inside.

$2xy^2\sqrt[4]{3x}$

A nested radical

Write $\sqrt{7 + 4\sqrt3}$ in the form $a + \sqrt b$.

  1. Suppose $\sqrt{7+4\sqrt3} = \sqrt a + \sqrt b$. Squaring gives $7 + 4\sqrt3 = a + b + 2\sqrt{ab}$.

    Squaring removes the outer root so we can compare parts.

  2. Match parts: $a + b = 7$ and $2\sqrt{ab} = 4\sqrt3$, so $ab = 12$.

    The rational parts and the radical parts must each agree.

  3. Two numbers with sum $7$ and product $12$ are $4$ and $3$. So the root is $\sqrt4 + \sqrt3 = 2 + \sqrt3$.

    $4 + 3 = 7$ and $4 \cdot 3 = 12$.

  4. Check: $(2+\sqrt3)^2 = 4 + 4\sqrt3 + 3 = 7 + 4\sqrt3$. ✓

    Verification confirms the guess, and $2 + \sqrt 3 > 0$ as a square root must be.

$2 + \sqrt{3}$

Guess a form, square it, and match rational and irrational parts.

1.4 Rationalizing Denominators

Hook

Without a calculator, estimate $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$. Dividing $1$ by $1.41421\ldots$ is painful. But $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2} = \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$, and half of $1.414$ is easy: about $0.707$. Moving the radical to the top made the arithmetic easy.

Concept

Rationalizing the denominator means rewriting a fraction so no radicals remain in the denominator. We multiply by a cleverly chosen form of $1$.

Single radical: multiply top and bottom by whatever completes a perfect power. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt5} \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt5}{\sqrt5} = \dfrac{\sqrt5}{5}$, and $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{2}}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{4}}{\sqrt[3]{4}} = \dfrac{\sqrt[3]4}{2}$ because $2 \cdot 4 = 2^3$.

Two terms: use the conjugate. The difference of squares kills the radical:

$$(a + \sqrt b)(a - \sqrt b) = a^2 - b, \qquad (a\sqrt b + c\sqrt d)(a\sqrt b - c\sqrt d) = a^2b - c^2d.$$

The cross terms $\pm a\sqrt b$ cancel, so only rational numbers survive. For cube roots, the sum of cubes $x^3 + y^3 = (x+y)(x^2 - xy + y^2)$ plays the same role.

One square root

Rationalize $\dfrac{6}{\sqrt3}$.

  1. $\dfrac{6}{\sqrt3}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3} = \dfrac{6\sqrt3}{3}$.

    $\sqrt3 \cdot \sqrt3 = 3$ is rational, and $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3} = 1$ doesn't change the value.

  2. $\dfrac{6\sqrt3}{3} = 2\sqrt3$.

    Always reduce at the end.

$2\sqrt{3}$

Simplify first

Rationalize $\dfrac{5}{\sqrt{12}}$.

  1. $\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt3$, so the fraction is $\dfrac{5}{2\sqrt3}$.

    Simplifying the radical first means we multiply by a smaller root.

  2. $\dfrac{5}{2\sqrt3}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3} = \dfrac{5\sqrt3}{6}$.

    Now only $\sqrt3$ needs to be cleared.

$\dfrac{5\sqrt{3}}{6}$

A cube root in the denominator

Rationalize $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{2}}$.

  1. We need the denominator to become $\sqrt[3]{2^3}$, so multiply by $\sqrt[3]{2^2} = \sqrt[3]{4}$.

    A cube root clears only when the number inside is a perfect cube.

  2. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{2}}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{4}}{\sqrt[3]{4}} = \dfrac{\sqrt[3]{4}}{\sqrt[3]{8}} = \dfrac{\sqrt[3]{4}}{2}$.

    $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2$.

$\dfrac{\sqrt[3]{4}}{2}$

Use the conjugate

Rationalize $\dfrac{4}{3 - \sqrt5}$.

  1. Multiply by $\dfrac{3+\sqrt5}{3+\sqrt5}$.

    The conjugate flips the sign between the terms.

  2. Denominator: $(3-\sqrt5)(3+\sqrt5) = 9 - 5 = 4$.

    Difference of squares: the radical vanishes.

  3. $\dfrac{4(3+\sqrt5)}{4} = 3 + \sqrt5$.

    The $4$'s cancel.

$3 + \sqrt{5}$

Next in the book: Exercise 1-5.

Radicals on top and bottom

Rationalize $\dfrac{\sqrt7 + \sqrt2}{\sqrt7 - \sqrt2}$.

  1. Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt7 + \sqrt2$.

    That is the conjugate of the denominator.

  2. Denominator: $7 - 2 = 5$. Numerator: $(\sqrt7+\sqrt2)^2 = 7 + 2\sqrt{14} + 2 = 9 + 2\sqrt{14}$.

    Square a binomial: $(x+y)^2 = x^2 + 2xy + y^2$, with $\sqrt7\sqrt2 = \sqrt{14}$.

  3. Result: $\dfrac{9 + 2\sqrt{14}}{5}$.

    Nothing more cancels.

$\dfrac{9 + 2\sqrt{14}}{5}$

A cube-root conjugate

Rationalize $\dfrac{1}{1 + \sqrt[3]{2}}$.

  1. Use the sum of cubes: $x^3 + y^3 = (x+y)(x^2 - xy + y^2)$ with $x = 1$, $y = \sqrt[3]{2}$.

    We want a product equal to $1^3 + (\sqrt[3]2)^3 = 1 + 2$, which is rational.

  2. Multiply top and bottom by $1 - \sqrt[3]{2} + \sqrt[3]{4}$. The denominator becomes $1 + 2 = 3$.

    Here $x^2 = 1$, $xy = \sqrt[3]2$ and $y^2 = \sqrt[3]4$.

  3. $\dfrac{1}{1+\sqrt[3]{2}} = \dfrac{1 - \sqrt[3]{2} + \sqrt[3]{4}}{3}$.

    Numerically both sides are about $0.4425$, a quick sanity check.

$\dfrac{1 - \sqrt[3]{2} + \sqrt[3]{4}}{3}$

The "conjugate" is whatever completes a factorization into rational terms.

Three radicals in a row

Rationalize $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2 + \sqrt3 + \sqrt5}$.

  1. Group as $(\sqrt2+\sqrt3) + \sqrt5$ and multiply top and bottom by $(\sqrt2+\sqrt3) - \sqrt5$.

    Treating $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$ as a single term lets the conjugate trick apply.

  2. Denominator: $(\sqrt2+\sqrt3)^2 - 5 = (5 + 2\sqrt6) - 5 = 2\sqrt6$.

    The $5$'s cancel, leaving just one radical.

  3. So far: $\dfrac{\sqrt2+\sqrt3-\sqrt5}{2\sqrt6}$. Multiply by $\dfrac{\sqrt6}{\sqrt6}$: $\dfrac{\sqrt{12}+\sqrt{18}-\sqrt{30}}{12}$.

    A single radical clears with itself.

  4. Simplify: $\dfrac{2\sqrt3 + 3\sqrt2 - \sqrt{30}}{12}$.

    $\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt3$ and $\sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt2$.

$\dfrac{2\sqrt{3} + 3\sqrt{2} - \sqrt{30}}{12}$

Rationalize in stages: each conjugate removes one layer of radicals.

1.5 Logarithms

Hook

Start with $1$ and keep doubling: $2, 4, 8, \ldots$ How many doublings until you pass one million? You're asking "$2$ to what power is $1{,}000{,}000$?" That missing exponent has a name: $\log_2 1{,}000{,}000 \approx 19.93$, so it takes $20$ doublings.

Concept

A logarithm answers the question "what exponent?":

$$\log_b x = y \quad\text{means}\quad b^y = x \qquad (b > 0,\ b \neq 1,\ x > 0).$$

So $\log_2 8 = 3$ because $2^3 = 8$, and $\log_{10} 0.01 = -2$ because $10^{-2} = 0.01$. Since $b^y$ is always positive, you can only take the log of a positive number.

Every exponent rule becomes a log rule:

$$\log_b(xy) = \log_b x + \log_b y \qquad \log_b\!\frac{x}{y} = \log_b x - \log_b y \qquad \log_b(x^n) = n\log_b x$$

$$\log_b x = \frac{\log_c x}{\log_c b} \quad\text{(change of base)} \qquad \log_b b = 1, \quad \log_b 1 = 0.$$

Graphically, $y = \log_b x$ is the mirror image of $y = b^x$ across the line $y = x$: logs and exponentials undo each other.

Translate between forms

Write $2^5 = 32$ as a logarithm, and $\log_{10} 0.001 = -3$ as an exponential equation.

  1. $2^5 = 32 \iff \log_2 32 = 5$.

    The base stays the base; the log is the exponent.

  2. $\log_{10} 0.001 = -3 \iff 10^{-3} = 0.001$.

    Read it as: "$10$ to the $-3$ is $0.001$."

$\log_2 32 = 5$ and $10^{-3} = 0.001$.

Evaluate simple logs

Evaluate $\log_3 81$ and $\log_5 \dfrac{1}{25}$.

  1. $3^4 = 81$, so $\log_3 81 = 4$.

    Ask "$3$ to what power gives $81$?"

  2. $5^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{25}$, so $\log_5 \dfrac1{25} = -2$.

    Reciprocals come from negative exponents.

$4$ and $-2$

Logs that aren't integers

Evaluate $\log_8 2$ and $\log_4 8$.

  1. $8^{1/3} = 2$, so $\log_8 2 = \dfrac13$.

    The cube root of $8$ is $2$.

  2. Let $\log_4 8 = y$, so $4^y = 8$, which means $2^{2y} = 2^3$.

    Rewrite both sides as powers of $2$, a common base.

  3. $2y = 3$, so $y = \dfrac32$.

    Check: $4^{3/2} = (\sqrt4)^3 = 8$. ✓

$\log_8 2 = \dfrac{1}{3}$ and $\log_4 8 = \dfrac{3}{2}$.

Combine with the product rule

Evaluate $\log_6 4 + \log_6 9$.

  1. $\log_6 4 + \log_6 9 = \log_6(4 \cdot 9) = \log_6 36$.

    A sum of logs with the same base is the log of the product.

  2. $6^2 = 36$, so the value is $2$.

    Neither log was nice on its own, but their sum is.

$2$

The quotient rule

Evaluate $\log_2 48 - \log_2 3$.

  1. $\log_2 48 - \log_2 3 = \log_2 \dfrac{48}{3} = \log_2 16$.

    A difference of logs is the log of the quotient.

  2. $2^4 = 16$, so the value is $4$.

    Again, combining turned two ugly logs into one nice one.

$4$

Change of base

Evaluate $\log_9 27$ and $\log_2 3 \cdot \log_3 8$.

  1. $\log_9 27 = \dfrac{\log_3 27}{\log_3 9} = \dfrac{3}{2}$.

    Base $3$ is natural because $9$ and $27$ are both powers of $3$.

  2. $\log_2 3 \cdot \log_3 8 = \dfrac{\log 3}{\log 2}\cdot\dfrac{\log 8}{\log 3} = \dfrac{\log 8}{\log 2}$.

    Write both in a common base (any base works); the $\log 3$ cancels.

  3. $\dfrac{\log 8}{\log 2} = \log_2 8 = 3$.

    Change of base in reverse.

$\log_9 27 = \dfrac32$ and $\log_2 3 \cdot \log_3 8 = 3$.

Chained logs telescope: $\log_a b \cdot \log_b c = \log_a c$.

Solve a log equation

Solve $\log_2 x + \log_2 (x - 2) = 3$.

  1. Combine: $\log_2\big(x(x-2)\big) = 3$, so $x(x-2) = 2^3 = 8$.

    Product rule, then translate to exponential form.

  2. $x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0 \Rightarrow (x - 4)(x + 2) = 0$, so $x = 4$ or $x = -2$.

    A quadratic; Chapter 6 covers factoring in depth.

  3. Reject $x = -2$: $\log_2(-2)$ is undefined. Check $x = 4$: $\log_2 4 + \log_2 2 = 2 + 1 = 3$. ✓

    Logs need positive inputs, so always check solutions in the original equation.

$x = 4$

Counting digits with logs

Given $\log_{10} 2 \approx 0.30103$, how many digits does $2^{50}$ have?

  1. A positive integer $N$ has $d$ digits exactly when $10^{d-1} \le N < 10^{d}$, that is, when $d - 1 \le \log_{10} N < d$.

    The integer part of $\log_{10} N$ tells you its size in powers of ten.

  2. $\log_{10} 2^{50} = 50 \log_{10} 2 \approx 50 \times 0.30103 = 15.05$.

    The power rule turns the exponent into a multiplier.

  3. $15 \le 15.05 < 16$, so $2^{50}$ has $16$ digits.

    Indeed $2^{50} = 1{,}125{,}899{,}906{,}842{,}624$.

$16$ digits

$\lfloor \log_{10} N \rfloor + 1$ counts the digits of $N$.
