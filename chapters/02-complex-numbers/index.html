Chapter 2 · Algebra & Number

Complex Numbers

The equation $x^2 = -1$ has no real solution, so mathematicians did something bold: they invented one. In this chapter you'll meet $i$, watch its powers spin in a circle, and learn to add, multiply and divide numbers that have a real part and an imaginary part.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • $i^2 = -1$, and for a positive real $a$, $\sqrt{-a} = i\sqrt{a}$. Pull out the $i$ before combining radicals.
  • Powers of $i$ cycle with period 4: $i, -1, -i, 1$. Reduce the exponent modulo 4.
  • Any four consecutive powers of $i$ add to $0$.
  • Add and subtract complex numbers part by part; multiply with the distributive law and replace $i^2$ by $-1$.
  • $(a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 + b^2$ is real, so divide by multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator.
  • Two complex numbers are equal exactly when their real parts match and their imaginary parts match.

2.1 The Square Root of −1

Hook

Negative numbers were once called "absurd": how can you have less than nothing? Yet today a bank balance of $-40$ makes perfect sense. The square root of $-1$ went through the same journey. It started as a forbidden symbol and became an everyday tool for engineers working with electricity, waves and rotations.

Concept

No real number squares to a negative, so we define a new number $i$ with the single property

$$i^2 = -1.$$

Then $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has the two solutions $x = \pm i$. A real number times $i$, such as $5i$ or $\sqrt{2}\,i$, is called pure imaginary. "Imaginary" is just a name: these numbers obey the usual rules of algebra, with $i^2$ replaced by $-1$ whenever it appears.

For a positive real number $a$, we write $\sqrt{-a} = i\sqrt{a}$. So $\sqrt{-9} = 3i$ and $\sqrt{-12} = i\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}\,i$.

Trap. The rule $\sqrt{x}\sqrt{y} = \sqrt{xy}$ works for nonnegative $x$ and $y$ only. $\sqrt{-1}\cdot\sqrt{-1} = i \cdot i = -1$, but "combining" first would give $\sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{1} = 1$, which is wrong. Always rewrite $\sqrt{-a}$ as $i\sqrt a$ first.

Keep multiplying by $i$ and a pattern appears: $i^1 = i$, $i^2 = -1$, $i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = -i$, $i^4 = (i^2)^2 = 1$. Since $i^4 = 1$, the next four powers repeat the same values. The powers of $i$ cycle with period 4:

$$i^{4q + r} = (i^4)^q \cdot i^r = i^r, \qquad\text{so only the remainder of the exponent on division by 4 matters.}$$

Square roots of negatives

Write $\sqrt{-49}$ and $\sqrt{-20}$ in terms of $i$.

  1. $\sqrt{-49} = i\sqrt{49} = 7i$.

    Pull the $-1$ out as $i$, then take the ordinary square root of the positive part.

  2. $\sqrt{-20} = i\sqrt{20} = i\sqrt{4\cdot 5} = 2\sqrt{5}\,i$.

    After extracting $i$, simplify the remaining radical exactly as in Chapter 1.

$7i$ and $2\sqrt{5}\,i$

The first few powers

Compute $i^3$, $i^4$ and $i^5$ from $i^2 = -1$.

  1. $i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = (-1)i = -i$.

    Peel off one factor of $i$ so that the rest is the known value $i^2$.

  2. $i^4 = i^2 \cdot i^2 = (-1)(-1) = 1$.

    Two copies of $i^2$ multiply to a positive number.

  3. $i^5 = i^4 \cdot i = 1 \cdot i = i$.

    Because $i^4 = 1$, multiplying by it changes nothing: the cycle starts over.

$i^3 = -i$, $i^4 = 1$, $i^5 = i$

Large powers of i

Evaluate $i^{30}$ and $i^{103}$.

  1. $30 = 4\cdot 7 + 2$, so $i^{30} = (i^4)^7 \cdot i^2 = 1 \cdot (-1) = -1$.

    Each block of four factors of $i$ is $i^4 = 1$, so only the remainder $2$ survives.

  2. $103 = 4 \cdot 25 + 3$, so $i^{103} = i^3 = -i$.

    Same idea: divide the exponent by 4 and keep the remainder.

$i^{30} = -1$ and $i^{103} = -i$

Don't combine the radicals

Evaluate $\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9}$.

  1. $\sqrt{-4} = 2i$ and $\sqrt{-9} = 3i$.

    Rewrite each square root of a negative with $i$ before doing anything else.

  2. $(2i)(3i) = 6i^2 = -6$.

    Multiply the real coefficients and the $i$'s, then use $i^2 = -1$.

  3. Note that $\sqrt{(-4)(-9)} = \sqrt{36} = 6$ gives the wrong sign.

    The product rule for radicals only holds when the numbers under the roots are nonnegative.

$-6$

Negative powers of i

Evaluate $i^{-1}$ and $i^{-7}$.

  1. $i^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{i} = \dfrac{1}{i}\cdot\dfrac{i}{i} = \dfrac{i}{i^2} = \dfrac{i}{-1} = -i$.

    Multiplying top and bottom by $i$ turns the denominator into the real number $-1$.

  2. $i^{-7} = i^{-8}\cdot i^{1} = (i^4)^{-2}\cdot i = 1\cdot i = i$.

    Add a multiple of 4 to the exponent to make it nonnegative: $-7 + 8 = 1$. Multiples of 4 contribute a factor of $1$.

$i^{-1} = -i$ and $i^{-7} = i$

The cycle runs backwards too: reduce any integer exponent to a remainder $0, 1, 2$ or $3$.

Solving with imaginary roots

Solve $x^2 + 25 = 0$ and $2x^2 + 36 = 0$.

  1. $x^2 = -25$, so $x = \pm\sqrt{-25} = \pm 5i$.

    Like $x^2 = 25$, the equation has two square roots, one the negative of the other.

  2. $2x^2 = -36$ gives $x^2 = -18$, so $x = \pm i\sqrt{18} = \pm 3\sqrt{2}\,i$.

    Isolate $x^2$ first, then take both square roots and simplify $\sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt2$.

  3. Check one: $(3\sqrt2\, i)^2 = 9 \cdot 2 \cdot i^2 = -18$. ✓

    Squaring the answer confirms it satisfies $x^2 = -18$.

$x = \pm 5i$; $\ x = \pm 3\sqrt{2}\,i$

A long sum of powers

Evaluate $i + i^2 + i^3 + \cdots + i^{50}$.

  1. $i + i^2 + i^3 + i^4 = i - 1 - i + 1 = 0$.

    One full trip around the cycle cancels completely. The same is true of any four consecutive powers, since each block is the first block times some power of $i$.

  2. $50 = 4 \cdot 12 + 2$, so the first $48$ terms form $12$ blocks that each sum to $0$.

    Group the terms in fours from the start.

  3. What remains is $i^{49} + i^{50} = i^1 + i^2 = i - 1$.

    Reduce the leftover exponents modulo 4.

$-1 + i$

Four consecutive powers of $i$ always sum to zero.

A product of many powers

Evaluate $i^1 \cdot i^2 \cdot i^3 \cdots i^{20}$.

  1. The product equals $i^{1+2+3+\cdots+20}$.

    Multiplying powers of the same base adds the exponents.

  2. $1 + 2 + \cdots + 20 = \dfrac{20 \cdot 21}{2} = 210$.

    Pair the first and last terms: ten pairs, each adding to $21$.

  3. $210 = 4 \cdot 52 + 2$, so $i^{210} = i^2 = -1$.

    Only the remainder of the exponent modulo 4 matters.

$-1$

Coefficients that climb

Evaluate $1 + 2i + 3i^2 + 4i^3 + \cdots + 20i^{19}$.

  1. The first four terms are $1 + 2i - 3 - 4i = -2 - 2i$.

    Replace $i^2, i^3$ by $-1, -i$ and collect real and imaginary parts.

  2. A general block is $(4k+1) + (4k+2)i - (4k+3) - (4k+4)i = -2 - 2i$.

    Within each block the powers of $i$ are again $1, i, -1, -i$. The $4k$'s cancel in pairs, so every block gives the same value.

  3. There are $20/4 = 5$ blocks, so the sum is $5(-2 - 2i) = -10 - 10i$.

    The twenty terms split evenly into five blocks of four.

$-10 - 10i$

Group by the period of the cycle, even when the blocks don't cancel completely.

2.2 Complex Number Operations

Hook

A real number is a point on a line. A complex number needs two coordinates, so it lives in a plane: $3 + 2i$ sits three steps right and two steps up. Adding complex numbers turns out to be sliding arrows tip to tail, and multiplying by $i$ is a quarter turn. Arithmetic becomes geometry.

Concept

A complex number has the form $z = a + bi$ with $a$ and $b$ real. The real part is $\operatorname{Re}(z) = a$, and $\operatorname{Im}(z) = b$ is the real coefficient of the imaginary part (note: $b$, not $bi$). Every real number is complex with $b = 0$. A complex number that is not real is called imaginary; it is pure imaginary if, in addition, $a = 0$.

Arithmetic follows ordinary algebra with $i^2 = -1$:

$$(a+bi) \pm (c+di) = (a \pm c) + (b \pm d)i$$

$$(a+bi)(c+di) = ac + adi + bci + bdi^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i$$

The conjugate of $z = a + bi$ is $\overline{z} = a - bi$ (a reflection across the real axis). Its key property is that a number times its conjugate is real:

$$z\overline{z} = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 - b^2i^2 = a^2 + b^2.$$

That is exactly what we need to divide. Just as we rationalized $\dfrac{1}{a+\sqrt b}$ with a conjugate in Chapter 1, we clear $i$ from a denominator by multiplying top and bottom by the denominator's conjugate:

$$\frac{a+bi}{c+di} = \frac{(a+bi)(c-di)}{(c+di)(c-di)} = \frac{(a+bi)(c-di)}{c^2+d^2}.$$

Two complex numbers are equal only if their real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are equal. One complex equation therefore gives two real equations.

Sorting out the names

Classify $-6$, $\ 4i$, $\ 2 - 5i$ and $0$ as real, imaginary, and/or pure imaginary.

  1. $-6 = -6 + 0i$ is real (and complex), not imaginary.

    Its imaginary coefficient is $0$.

  2. $4i = 0 + 4i$ is imaginary and pure imaginary.

    It is not real, and its real part is $0$.

  3. $2 - 5i$ is imaginary but not pure imaginary.

    It is not real, but its real part $2$ is not zero.

  4. $0$ is real, so it is not imaginary.

    $0 = 0 + 0i$ has imaginary coefficient $0$, which makes it real by definition.

$-6$ and $0$: real. $4i$: imaginary and pure imaginary. $2-5i$: imaginary only.

Add and subtract part by part

Compute $(4 - 7i) + (-1 + 3i)$ and $(2 + 5i) - (6 - i)$.

  1. $(4 - 1) + (-7 + 3)i = 3 - 4i$.

    Real parts combine with real parts and imaginary parts with imaginary parts, like collecting like terms.

  2. $(2 - 6) + (5 - (-1))i = -4 + 6i$.

    Subtracting distributes the minus sign over both parts of $6 - i$; watch the double negative.

$3 - 4i$ and $-4 + 6i$

Multiply with FOIL

Compute $(2 + 3i)(4 - i)$.

  1. $(2)(4) + (2)(-i) + (3i)(4) + (3i)(-i) = 8 - 2i + 12i - 3i^2$.

    Distribute every term of the first factor over every term of the second.

  2. $-3i^2 = -3(-1) = +3$.

    This is the one new move: every $i^2$ becomes $-1$, often flipping a sign.

  3. $8 + 3 + (-2 + 12)i = 11 + 10i$.

    Collect real and imaginary parts.

$11 + 10i$

Next in the book: Exercises 2-4 and 2-5.

A number times its conjugate

Compute $(5 + 2i)(5 - 2i)$.

  1. $(5+2i)(5-2i) = 5^2 - (2i)^2$.

    This is a difference of squares, $(x+y)(x-y) = x^2 - y^2$; the middle terms $\pm 10i$ cancel.

  2. $25 - 4i^2 = 25 + 4 = 29$.

    $(2i)^2 = 4i^2 = -4$, and subtracting a negative adds.

$29$, a real number, equal to $5^2 + 2^2$.

Divide using the conjugate

Write $\dfrac{7 + i}{1 - 2i}$ in the form $a + bi$.

  1. Multiply top and bottom by $1 + 2i$: $\dfrac{(7 + i)(1 + 2i)}{(1 - 2i)(1 + 2i)}$.

    Multiplying by $\frac{1+2i}{1+2i} = 1$ doesn't change the value, but it makes the denominator real.

  2. Denominator: $1^2 + 2^2 = 5$.

    A number times its conjugate is the sum of the squares of its parts.

  3. Numerator: $7 + 14i + i + 2i^2 = 7 - 2 + 15i = 5 + 15i$.

    FOIL, then replace $i^2$ with $-1$.

  4. $\dfrac{5 + 15i}{5} = 1 + 3i$.

    Divide both parts by the real denominator.

$1 + 3i$

Check by multiplying back: $(1+3i)(1-2i) = 1 - 2i + 3i + 6 = 7 + i$. ✓

Real part, imaginary part, conjugate

Let $z = 2 - 3i$. Find $\operatorname{Im}(z)$, $\ z + \overline{z}$, $\ z - \overline{z}$ and $z\overline{z}$.

  1. $\operatorname{Im}(z) = -3$ and $\overline{z} = 2 + 3i$.

    $\operatorname{Im}$ is the real coefficient of $i$, so it is $-3$, not $-3i$. The conjugate flips the sign of the imaginary part.

  2. $z + \overline{z} = 4$ and $z - \overline{z} = -6i$.

    Adding cancels the imaginary parts, leaving $2\operatorname{Re}(z)$; subtracting cancels the real parts, leaving $2i\operatorname{Im}(z)$.

  3. $z\overline{z} = 2^2 + 3^2 = 13$.

    A number times its conjugate is $a^2 + b^2$.

$-3$, $\ 4$, $\ -6i$, $\ 13$

Powers of a complex number

Compute $(3 - 2i)^2$ and $(1 + i)^8$.

  1. $(3 - 2i)^2 = 9 - 12i + 4i^2 = 5 - 12i$.

    Use $(x - y)^2 = x^2 - 2xy + y^2$, then $4i^2 = -4$.

  2. $(1 + i)^2 = 1 + 2i + i^2 = 2i$.

    Squaring first gives a pure imaginary number, which is easy to raise to powers.

  3. $(1+i)^8 = \left((1+i)^2\right)^4 = (2i)^4 = 16i^4 = 16$.

    Power of a power, then $i^4 = 1$.

$5 - 12i$ and $16$

A reciprocal

Write $\dfrac{1}{3 + 4i}$ in the form $a + bi$.

  1. $\dfrac{1}{3+4i}\cdot\dfrac{3-4i}{3-4i} = \dfrac{3 - 4i}{3^2 + 4^2}$.

    Multiply by the conjugate over itself to make the denominator real.

  2. $= \dfrac{3 - 4i}{25} = \dfrac{3}{25} - \dfrac{4}{25}i$.

    Split into real and imaginary parts.

$\dfrac{3}{25} - \dfrac{4}{25}i$

In general $\dfrac{1}{z} = \dfrac{\overline{z}}{z\overline{z}} = \dfrac{\overline z}{a^2+b^2}$.

Solve a complex linear equation

Find the complex number $z$ with $z + 2\overline{z} = 6 - i$.

  1. Let $z = a + bi$, so $\overline{z} = a - bi$.

    The conjugate can't be handled by ordinary algebra on $z$ alone, so name the real and imaginary parts.

  2. $z + 2\overline{z} = (a + bi) + (2a - 2bi) = 3a - bi$.

    Combine real parts and imaginary parts.

  3. $3a - bi = 6 - i$ gives $3a = 6$ and $-b = -1$.

    Complex numbers are equal only when both their real parts and their imaginary parts match, so one complex equation is two real equations.

  4. $a = 2$, $b = 1$, so $z = 2 + i$. Check: $(2 + i) + 2(2 - i) = 6 - i$. ✓

    Substitute back to confirm.

$z = 2 + i$

Matching real and imaginary parts turns one complex equation into two real ones.

A quotient raised to a huge power

Evaluate $\left(\dfrac{1 + i}{1 - i}\right)^{2026}$.

  1. $\dfrac{1+i}{1-i}\cdot\dfrac{1+i}{1+i} = \dfrac{(1+i)^2}{1^2 + 1^2} = \dfrac{2i}{2} = i$.

    Simplify the base before thinking about the exponent; the conjugate trick reduces it to a single power of $i$.

  2. So the expression is $i^{2026}$, and $2026 = 4 \cdot 506 + 2$.

    Divide the exponent by 4 to find where we are in the cycle.

  3. $i^{2026} = i^2 = -1$.

    Only the remainder $2$ matters.

$-1$
