Chapter 5 · Algebra & Number

Using the Integers

The whole numbers look simple, but they hide a lot of structure. In this chapter you'll learn what it means for one number to divide another, write numbers in other bases, predict last digits of enormous powers, compute with remainders, spot divisibility at a glance, break numbers into primes, and find what two numbers share.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • $m \mid n$ means $n = km$ for some integer $k$. Divisors come in pairs $d$ and $n/d$. If $d \mid a$ and $d \mid b$, then $d \mid a \pm b$.
  • In base $b$, the digits $d_k \ldots d_1 d_0$ stand for $d_k b^k + \cdots + d_1 b + d_0$. To convert into base $b$, divide by $b$ again and again and read the remainders from the bottom up.
  • The last digit of a sum or product depends only on the last digits. Last digits of powers repeat in cycles of length 1, 2 or 4.
  • $a \equiv b \pmod m$ means $m \mid a - b$, or equivalently that $a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder. You may add, subtract, multiply and raise to powers, reducing at any time, but you may not simply divide.
  • Because $10 \equiv 1 \pmod 9$ and $10 \equiv -1 \pmod{11}$, digit sums test for $3$ and $9$, and alternating digit sums test for $11$. Last digits test for $2, 4, 5, 8, 10$.
  • Every integer greater than $1$ factors into primes in exactly one way. To test whether $N$ is prime, try primes only up to $\sqrt N$.
  • GCF: take the smaller power of each shared prime. LCM: take the larger power of every prime. And $\gcd(a,b)\cdot\operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = ab$.

5.1 Divisibility

Hook

You have 24 chairs to arrange in a rectangle with equal rows. You can make 1 row of 24, 2 rows of 12, 3 of 8, 4 of 6, and so on. With 23 chairs you're stuck: the only rectangle is one long line. That difference between 24 and 23 is what this section is about.

Concept

An integer $n$ is divisible by an integer $m$ if $n$ is a whole-number multiple of $m$, that is, $n = km$ for some integer $k$. We then call $m$ a divisor (or factor) of $n$ and write $m \mid n$, read "$m$ divides $n$". If $m$ does not divide $n$ we write $m \nmid n$. So $6 \mid 24$ but $5 \nmid 24$.

Divisors come in pairs: if $d \mid n$ then $n/d$ divides $n$ too. For $24$ the pairs are $1 \cdot 24$, $2 \cdot 12$, $3 \cdot 8$, $4 \cdot 6$: exactly the chair rectangles. A number greater than $1$ whose only positive divisors are $1$ and itself is prime; a number greater than $1$ with other divisors is composite.

$$m \mid n \iff n = km \text{ for some integer } k. \qquad \text{If } d \mid a \text{ and } d \mid b, \text{ then } d \mid a + b \text{ and } d \mid a - b.$$

The number $1$ is neither prime nor composite. Also, every integer divides $0$ (since $0 = 0 \cdot m$), but $0$ divides nothing except $0$.

List divisors in pairs

List all positive divisors of $36$.

  1. Test $1, 2, 3, \ldots$ and record each partner: $1 \cdot 36$, $2 \cdot 18$, $3 \cdot 12$, $4 \cdot 9$, $6 \cdot 6$.

    Each time a small number divides $36$, the quotient is a divisor too, so we find two at once.

  2. Stop at $6$: the next candidate, $7$, already has $7 \cdot 7 = 49 > 36$.

    Past $\sqrt{36} = 6$, any divisor would be the partner of one we already found.

  3. Divisors: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36$.

    Write them in order. The $6$ is paired with itself, so it's listed once.

$1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36$ (nine divisors)

Prime, composite, or neither?

Classify each of $1$, $2$, $15$, $17$ and $27$ as prime, composite, or neither.

  1. $1$ is neither.

    By definition primes and composites are greater than $1$.

  2. $2$ and $17$ are prime.

    Their only positive divisors are $1$ and themselves. Note that $2$ is the only even prime.

  3. $15 = 3 \cdot 5$ and $27 = 3 \cdot 9$ are composite.

    Each has a divisor other than $1$ and itself.

Prime: $2, 17$. Composite: $15, 27$. Neither: $1$.

Reading the notation

True or false: $4 \mid 20$, $20 \mid 4$, $13 \mid 0$, $0 \mid 13$, $-7 \mid 21$.

  1. $4 \mid 20$ is true since $20 = 5 \cdot 4$; $20 \mid 4$ is false since $4/20$ is not an integer.

    The divisor goes on the left of the bar. Order matters.

  2. $13 \mid 0$ is true because $0 = 0 \cdot 13$.

    Zero is a multiple (the zeroth multiple) of every integer.

  3. $0 \mid 13$ is false: no integer $k$ gives $13 = k \cdot 0$.

    Every multiple of $0$ is $0$.

  4. $-7 \mid 21$ is true since $21 = (-3)(-7)$.

    The definition allows negative integers too.

True, false, true, false, true.

Split a number into friendly parts

Without long division, decide whether $7 \mid 1435$ and whether $7 \mid 2109$.

  1. $1435 = 1400 + 35$, and $7 \mid 1400$ and $7 \mid 35$. So $7 \mid 1435$.

    If $d$ divides both pieces of a sum, it divides the sum.

  2. $2109 = 2100 + 9$. Here $7 \mid 2100$ but $7 \nmid 9$.

    If $7$ divided $2109$, it would also divide the difference $2109 - 2100 = 9$, which it doesn't.

  3. So $7 \nmid 2109$. (Its remainder is $2$, the remainder of $9$.)

    Splitting off a known multiple leaves a small number that decides the question.

$7 \mid 1435$, but $7 \nmid 2109$.

When is a fraction a whole number?

Find all positive integers $n$ for which $\dfrac{n + 6}{n}$ is an integer.

  1. $\dfrac{n+6}{n} = 1 + \dfrac{6}{n}$.

    Splitting the fraction isolates the part that might fail to be whole.

  2. This is an integer exactly when $n \mid 6$.

    $1$ is already an integer, so we need $6/n$ to be one.

  3. $n \in \{1, 2, 3, 6\}$.

    These are the positive divisors of $6$.

$n = 1, 2, 3, 6$

Who has an odd number of divisors?

How many integers from $1$ to $50$ have an odd number of positive divisors?

  1. Pair each divisor $d$ with $n/d$. The pair has two different members unless $d = n/d$, that is, $d^2 = n$.

    Pairs contribute an even count; only an unpaired divisor can make the total odd.

  2. So $n$ has an odd number of divisors exactly when $n$ is a perfect square.

    Then $\sqrt n$ is the one divisor paired with itself.

  3. The squares up to $50$ are $1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49$.

    $7^2 = 49 \le 50 < 64 = 8^2$.

$7$

Pairing divisors shows: odd number of divisors $\iff$ perfect square.

A shifted fraction

For how many positive integers $n$ is $\dfrac{n + 15}{n + 3}$ an integer?

  1. $n + 15 = (n + 3) + 12$, so $\dfrac{n+15}{n+3} = 1 + \dfrac{12}{n+3}$.

    Pulling out a copy of the denominator leaves a constant on top.

  2. We need $(n+3) \mid 12$ with $n + 3 \ge 4$.

    Since $n \ge 1$, the denominator is at least $4$.

  3. Divisors of $12$ that are at least $4$: $4, 6, 12$. So $n = 1, 3, 9$.

    Subtract $3$ from each. Check $n = 9$: $\dfrac{24}{12} = 2$.

$3$ values ($n = 1, 3, 9$)

Rewrite the numerator as (multiple of the denominator) + constant; then the denominator must divide the constant.

The locker hallway

A hallway has $50$ closed lockers numbered $1$ to $50$. Student $1$ toggles every locker, student $2$ toggles every 2nd locker, student $3$ every 3rd, and so on through student $50$. ("Toggle" means open it if closed, close it if open.) How many lockers end up open?

  1. Locker $n$ is toggled by student $k$ exactly when $k \mid n$.

    Student $k$ touches lockers $k, 2k, 3k, \ldots$, the multiples of $k$.

  2. So locker $n$ is toggled once per divisor of $n$, and ends open exactly when $n$ has an odd number of divisors.

    It starts closed; an odd number of toggles leaves it open.

  3. By the previous example, those are the perfect squares $1, 4, 9, \ldots, 49$.

    A story problem turned into a question about divisors.

$7$ lockers (numbers $1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49$)

5.2 Number Bases

Hook

A car's odometer rolls from $0999$ to $1000$: the ones wheel wraps to $0$ and pushes the next wheel along, which wraps and pushes the next, and so on. Nothing about that mechanism needs ten symbols per wheel. Give each wheel only the symbols $0$ to $7$ and you have a perfectly good counter in base $8$.

Concept

Our usual numbers are written in base 10: each place is worth ten times the place to its right. So $4072 = 4 \cdot 10^3 + 0 \cdot 10^2 + 7 \cdot 10 + 2$. In base $b$ we use the digits $0, 1, \ldots, b-1$ and the places are worth $1, b, b^2, b^3, \ldots$ We mark the base with a subscript:

$$(d_k d_{k-1} \ldots d_1 d_0)_b = d_k b^k + d_{k-1} b^{k-1} + \cdots + d_1 b + d_0, \qquad 0 \le d_i \le b - 1.$$

From base $b$ to base 10: expand by place value. From base 10 to base $b$: either peel off the largest power of $b$ that fits, again and again, or divide repeatedly by $b$; the remainders are the digits, read from last to first.

For bases above ten we need extra digits: base 16 (hexadecimal) uses $A = 10, B = 11, \ldots, F = 15$. Base 2 (binary) uses only $0$ and $1$. Addition with carrying works in any base; you carry whenever a column reaches $b$.

Base 5 to base 10

Write $243_5$ in base $10$.

  1. The places are worth $25, 5, 1$: $243_5 = 2 \cdot 25 + 4 \cdot 5 + 3 \cdot 1$.

    In base $5$ the places are $5^2, 5^1, 5^0$.

  2. $50 + 20 + 3 = 73$.

    Add the contributions.

$73$

Read a binary number

Write $1011011_2$ in base $10$.

  1. Label the places from the right: $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64$. The digits $1$ sit in places $64, 16, 8, 2, 1$.

    In binary each digit is either "use this power of $2$" or "don't".

  2. $64 + 16 + 8 + 2 + 1 = 91$.

    Only the places holding a $1$ contribute.

$91$

Peel off powers

Write $100$ in base $3$.

  1. Powers of $3$: $1, 3, 9, 27, 81, 243$. The largest that fits is $81$, once: $100 - 81 = 19$.

    $243 > 100$, so the number has five base-3 digits, led by the $81$s place.

  2. $27$s: $0$ (too big). $9$s: $2$, leaving $19 - 18 = 1$.

    Each place takes as many copies as fit, at most $b - 1 = 2$.

  3. $3$s: $0$. $1$s: $1$. Reading the digits: $1, 0, 2, 0, 1$.

    Don't skip the zeros; they hold the places.

  4. Check: $81 + 2 \cdot 9 + 1 = 100$. ✓

    Converting back catches slips.

$10201_3$

Repeated division

Write $200$ in base $6$ using repeated division.

  1. $200 = 33 \cdot 6 + 2$. $33 = 5 \cdot 6 + 3$. $5 = 0 \cdot 6 + 5$.

    Each remainder is the next digit, starting from the ones place.

  2. Read the remainders bottom to top: $532_6$.

    The first remainder is the ones digit, so it goes on the right.

  3. Check: $5 \cdot 36 + 3 \cdot 6 + 2 = 180 + 18 + 2 = 200$. ✓

    Why it works: $200 = (5 \cdot 6 + 3)\cdot 6 + 2 = 5 \cdot 6^2 + 3 \cdot 6 + 2$.

$532_6$

Hexadecimal both ways

Write $2AF_{16}$ in base 10, and write $250$ in base 16.

  1. $2AF_{16} = 2 \cdot 256 + 10 \cdot 16 + 15 = 512 + 160 + 15 = 687$.

    $A = 10$, $F = 15$, and the places are $16^2 = 256$, $16$, $1$.

  2. $250 = 15 \cdot 16 + 10$.

    One division by $16$ is enough, since the quotient $15$ is already less than $16$.

  3. Digits $15 \to F$ and $10 \to A$: $250 = FA_{16}$.

    Every digit must be a single symbol, so two-digit values become letters.

$2AF_{16} = 687$ and $250 = FA_{16}$.

Add in base 7

Compute $356_7 + 254_7$ without converting to base 10.

  1. Ones: $6 + 4 = 10 = 1 \cdot 7 + 3$. Write $3$, carry $1$.

    Carry whenever a column reaches $7$, not $10$.

  2. Sevens: $5 + 5 + 1 = 11 = 1 \cdot 7 + 4$. Write $4$, carry $1$.

    Include the carry from the previous column.

  3. Forty-nines: $3 + 2 + 1 = 6$. Write $6$. Result: $643_7$.

    $6 < 7$, so nothing more to carry.

  4. Check: $188 + 137 = 325$ and $643_7 = 294 + 28 + 3 = 325$. ✓

    $356_7 = 147 + 35 + 6$ and $254_7 = 98 + 35 + 4$.

$643_7$

Find the base

In some base $b$, the number $52_b$ equals $37$. Find $b$.

  1. $52_b = 5b + 2$, so $5b + 2 = 37$.

    Place value works the same way in an unknown base.

  2. $5b = 35$, so $b = 7$.

    A linear equation.

  3. Check the digits: $5$ and $2$ are both less than $7$. ✓

    A digit must be smaller than the base, so always confirm the base is big enough.

$b = 7$

An unknown base with three digits

Find the base $b$ for which $253_b = 136$.

  1. $2b^2 + 5b + 3 = 136$, so $2b^2 + 5b - 133 = 0$.

    Three digits give a quadratic in $b$.

  2. Try $b = 6$: $72 + 30 + 3 = 105$. Try $b = 7$: $98 + 35 + 3 = 136$. ✓

    $b > 5$ because $5$ is a digit, and the left side grows with $b$, so testing upward works. (Chapter 6 solves such equations directly.)

  3. Since $2b^2 + 5b + 3$ keeps increasing for larger $b$, $b = 7$ is the only answer.

    An increasing expression can hit $136$ only once.

$b = 7$

An unknown base turns place value into a polynomial equation in $b$.

5.3 The Last Digit

Hook

$7^{100}$ has $85$ digits. Nobody wants to compute it. Yet you can know its last digit in a few seconds, because when you multiply, the last digit of the answer only ever depends on the last digits of what you multiplied.

Concept

Write any integer as $10q + u$, where $u$ is its units digit. When you add or multiply two such numbers, every term involving $10q$ is a multiple of $10$ and cannot affect the units digit. So:

The last digit of a sum or product equals the last digit of the sum or product of the last digits.

For powers this means the last digits repeat. The last digits of $3^1, 3^2, 3^3, 3^4, 3^5, \ldots$ are $3, 9, 7, 1, 3, \ldots$: once a $1$ appears, the cycle restarts. Every digit has a cycle of length $1$, $2$ or $4$, so to find the last digit of $a^n$, find where $n$ falls in the cycle by looking at its remainder.

Only the units digit of the base matters: $23^{n}$ ends the same way as $3^{n}$. The same idea works in any base, and in Section 5.4 it grows into modular arithmetic.

Last digit of a product

What is the units digit of $47 \times 83$?

  1. Multiply the units digits: $7 \times 3 = 21$.

    The tens and higher digits only produce multiples of $10$.

  2. The units digit is $1$. (Indeed $47 \times 83 = 3901$.)

    Take the last digit of that small product.

$1$

Last digit of a sum

What is the units digit of $1234 + 5678 + 999$?

  1. $4 + 8 + 9 = 21$, so the units digit is $1$.

    Same principle for sums: only the units digits matter.

$1$

A cycle of four

Find the units digit of $3^{20}$.

  1. Last digits of $3^1, 3^2, 3^3, 3^4$: $3, 9, 7, 1$. Then $3^5$ ends in $1 \cdot 3 = 3$ and the cycle repeats.

    Each step multiplies the previous last digit by $3$ and keeps only the last digit.

  2. $20$ is a multiple of $4$, so $3^{20}$ sits at the end of a cycle, like $3^4$.

    $3^{20} = (3^4)^5$, and $3^4$ ends in $1$.

$1$

Use the remainder

Find the units digit of $7^{35}$.

  1. Cycle for $7$: $7, 9, 3, 1$ (length $4$).

    $7 \cdot 7 = 49$, $9 \cdot 7 = 63$, $3 \cdot 7 = 21$, $1 \cdot 7 = 7$.

  2. $35 = 4 \cdot 8 + 3$, so $7^{35} = (7^4)^8 \cdot 7^3$.

    Full cycles contribute a last digit of $1$; only the leftover $3$ steps matter.

  3. The 3rd entry of the cycle is $3$.

    $7^3 = 343$ ends in $3$.

$3$

Last digits of fourth powers

Which digits can be the units digit of $n^4$ for an integer $n$?

  1. Only the last digit of $n$ matters, so test $0$ through $9$: their fourth powers end in $0, 1, 6, 1, 6, 5, 6, 1, 6, 1$.

    For example $2^4 = 16$, $3^4 = 81$, $7^4 = 2401$.

  2. Possible units digits: $0, 1, 5, 6$.

    Ten cases cover every integer at once.

$0, 1, 5, 6$ only

Only ten cases ever matter for a units-digit question.

Two powers added

Find the units digit of $13^{41} + 22^{30}$.

  1. $13^{41}$ ends like $3^{41}$. Since $41 = 4 \cdot 10 + 1$, it's the 1st entry of $3, 9, 7, 1$: units digit $3$.

    Ignore the tens digit of the base.

  2. $22^{30}$ ends like $2^{30}$. Cycle $2, 4, 8, 6$; $30 = 4 \cdot 7 + 2$, so the 2nd entry: $4$.

    Same method, different cycle.

  3. $3 + 4 = 7$.

    Add the last digits.

$7$

A sum of factorials

Find the units digit of $1! + 2! + 3! + \cdots + 50!$.

  1. For $k \ge 5$, $k!$ contains both $2$ and $5$ as factors, so it ends in $0$.

    $2 \cdot 5 = 10$ divides every factorial from $5!$ on.

  2. $1 + 2 + 6 + 24 = 33$, which ends in $3$.

    Only the first four terms can affect the units digit.

$3$

The last two digits

Find the last two digits of $7^{2026}$.

  1. The last two digits of a product depend only on the last two digits of the factors.

    Write each number as $100q + r$; the $100q$ parts contribute only multiples of $100$.

  2. $7^2 = 49$, and $7^4 = 49^2 = 2401$, which ends in $01$.

    Ending in $01$ is the two-digit version of ending in $1$: the cycle restarts.

  3. $2026 = 4 \cdot 506 + 2$, so $7^{2026} = (7^4)^{506}\cdot 7^2$ ends like $01 \cdot 49$.

    A number ending in $01$ times one ending in $49$ ends in $49$.

$49$

Find a power that ends in $1$ (or $01$); then the exponent only matters through its remainder.

5.4 Modular Arithmetic

Hook

It's 9 o'clock. What time will it be in 100 hours? You don't count all 100: the clock forgets every complete trip of 12 hours. $100 = 8 \cdot 12 + 4$, so it's the same as 4 hours later: 1 o'clock. Arithmetic that forgets multiples of a fixed number is called modular arithmetic.

Concept

Fix a positive integer $m$, the modulus. Two integers are congruent mod $m$ if they leave the same remainder when divided by $m$, or equivalently if their difference is a multiple of $m$:

$$a \equiv b \pmod m \iff m \mid (a - b).$$

So $17 \equiv 2 \pmod 5$, and also $17 \equiv -3 \pmod 5$: negative representatives are allowed and often handy. Finding the smallest nonnegative number congruent to $a$ is called "reducing" $a$ mod $m$; it's just the remainder. (It's also the last digit of $a$ written in base $m$.)

Congruences can be added, subtracted, multiplied and raised to positive integer powers. If $a \equiv b$ and $c \equiv d \pmod m$, then

$$a + c \equiv b + d, \qquad a - c \equiv b - d, \qquad ac \equiv bd, \qquad a^k \equiv b^k \pmod m.$$

In practice: reduce first, then compute. The numbers stay small.

Division is not allowed in general. $10 \equiv 20 \pmod{10}$, but dividing both sides by $10$ gives $1 \equiv 2$, which is false.

Reduce, including a negative

Reduce $47$ mod $6$ and $-8$ mod $5$.

  1. $47 = 7 \cdot 6 + 5$, so $47 \equiv 5 \pmod 6$.

    The reduced value is the remainder.

  2. $-8 = (-2)\cdot 5 + 2$, so $-8 \equiv 2 \pmod 5$.

    Choose the multiple just below $-8$ so the remainder is between $0$ and $4$. Check: $-8 - 2 = -10$ is a multiple of $5$.

$47 \equiv 5 \pmod 6$ and $-8 \equiv 2 \pmod 5$.

Test a congruence

Is $83 \equiv 23 \pmod{12}$? Is $83 \equiv 23 \pmod{7}$?

  1. $83 - 23 = 60$.

    Congruence is about the difference.

  2. $12 \mid 60$, so yes mod $12$. But $7 \nmid 60$, so no mod $7$.

    $60 = 5 \cdot 12$, while $60 = 8 \cdot 7 + 4$.

Yes mod $12$; no mod $7$.

Counting members of a class

How many integers from $1$ to $200$ are congruent to $4$ mod $7$?

  1. Such numbers have the form $7k + 4$. The smallest is $4$ ($k = 0$).

    Every member of the class is $4$ more than a multiple of $7$.

  2. Largest: $200 = 7 \cdot 28 + 4$, so $200$ itself works ($k = 28$).

    Reduce the upper bound to see where the class ends.

  3. $k$ runs over $0, 1, \ldots, 28$: that's $29$ values.

    Counting from $0$ to $28$ inclusive gives $28 + 1$.

Reduce before multiplying

Find the remainder when $1234 \times 5678$ is divided by $7$.

  1. $1234 = 7 \cdot 176 + 2$, so $1234 \equiv 2$. $5678 = 7 \cdot 811 + 1$, so $5678 \equiv 1$.

    Reduce each factor first; the numbers become tiny.

  2. $1234 \times 5678 \equiv 2 \times 1 = 2 \pmod 7$.

    Multiplication respects congruence.

A huge power mod 7

Find the remainder when $2^{100}$ is divided by $7$.

  1. $2^3 = 8 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$.

    A power congruent to $1$ makes the rest collapse.

  2. $2^{100} = (2^3)^{33}\cdot 2 \equiv 1^{33}\cdot 2 = 2 \pmod 7$.

    $100 = 3 \cdot 33 + 1$, and we may raise congruences to powers.

$2$

Algebra with congruences

When $n$ is divided by $5$ the remainder is $3$. What is the remainder when $4n + 7$ is divided by $5$?

  1. $n \equiv 3 \pmod 5$, so $4n + 7 \equiv 4 \cdot 3 + 7 = 19 \pmod 5$.

    Substitute any number in the class; the answer can't depend on which.

  2. $19 \equiv 4 \pmod 5$.

    Reduce at the end. Check with $n = 8$: $4 \cdot 8 + 7 = 39 \equiv 4$. ✓

$4$

Why you can't just divide

Find all $x$ in $\{0, 1, \ldots, 8\}$ with $6x \equiv 6 \pmod 9$.

  1. Test each value: $6x$ for $x = 0, \ldots, 8$ is $0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48$, which reduce to $0, 6, 3, 0, 6, 3, 0, 6, 3$.

    With only nine cases, checking them all is safest.

  2. $6x \equiv 6$ for $x = 1, 4, 7$.

    "Dividing by $6$" would have found only $x = 1$.

  3. The reason: $6x \equiv 6$ means $9 \mid 6(x-1)$, which only needs $3 \mid x - 1$, since $6$ already supplies one factor of $3$.

    Because $6$ and $9$ share a factor, cancelling the $6$ loses information.

$x = 1, 4, 7$

Never cancel a factor in a congruence without checking; it can lose solutions or create false statements.

Not a sum of two squares

Show that $2023$ cannot be written as $a^2 + b^2$ for integers $a, b$.

  1. Mod $4$, every integer is $\equiv 0, 1, 2$ or $3$, and the squares are $0, 1, 4, 9 \equiv 0, 1, 0, 1$.

    Squaring respects congruence, so four cases cover all integers.

  2. So $a^2 + b^2 \equiv 0 + 0,\ 0 + 1$ or $1 + 1$, that is, $0, 1$ or $2 \pmod 4$.

    Add the possible remainders in every combination.

  3. $2023 = 4 \cdot 505 + 3 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, which is not on the list.

    A sum of two squares can never be $3$ mod $4$.

Impossible, since $2023 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ while $a^2 + b^2 \not\equiv 3 \pmod 4$.

To prove something is impossible, find a modulus where the two sides can never match.

Use $-1$ as a stepping stone

Find the remainder when $5^{100}$ is divided by $13$.

  1. $5^2 = 25 = 26 - 1 \equiv -1 \pmod{13}$.

    Being $1$ less than a multiple of $13$ is as useful as being $1$ more.

  2. $5^{100} = (5^2)^{50} \equiv (-1)^{50} = 1 \pmod{13}$.

    An even power of $-1$ is $1$.

$1$

5.5 Tricks

Hook

Is $8{,}675{,}309$ divisible by $3$? You could do long division, or you could add its digits: $8 + 6 + 7 + 5 + 3 + 0 + 9 = 38$, and $38$ isn't a multiple of $3$, so neither is the big number. That shortcut isn't magic; it's modular arithmetic in disguise.

Concept

A number $N$ is divisible by $d$ exactly when $N \equiv 0 \pmod d$. Each divisibility trick comes from knowing what $10$ (and its powers) are mod $d$.

Last-digit tests. Since $10 \equiv 0 \pmod 2$ and $\pmod 5$, a number is congruent to its last digit mod $2$, $5$ and $10$. Since $100 \equiv 0 \pmod 4$, a number is congruent to its last two digits mod $4$; since $1000 \equiv 0 \pmod 8$, to its last three digits mod $8$.

Digit sums. Since $10 \equiv 1 \pmod 9$, every power of $10$ is $\equiv 1$, so

$$N \equiv (\text{sum of its digits}) \pmod 9 \text{ and } \pmod 3.$$

Alternating sums. Since $10 \equiv -1 \pmod{11}$, powers of $10$ alternate $1, -1, 1, \ldots$, so $N$ is congruent mod $11$ to its digits added with alternating signs, starting with $+$ on the units digit.

Composite divisors. To test $12$, test $4$ and $3$; to test $36$, test $4$ and $9$. Use factors that share no common factor.

Tests by 4 and 8

Is $7452$ divisible by $4$? By $8$?

  1. Last two digits: $52 = 4 \cdot 13$, so $4 \mid 7452$.

    $7400$ is a multiple of $100$, hence of $4$; only $52$ matters.

  2. Last three digits: $452 = 8 \cdot 56 + 4$, so $8 \nmid 7452$.

    $7000$ is a multiple of $1000$, hence of $8$; only $452$ matters.

Divisible by $4$, not by $8$.

Digit sum for 3 and 9

Is $48{,}213$ divisible by $3$? By $9$?

  1. $4 + 8 + 2 + 1 + 3 = 18$.

    The number is congruent to its digit sum mod $9$ (and mod $3$).

  2. $18$ is a multiple of both $3$ and $9$, so $48{,}213$ is too.

    Indeed $48{,}213 = 9 \cdot 5357$.

Divisible by both $3$ and $9$.

The remainder, not just yes or no

Find the remainder when $8{,}675{,}309$ is divided by $9$.

  1. Digit sum: $8 + 6 + 7 + 5 + 3 + 0 + 9 = 38$.

    The rule gives a congruence, so it finds remainders too.

  2. Repeat: $3 + 8 = 11$, and $1 + 1 = 2$.

    $38$ is itself congruent to its digit sum, so we can keep going.

$2$

Alternating sum for 11

Is $918{,}082$ divisible by $11$?

    $10 \equiv -1 \pmod{11}$, so the digit in the $10^k$ place gets sign $(-1)^k$.

    A negative multiple of $11$ (or $0$) counts. Indeed $918{,}082 = 11 \cdot 83{,}462$.

Combine two tests

Is $5412$ divisible by $12$?

  1. By $4$: $12 = 4 \cdot 3$ ✓. By $3$: $5 + 4 + 1 + 2 = 12$ ✓.

    $12 = 4 \cdot 3$ with $4$ and $3$ sharing no factor, so passing both tests is enough.

  2. So $12 \mid 5412$. (Indeed $5412 = 12 \cdot 451$.)

    Don't use $2$ and $6$ instead: they share a factor, and $18$ passes both tests without being a multiple of $12$.

Find the missing digit

The four-digit number $47d2$ is divisible by $36$. Find the digit $d$.

  1. $36 = 4 \cdot 9$, so we need both $4 \mid 47d2$ and $9 \mid 47d2$.

    Split into factors with no common factor.

  2. For $9$: $4 + 7 + d + 2 = 13 + d$ must be a multiple of $9$, so $d = 5$.

    $d$ is a digit, so $13 + d$ lies between $13$ and $22$; the only multiple of $9$ there is $18$.

  3. For $4$: the last two digits are $52$, and $4 \mid 52$. ✓

    Always confirm the other condition. $4752 = 36 \cdot 132$.

$d = 5$

Every palindrome of length four

Show that every four-digit number of the form $abba$ (such as $3773$) is divisible by $11$.

  1. Alternating sum from the right: $a - b + b - a = 0$.

    The digits from the right are $a, b, b, a$ with signs $+, -, +, -$.

  2. $0$ is divisible by $11$, so $11 \mid abba$.

    For instance $3773 = 11 \cdot 343$.

The alternating digit sum is always $0$, so $11$ divides $abba$.

A multiple made of 8s and 0s

Find the smallest positive multiple of $15$ whose digits are all $8$s and $0$s.

  1. Divisible by $5$ means the last digit is $0$ or $5$; with only $8$s and $0$s it must end in $0$.

    $15 = 3 \cdot 5$; handle each factor.

  2. Divisible by $3$: the digit sum is $8k$ for $k$ eights, and $3 \mid 8k$ requires $3 \mid k$.

    $8$ and $3$ share no factor, so the $3$ must come from $k$.

  3. Fewest digits: three $8$s and a final $0$, giving $8880$.

    Any valid number has at least $4$ digits, and among those $8880$ is the only one.

$8880$ (which is $15 \cdot 592$)

Build your own rule: 99

Find the remainder when $123{,}456{,}789$ is divided by $99$.

  1. $100 \equiv 1 \pmod{99}$, so every power of $100$ is $\equiv 1$.

    This is the digit-sum idea, but using two-digit blocks, since $100$ is the "base" here.

  2. Split into pairs from the right: $1 \mid 23 \mid 45 \mid 67 \mid 89$. Sum: $1 + 23 + 45 + 67 + 89 = 225$.

    $123456789 = 1 \cdot 100^4 + 23 \cdot 100^3 + 45 \cdot 100^2 + 67 \cdot 100 + 89$.

  3. Repeat: $225 \to 2 + 25 = 27$.

    $27 < 99$, so that's the remainder.

$27$

Every divisibility rule comes from what $10$, $100$ or $1000$ is congruent to.

5.6 Primes

Hook

Chemists describe every substance by the elements it's made of. Number theorists do the same with primes: $360$ is "three 2s, two 3s and a 5", and that description is unique. Primes are the atoms of multiplication.

Concept

Every integer greater than $1$ can be written as a product of primes, and apart from the order of the factors there is only one way to do it. This is the prime factorization; we usually group equal primes with exponents, as in $360 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$.

To factor a number, divide out small primes one at a time, using the tricks from Section 5.5 to spot $2, 3, 5, 11$ quickly. Always keep dividing the quotient.

If $N > 1$ has no prime divisor $p$ with $p \le \sqrt N$, then $N$ is prime.

The reason: if $N = ab$ with both $a, b > \sqrt N$, then $ab > N$, a contradiction. So a composite number always has a factor no bigger than its square root.

There are infinitely many primes. Suppose there were only finitely many, $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n$. The number $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n + 1$ leaves remainder $1$ when divided by every $p_i$, yet it must have some prime factor. That factor is a prime not on the list, a contradiction.

A first factorization

Find the prime factorization of $360$.

  1. $360 = 10 \cdot 36 = (2 \cdot 5)(2^2 \cdot 3^2)$.

    Any convenient split works; uniqueness guarantees the same final answer.

  2. Collect: $2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$.

    Group equal primes with exponents.

$2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$

Primes in a range

List the primes between $30$ and $50$.

  1. Skip evens and multiples of $5$: candidates $31, 33, 37, 39, 41, 43, 47, 49$.

    Remove the easy composites first.

  2. Remove $33 = 3 \cdot 11$, $39 = 3 \cdot 13$, $49 = 7^2$.

    Since $\sqrt{50} < 8$, testing $3$ and $7$ is all that's left.

$31, 37, 41, 43, 47$

Prime or not: 221

Is $221$ prime?

  1. $14^2 = 196$ and $15^2 = 225$, so $\sqrt{221} < 15$. Test primes up to $13$.

    A composite $221$ must have a prime factor at most $\sqrt{221}$.

  2. $2, 3, 5$ fail (odd; digit sum $5$; last digit $1$). $221 = 7 \cdot 31 + 4$, and alternating sum $1 - 2 + 2 = 1$, so $7$ and $11$ fail.

    Use the tricks wherever possible.

  3. $221 = 13 \cdot 17$.

    Found a factor, so $221$ is composite.

Prime or not: 197

Is $197$ prime?

  1. $14^2 = 196 < 197 < 225 = 15^2$, so test primes up to $13$.

    Only $2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13$ need checking.

  2. Odd; digit sum $17$; ends in $7$; $197 = 7 \cdot 28 + 1$; alternating sum $7 - 9 + 1 = -1$; $197 = 13 \cdot 15 + 2$.

    None of the six primes divides $197$.

    No prime factor up to $\sqrt{197}$ means no factor at all.

Factor a larger number with the tricks

Find the prime factorization of $13{,}860$.

  1. Ends in $0$: $13860 = 10 \cdot 1386 = 2 \cdot 5 \cdot 1386$. Even again: $1386 = 2 \cdot 693$.

    Strip off $2$s and $5$s first.

  2. $693$: digit sum $18$, so $9 \mid 693$ and $693 = 9 \cdot 77$.

    The digit-sum test spots factors of $3$ and $9$.

  3. $77 = 7 \cdot 11$. Altogether $13860 = 2^2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11$.

    Check: $4 \cdot 9 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 = 13860$. ✓

$2^2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11$

Count divisors from the factorization

How many positive divisors does $360$ have?

  1. Every divisor has the form $2^a 3^b 5^c$ with $0 \le a \le 3$, $0 \le b \le 2$, $0 \le c \le 1$.

    By uniqueness of factorization, a divisor can't contain any other prime or a higher power.

  2. Choices: $4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 24$.

    Each exponent is chosen independently.

$24$

If $N = p^a q^b r^c \cdots$, then $N$ has $(a+1)(b+1)(c+1)\cdots$ divisors.

Euclid's number is not always prime

Compute $P = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 + 1$. Is it prime? How does this fit the proof that there are infinitely many primes?

  1. $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 = 30030$, so $P = 30031$.

    Multiply step by step: $210 \cdot 143 = 30030$.

  2. $30031 = 59 \cdot 509$, so $P$ is composite.

    Trial division finds it eventually; it's not prime.

  3. But $59$ and $509$ are primes not in the list $2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13$.

    The proof only needs some prime factor of $P$ to be new, and every prime factor of $P$ is new, because each listed prime leaves remainder $1$.

$P = 30031 = 59 \cdot 509$ is composite, but its prime factors are new primes, exactly as the proof needs.

A prime triple

Find all primes $p$ such that $p$, $p + 10$ and $p + 20$ are all prime.

  1. Mod $3$: $10 \equiv 1$ and $20 \equiv 2$, so the three numbers are $\equiv p,\ p + 1,\ p + 2$.

    Three consecutive residues mod $3$ cover $0, 1, 2$, so one of the numbers is divisible by $3$.

  2. A prime divisible by $3$ must be $3$ itself. Since $p + 10$ and $p + 20$ exceed $3$, we need $p = 3$.

    The only way to survive is for the multiple of $3$ to be $3$.

  3. Check: $3, 13, 23$ are all prime. ✓

    The argument shows nothing else can work; the check shows $p = 3$ does.

$p = 3$ only

Modular arithmetic can force a factor, ruling out primes all at once.

5.7 Common and Uncommon Factors

Hook

One light blinks every 12 seconds and another every 18 seconds. They just blinked together. When will they next blink together? And what is the largest square tile that exactly fills a 12-by-18 floor? The first question asks for what the two numbers have in common as multiples; the second for what they share as factors.

Concept

The greatest common factor (GCF, or gcd) of $a$ and $b$ is the largest integer dividing both; it's written $(a, b)$ or $\gcd(a,b)$. The least common multiple (LCM) is the smallest positive integer that both divide; it's written $[a, b]$ or $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$. If $(a, b) = 1$, then $a$ and $b$ are relatively prime.

Prime factorizations make both easy. For $84 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 7$ and $112 = 2^4 \cdot 7$:

GCF: the smaller power of each shared prime, $2^2 \cdot 7 = 28$. LCM: the larger power of every prime present, $2^4 \cdot 3 \cdot 7 = 336$.

Since each prime's two exponents split into "the smaller" (in the GCF) and "the larger" (in the LCM),

$$\gcd(a, b)\cdot \operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = ab.$$

The least common denominator for adding fractions is just an LCM. And any common factor of $a$ and $b$ also divides $a - b$, which helps when the numbers are close together.

Start small

Find $\gcd(18, 24)$ and $\operatorname{lcm}(18, 24)$.

  1. $18 = 2 \cdot 3^2$ and $24 = 2^3 \cdot 3$.

    Factor first.

  2. GCF $= 2 \cdot 3 = 6$; LCM $= 2^3 \cdot 3^2 = 72$.

    Smaller powers for the GCF, larger powers for the LCM. Check: $6 \cdot 72 = 432 = 18 \cdot 24$. ✓

GCF $6$, LCM $72$

Relatively prime?

Are $35$ and $72$ relatively prime?

  1. $35 = 5 \cdot 7$ and $72 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2$.

    Compare the primes.

  2. No prime in common, so $\gcd(35, 72) = 1$.

    Neither number needs to be prime for them to be relatively prime.

GCF and LCM from factorizations

Find $(84, 126)$ and $[84, 126]$.

  1. $84 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 7$ and $126 = 2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 7$.

    Line up the primes $2, 3, 7$.

  2. GCF: $2^1 \cdot 3^1 \cdot 7 = 42$.

Gears meeting again

Two meshing gears have $18$ and $30$ teeth. A marked tooth on each is touching. How many turns does each gear make before those two marks touch again?

Count pairs with a given GCF and LCM

How many ordered pairs of positive integers $(a, b)$ have $\gcd(a,b) = 6$ and $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = 60$?

  1. Write $a = 6x$, $b = 6y$ with $\gcd(x, y) = 1$.

    Both are multiples of $6$; dividing out the GCF leaves relatively prime parts.

  2. Then $ab = 36xy = 6 \cdot 60 = 360$, so $xy = 10$.

    Use GCF $\times$ LCM $=$ product.

  3. Relatively prime pairs with product $10$: $(1, 10), (2, 5), (5, 2), (10, 1)$.

    Each of the primes $2$ and $5$ goes entirely to $x$ or entirely to $y$, giving $2 \cdot 2 = 4$ choices.

  4. So $(a, b) = (6, 60), (12, 30), (30, 12), (60, 6)$.

    Check one: $\gcd(12, 30) = 6$, $\operatorname{lcm}(12, 30) = 60$. ✓
