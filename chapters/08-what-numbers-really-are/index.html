Chapter 8 · Algebra & Number

What Numbers Really Are

You have used numbers for years, but could you say exactly what a rational number is, or prove that some number is not one? This chapter builds the number system one layer at a time: integers, then fractions and their decimals, then the irrationals that fractions can't reach, and finally the complex numbers, where every polynomial has its roots.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • A rational number is $\frac{p}{q}$ with integers $p, q$ and $q > 0$. Every integer is rational ($n = \frac{n}{1}$).
  • Fraction to decimal: long division. The decimal terminates exactly when the reduced denominator has no prime factors other than $2$ and $5$; otherwise it repeats.
  • Repeating decimal to fraction: multiply by $10^k$ ($k$ = length of the repeating block) and subtract to cancel the endless tail.
  • Compare $\frac ab$ and $\frac cd$ (positive denominators) by comparing $ad$ and $bc$: put a "?" between them and clear denominators.
  • Lowest terms: numerator and denominator share no common factor. Assuming a fraction is in lowest terms and finding a common factor is the classic irrationality proof.
  • Rational $\pm$ irrational is irrational; a rational multiple (nonzero) of an irrational is irrational.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: a polynomial of degree $n \ge 1$ has a complex root, so it factors into $n$ linear factors over $\mathbb{C}$. Real polynomials have non-real roots in conjugate pairs.

8.1 Integers and Rationals

Hook

Type $1 \div 7$ into a calculator: $0.142857142\ldots$ The six digits $142857$ repeat forever. Try $1 \div 8$ and the decimal simply stops at $0.125$. Why do some fractions stop while others cycle, and why is the cycle for sevenths exactly six digits long? The answer is hiding in the remainders of long division.

Concept

The natural numbers $1, 2, 3, \ldots$ count things. Adding $0$ and the negatives gives the integers $\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots$. Watch the wording in problems: positive integers start at $1$, while nonnegative integers include $0$.

A rational number is a ratio $\frac{p}{q}$ of integers with $q > 0$; $p$ is the numerator and $q$ the denominator. Every integer is rational, since $n = \frac{n}{1}$.

Long division turns any fraction into a decimal. At each step the remainder is one of $0, 1, \ldots, q-1$. If a remainder is $0$, the decimal terminates. If not, some remainder must eventually come back, and from then on the digits repeat. The repeating block can be at most $q - 1$ digits long.

In lowest terms, $\dfrac{p}{q}$ terminates $\iff$ $q = 2^a 5^b$ for some $a, b \ge 0$.

Going backwards, a terminating decimal is a fraction over a power of ten, such as $0.375 = \frac{375}{1000}$. For a repeating decimal, shift by the length of the block and subtract:

$$x = 0.\overline{27} \;\Rightarrow\; 100x = 27.\overline{27} \;\Rightarrow\; 99x = 27 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \tfrac{27}{99} = \tfrac{3}{11}.$$

To decide which of two fractions is larger, write $\frac ab \;?\; \frac cd$ with the unknown symbol in the middle. Multiplying both sides by the positive numbers $b$ and $d$ doesn't change the symbol, so it is the same as the one in $ad \;?\; bc$.

Sorting numbers into sets

For each of $-3$, $0$, $\dfrac{12}{4}$, $2.5$ and $-\dfrac{4}{6}$, decide whether it is a positive integer, a nonnegative integer, an integer, and a rational number.

  1. $\dfrac{12}{4} = 3$: positive integer, nonnegative integer, integer, rational.

    Simplify first. What matters is the number, not how it's written.

  2. $0$: nonnegative integer, integer, rational, but not positive.

    Zero is neither positive nor negative, which is why "nonnegative" and "positive" differ.

  3. $-3$: integer and rational only.

    Negative, so neither positive nor nonnegative.

  4. $2.5 = \dfrac52$ and $-\dfrac46 = -\dfrac23$: rational, not integers.

    Each is a ratio of integers with positive denominator, but neither is a whole number.

Only $\frac{12}{4} = 3$ is a positive integer; $0$ and $3$ are nonnegative integers; $-3, 0, 3$ are integers; all five are rational.

A terminating decimal as a fraction

Write $0.375$ as a fraction in lowest terms.

  1. $0.375 = \dfrac{375}{1000}$.

    Three digits after the point means a denominator of $10^3$.

  2. $\dfrac{375}{1000} = \dfrac{3 \cdot 125}{8 \cdot 125} = \dfrac38$.

    Divide top and bottom by their greatest common factor, $125$.

Long division that starts repeating late

Write $\dfrac{5}{12}$ as a decimal.

  1. $50 \div 12 = 4$ remainder $2$. Digit: $4$.

    Bring down a zero after $5$ and divide.

  2. $20 \div 12 = 1$ remainder $8$. Digit: $1$.

    Each new remainder, times ten, is the next thing to divide.

  3. $80 \div 12 = 6$ remainder $8$. Digit: $6$.

    The remainder $8$ has appeared before, so the same step will repeat forever.

  4. $\dfrac{5}{12} = 0.41666\ldots = 0.41\overline{6}$.

    Only the $6$ repeats; the $4$ and $1$ come before the loop.

$0.41\overline{6}$

A pure repeating decimal

Write $0.\overline{36}$ as a fraction in lowest terms.

  1. Let $x = 0.363636\ldots$. Then $100x = 36.363636\ldots$

    The block has length $2$, so multiplying by $10^2$ lines the tails up exactly.

  2. Subtract: $100x - x = 36$, so $99x = 36$.

    The infinite tails are identical and cancel completely.

  3. $x = \dfrac{36}{99} = \dfrac{4}{11}$.

    Divide top and bottom by $9$.

$\dfrac{4}{11}$

A decimal with a delay before it repeats

Write $2.1\overline{45} = 2.1454545\ldots$ as a fraction in lowest terms.

  1. Let $x = 2.1454545\ldots$. Then $10x = 21.454545\ldots$ and $1000x = 2145.454545\ldots$

    First shift the non-repeating digit past the point ($\times 10$); then shift one more full block ($\times 1000$).

  2. $1000x - 10x = 2145 - 21 = 2124$, so $990x = 2124$.

    Both numbers end in the same tail $.4545\ldots$, so it cancels.

  3. $x = \dfrac{2124}{990} = \dfrac{118}{55}$.

    $\gcd(2124, 990) = 18$. Check: $118 \div 55 = 2.14545\ldots$ ✓

$\dfrac{118}{55}$

Which fractions terminate?

Without dividing, decide which of $\dfrac{7}{40}$, $\dfrac{3}{14}$, $\dfrac{21}{56}$ and $\dfrac{11}{75}$ have terminating decimals.

  1. Reduce each fraction: $\dfrac{7}{40}$, $\dfrac{3}{14}$, $\dfrac{21}{56} = \dfrac{3}{8}$, $\dfrac{11}{75}$.

    The rule applies to the denominator in lowest terms; a common factor could hide or create a bad prime.

  2. $40 = 2^3 \cdot 5$ ✓, $14 = 2 \cdot 7$ ✗, $8 = 2^3$ ✓, $75 = 3 \cdot 5^2$ ✗.

    A fraction over $2^a5^b$ can be rewritten over a power of $10$; any other prime prevents that.

  3. Indeed $\dfrac{7}{40} = 0.175$ and $\dfrac38 = 0.375$.

    For example, $\frac{7}{40} = \frac{7 \cdot 25}{40 \cdot 25} = \frac{175}{1000}$.

$\dfrac{7}{40}$ and $\dfrac{21}{56}$ terminate; $\dfrac{3}{14}$ and $\dfrac{11}{75}$ repeat.

Compare two close fractions

Which is larger, $\dfrac{13}{29}$ or $\dfrac{22}{49}$?

  1. Write $\dfrac{13}{29} \;?\; \dfrac{22}{49}$ and multiply both sides by $29 \cdot 49$: $13 \cdot 49 \;?\; 22 \cdot 29$.

    Multiplying by a positive number keeps the direction of any inequality.

  2. $637 \;?\; 638$, so the symbol is $<$.

    The integers are easy to compare.

$\dfrac{22}{49}$ is larger (just barely).

Is 0.999... really 1?

Find the fraction equal to $0.\overline{9}$.

  1. Let $x = 0.999\ldots$. Then $10x = 9.999\ldots$

    The repeating block has length $1$.

  2. $10x - x = 9$, so $9x = 9$ and $x = 1$.

    The same subtraction trick as before; nothing special happens just because the digit is $9$.

  3. Another view: $\tfrac13 = 0.\overline{3}$, and tripling gives $1 = 0.\overline{9}$.

    Two different decimals can name the same number; $0.\overline{9}$ and $1$ have no gap between them.

$0.\overline{9} = 1$

Adding one to the top and bottom

If $a$ and $b$ are positive integers with $a < b$, which is larger: $\dfrac{a}{b}$ or $\dfrac{a+1}{b+1}$?

  1. $\dfrac{a}{b} \;?\; \dfrac{a+1}{b+1}$ becomes $a(b+1) \;?\; b(a+1)$.

    Clear the positive denominators $b$ and $b + 1$.

  2. $ab + a \;?\; ab + b$, so $a \;?\; b$.

    Subtract $ab$ from both sides; the symbol doesn't change.

  3. We know $a < b$, so the symbol is $<$ and $\dfrac{a}{b} < \dfrac{a+1}{b+1}$.

    Working backwards from a known fact reveals the original symbol. Example: $\frac23 < \frac34$.

$\dfrac{a+1}{b+1}$ is larger.

Put a "?" between two quantities, simplify, and read off the symbol at the end.

How long can the cycle be?

Find the repeating block of $\dfrac{1}{13}$ and explain why no fraction with denominator $13$ can have a block longer than $12$ digits.

  1. Remainders: $1 \to 10 \to 9 \to 12 \to 3 \to 4 \to 1$. Digits: $10 \div 13 = 0$, $100 \div 13 = 7$, $90 \div 13 = 6$, $120 \div 13 = 9$, $30 \div 13 = 2$, $40 \div 13 = 3$.

    Each step multiplies the remainder by $10$ and divides by $13$; the remainder $1$ returns after six steps.

  2. So $\dfrac{1}{13} = 0.\overline{076923}$, a block of $6$ digits.

    Once a remainder repeats, every later step repeats too.

  3. For a repeating decimal, the remainders are all from $\{1, 2, \ldots, 12\}$. Within $12$ steps one must repeat, so the block has at most $12$ digits.

    There are only $12$ possible nonzero remainders, so after at most $12$ steps the division must return to one it has already seen.

$\dfrac{1}{13} = 0.\overline{076923}$; a block for denominator $13$ has at most $12$ digits.

Finitely many remainders force every rational decimal to stop or repeat.

8.2 Lowest Terms and Irrationals

Hook

$\frac{7}{5} = 1.4$, $\frac{17}{12} \approx 1.4167$, $\frac{41}{29} \approx 1.41379$, $\frac{99}{70} \approx 1.414286$. These fractions squeeze ever closer to $\sqrt2 \approx 1.414214$. Is there some fraction, perhaps with a huge denominator, that hits $\sqrt2$ exactly? Over two thousand years ago, a short argument about lowest terms answered no.

Concept

A fraction $\frac pq$ is in lowest terms when $p$ and $q$ have no common factor greater than $1$. Every rational can be reduced to lowest terms by dividing out $\gcd(p, q)$, and that form is unique.

That simple fact powers proof by contradiction. To show $\sqrt2$ is not rational:

  1. Suppose $\sqrt2 = \frac pq$ in lowest terms. Then $p^2 = 2q^2$, so $p^2$ is even, and therefore $p$ is even (an odd number squared is odd).
  2. Write $p = 2r$. Then $4r^2 = 2q^2$, so $q^2 = 2r^2$ and $q$ is even too.
  3. Now $p$ and $q$ share the factor $2$, contradicting lowest terms. So no such fraction exists.

Numbers that are not rational are irrational. Their decimals never terminate and never repeat. Rationals and irrationals together form the real numbers. Some irrationals, like $\sqrt2$, are roots of integer polynomials ($x^2 - 2 = 0$); others, like $\pi$ and $e$, are not, and are called transcendental.

Useful facts: if $r$ is rational and $x$ irrational, then $r + x$ and $rx$ (for $r \ne 0$) are irrational, because otherwise you could solve for $x$ as a rational. But two irrationals can add or multiply to a rational: $\sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt2 = 2$.

Irrationals can be approached by rationals as closely as we like, for example $1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, \ldots$ for $\sqrt2$, and that is how they are built rigorously from the rationals.

Reduce to lowest terms

Write $\dfrac{84}{126}$ in lowest terms.

  1. $84 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 7$ and $126 = 2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 7$, so $\gcd(84, 126) = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 7 = 42$.

    The gcd takes each shared prime to the smaller power.

  2. $\dfrac{84}{126} = \dfrac{84 \div 42}{126 \div 42} = \dfrac23$.

    Dividing by the gcd removes every common factor in one step.

$\dfrac{2}{3}$

Rational or not?

Which of $\sqrt{49}$, $\sqrt{\tfrac{9}{16}}$, $\sqrt{0.04}$, $0.\overline{3}$ and $\sqrt{50}$ are rational?

  1. $\sqrt{49} = 7$, $\sqrt{\tfrac9{16}} = \tfrac34$, $\sqrt{0.04} = 0.2$, $0.\overline3 = \tfrac13$.

    Each simplifies to a ratio of integers.

  2. $\sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt2$, which is irrational.

    A nonzero rational times the irrational $\sqrt2$ is irrational.

All except $\sqrt{50}$ are rational.

Prove that the square root of 3 is irrational

Prove that $\sqrt3$ is irrational.

  1. Suppose $\sqrt3 = \dfrac pq$ in lowest terms. Then $p^2 = 3q^2$.

    Square both sides and clear the denominator.

  2. $3$ divides $p^2$, so $3$ divides $p$. Write $p = 3r$.

    $3$ is prime: if it divides a product $p \cdot p$, it divides one of the factors.

  3. $9r^2 = 3q^2$, so $q^2 = 3r^2$, and $3$ divides $q$ as well.

    The same reasoning applied to $q$.

  4. Then $3$ divides both $p$ and $q$, contradicting lowest terms. So $\sqrt3$ is irrational.

    The assumption led to something impossible, so it was false.

$\sqrt3$ is irrational.

A decimal that never settles

Is $0.101001000100001\ldots$ (one more $0$ before each new $1$) rational?

  1. Every rational has a decimal that terminates or eventually repeats a fixed block.

    That's the remainder argument from Section 8.1.

  2. This decimal doesn't terminate (it has infinitely many $1$s). If it repeated a block of length $k$, it could never contain a run of more than $k$ zeros in a row, but its runs of zeros grow without bound.

    A repeating block of length $k$ that contains a $1$ puts a $1$ in every stretch of $k$ digits.

  3. So the decimal neither terminates nor repeats, and the number is irrational.

    It isn't any fraction.

No; it is irrational.

Rational plus irrational

Prove that $1 + \sqrt2$ is irrational.

  1. Suppose $1 + \sqrt2 = r$ for some rational $r$.

    Assume the opposite and look for a contradiction.

  2. Then $\sqrt2 = r - 1$, which is rational.

    The difference of two rationals is rational: $\frac ab - 1 = \frac{a-b}{b}$.

  3. But $\sqrt2$ is irrational, so $1 + \sqrt2$ is irrational.

    Contradiction with the proof given in the Concept above.

$1 + \sqrt2$ is irrational.

Reduce before you decide

Are $\sqrt{\tfrac{18}{50}}$ and $\sqrt{\tfrac{12}{27}}$ rational?

  1. $\dfrac{18}{50} = \dfrac{9}{25}$, so $\sqrt{\tfrac{18}{50}} = \dfrac35$.

    Neither $18$ nor $50$ is a perfect square, yet the fraction is the square of $\frac35$, because they share the factor $2$.

  2. $\dfrac{12}{27} = \dfrac{4}{9}$, so $\sqrt{\tfrac{12}{27}} = \dfrac23$.

    Here the common factor is $3$.

  3. Both are rational.

    In lowest terms, a fraction has a rational square root exactly when its numerator and denominator are both perfect squares.

Yes: $\dfrac35$ and $\dfrac23$.

The square root of 6

Prove that $\sqrt6$ is irrational.

  1. Suppose $\sqrt6 = \dfrac pq$ in lowest terms, so $p^2 = 6q^2$. Then $p^2$ is even, so $p$ is even; write $p = 2r$.

    Same opening as for $\sqrt2$: $6q^2$ is even.

  2. $4r^2 = 6q^2$, so $2r^2 = 3q^2$. The left side is even, so $3q^2$ is even, which forces $q$ to be even.

    $3$ is odd, so $3q^2$ is even only if $q^2$ is even.

  3. Both $p$ and $q$ are even, contradicting lowest terms.

    The factor $2$ is shared.

$\sqrt6$ is irrational.

Two irrationals can make a rational

Find two irrational numbers whose sum is rational, and two whose product is rational.

  1. $\sqrt2$ and $3 - \sqrt2$ are both irrational, and their sum is $3$.

    $3 - \sqrt2$ is irrational by the rational-plus-irrational argument.

  2. $\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt8 = 2\sqrt2$ are irrational, and their product is $\sqrt{16} = 4$.

    Products of square roots can become perfect squares.

For example $\sqrt2 + (3 - \sqrt2) = 3$ and $\sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt8 = 4$.

Rational with irrational stays irrational, but irrational with irrational can go either way.

An irrational logarithm

Prove that $\log_2 3$ is irrational.

  1. $\log_2 3 > 0$, since $3 > 1$. Suppose $\log_2 3 = \dfrac pq$ with positive integers $p, q$.

    A positive rational can be written with a positive numerator and denominator.

  2. Then $2^{p/q} = 3$, so $2^p = 3^q$.

    Translate the log to an exponent and raise both sides to the power $q$.

  3. $2^p$ is even (since $p \ge 1$) but $3^q$ is odd. They can't be equal.

    A parity contradiction, with no need for lowest terms at all.

$\log_2 3$ is irrational.

Turn the assumed fraction into an equation between integers, then look for something impossible.

A sum of two square roots

Prove that $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$ is irrational.

  1. Suppose $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3 = r$, rational. Square: $2 + 2\sqrt6 + 3 = r^2$.

    Squaring merges the two roots into a single one.

  2. So $\sqrt6 = \dfrac{r^2 - 5}{2}$, which would be rational.

    Rationals are closed under squaring, subtracting and dividing by $2$.

  3. But $\sqrt6$ is irrational (shown above). Contradiction, so $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$ is irrational.

    Reduce a new claim to one already proved.

$\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$ is irrational.

8.3 Complex and Beyond

Hook

Every time the number system was extended, it was to solve an equation the old numbers couldn't: $x + 5 = 2$ needs negatives, $3x = 2$ needs fractions, $x^2 = 2$ needs irrationals, and $x^2 = -1$ needs $i$. Will $x^5 + x + 1 = 0$ force yet another kind of number? Remarkably, no. With the complex numbers the story ends.

Concept

A complex number is $a + bi$ with $a, b$ real and $i^2 = -1$ (Chapter 2). The real numbers are the complex numbers with $b = 0$. So the layers nest:

$$\text{natural numbers} \subset \text{integers} \subset \text{rationals} \subset \text{reals} \subset \text{complex numbers}.$$

Over the reals, $x^2 + 1$ has no roots. Over the complex numbers it has two, $\pm i$. The key theorem says this always works:

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. Every polynomial of degree $n \ge 1$ with real or complex coefficients has at least one complex root.

If $a$ is a root of $f(z)$, then $f(z) = (z - a)g(z)$ with $g$ of degree $n - 1$. Apply the theorem to $g$ and repeat: $f$ splits into exactly $n$ linear factors. Some roots may repeat, as in $(z-1)^3$, so we say a degree-$n$ polynomial has $n$ roots counted with multiplicity.

If the coefficients are real, non-real roots come in conjugate pairs $a \pm bi$, and each pair multiplies to a real quadratic $(x-a)^2 + b^2$. That's why a real cubic always has at least one real root.

The smallest set each number belongs to

Name the smallest of natural numbers, integers, rationals, reals and complex numbers that contains each of $7$, $-2$, $\tfrac34$, $\sqrt2$, $\pi$, $\sqrt{-4}$.

  1. $7$: natural numbers. $-2$: integers. $\tfrac34$: rationals.

    Each one is in that set but not in the one before it.

  2. $\sqrt2$ and $\pi$: reals (both irrational).

    Neither can be written as a fraction.

  3. $\sqrt{-4} = 2i$: complex numbers.

    No real number squares to $-4$.

$7$: natural; $-2$: integer; $\tfrac34$: rational; $\sqrt2, \pi$: real; $\sqrt{-4} = 2i$: complex.

No real roots, two complex ones

Solve $x^2 + 9 = 0$.

  1. $x^2 = -9$.

    Isolate the square. No real number works, since real squares are $\ge 0$.

  2. $x = \pm\sqrt{-9} = \pm 3i$. Check: $(3i)^2 = 9i^2 = -9$. ✓

    Over the complex numbers, the degree-$2$ equation has its $2$ roots.

$x = 3i$ or $x = -3i$

A conjugate pair from the quadratic formula

Solve $x^2 - 4x + 13 = 0$.

  1. Complete the square: $(x - 2)^2 + 9 = 0$, so $(x-2)^2 = -9$.

    $x^2 - 4x + 4 = (x-2)^2$, and $13 = 4 + 9$.

  2. $x - 2 = \pm 3i$, so $x = 2 \pm 3i$.

    Take both square roots of $-9$.

  3. Check with Vieta: sum $= 4$ ✓, product $= 4 + 9 = 13$ ✓.

    $(2+3i)(2-3i) = 4 - 9i^2 = 13$.

$x = 2 \pm 3i$

Counting roots with multiplicity

How many roots does $(x-1)^2(x+2)$ have? How many different roots?

  1. Expanded, the polynomial has degree $2 + 1 = 3$.

    Degrees add when polynomials multiply.

  2. The roots are $1, 1, -2$: three roots counted with multiplicity.

    The factor $(x-1)$ appears twice, so the root $1$ counts twice.

  3. Only two are different: $1$ and $-2$.

    This is why the theorem is stated as "at least one root" and then factored repeatedly, rather than "$n$ different roots."

$3$ roots with multiplicity; $2$ different roots.

All three cube roots of 8

Find all complex solutions of $x^3 = 8$.

  1. $x^3 - 8 = (x - 2)(x^2 + 2x + 4) = 0$.

    Difference of cubes from Chapter 7.

  2. $x = 2$, or $x^2 + 2x + 4 = 0$, giving $x = \dfrac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 16}}{2} = -1 \pm i\sqrt3$.

    The quadratic formula with discriminant $-12$, and $\sqrt{-12} = 2i\sqrt3$.

$x = 2$, $x = -1 + i\sqrt3$, $x = -1 - i\sqrt3$

Factoring depends on your numbers

Factor $x^4 - 16$ completely over the integers, and then over the complex numbers.

  1. $x^4 - 16 = (x^2 - 4)(x^2 + 4) = (x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)$.

    Difference of squares twice. Over the integers (and the reals) $x^2 + 4$ is stuck.

  2. Over $\mathbb{C}$: $x^2 + 4 = (x - 2i)(x + 2i)$.

    $x^2 + 4 = x^2 - (2i)^2$ is a difference of squares once $i$ is allowed.

  3. $x^4 - 16 = (x-2)(x+2)(x-2i)(x+2i)$.

    Four linear factors for a degree-$4$ polynomial, as the theorem promises.

Integers: $(x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)$. Complex: $(x-2)(x+2)(x-2i)(x+2i)$.

Build a polynomial from a complex root

Find a quadratic with integer coefficients that has $1 + i$ as a root.

  1. Its conjugate $1 - i$ must also be a root.

    Real polynomials have non-real roots in conjugate pairs.

  2. $\big(x - (1+i)\big)\big(x - (1-i)\big) = (x-1)^2 - i^2 = (x-1)^2 + 1$.

    Group as $\big((x-1) - i\big)\big((x-1) + i\big)$, a difference of squares.

  3. $= x^2 - 2x + 2$.

    Expand. Vieta check: sum $2$, product $(1+i)(1-i) = 2$. ✓

$x^2 - 2x + 2$

A cubic from one complex root

The cubic $x^3 + ax^2 + bx + 10$ has real coefficients $a$, $b$, and $2 - i$ is a root. Find $a$, $b$ and the other roots.

  1. $2 + i$ is also a root, and $\big(x - (2-i)\big)\big(x - (2+i)\big) = (x-2)^2 + 1 = x^2 - 4x + 5$.

    Conjugate pairs give a real quadratic factor.

  2. So $x^3 + ax^2 + bx + 10 = (x^2 - 4x + 5)(x + c)$. Constant terms: $5c = 10$, so $c = 2$.

    The leading coefficient is $1$, so the remaining factor is $x + c$.

  3. $(x^2 - 4x + 5)(x + 2) = x^3 - 2x^2 - 3x + 10$, so $a = -2$, $b = -3$.

    Expand: $x^2$ terms $2 - 4 = -2$; $x$ terms $5 - 8 = -3$.

  4. The roots are $2 - i$, $2 + i$ and $-2$.

    The third root comes from $x + 2 = 0$; a real cubic must have a real root.

$a = -2$, $b = -3$; the other roots are $2 + i$ and $-2$.

For real polynomials, one complex root hands you a second one for free.

The cube roots of 1

Find all solutions of $z^3 = 1$, check one non-real solution directly, and find the sum of all three.

  1. $z^3 - 1 = (z-1)(z^2 + z + 1)$, so $z = 1$ or $z = \dfrac{-1 \pm i\sqrt3}{2}$.

    Difference of cubes, then the quadratic formula with discriminant $1 - 4 = -3$.

  2. Let $\omega = \dfrac{-1 + i\sqrt3}{2}$. Then $\omega^2 = \dfrac{1 - 2i\sqrt3 - 3}{4} = \dfrac{-1 - i\sqrt3}{2}$.

    Square carefully: $(i\sqrt3)^2 = -3$. Notice $\omega^2$ is the other non-real root.

  3. $\omega^3 = \omega \cdot \omega^2 = \dfrac{(-1)^2 - (i\sqrt3)^2}{4} = \dfrac{1 + 3}{4} = 1$. ✓

    The product is a difference of squares.

  4. Sum: $1 + \dfrac{-1 + i\sqrt3}{2} + \dfrac{-1 - i\sqrt3}{2} = 1 - 1 = 0$.

    The imaginary parts cancel. Vieta agrees: $z^3 + 0z^2 + 0z - 1$ has root sum $0$.

$z = 1, \dfrac{-1 \pm i\sqrt3}{2}$; their sum is $0$.

$z^n = 1$ has exactly $n$ roots, evenly spaced on the unit circle, and they sum to $0$ for $n \ge 2$.
