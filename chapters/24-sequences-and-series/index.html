Sequences and Series

A sequence is a list of numbers that follows a rule; a series is what you get when you add the list up. Adding a hundred terms one at a time is slow, but a single clever trick (write the sum twice, or multiply it and subtract) collapses the whole thing into a formula. In this chapter you'll learn those tricks, see how an infinite list can have a finite total, and meet the shorthand mathematicians use to write sums.

Each example climbs from Warm-up to Challenge. Press N for the next step, F for the full solution.
Key methods in this chapter
  • Arithmetic: $n$th term $a + (n-1)d$; sum $S = \dfrac{n}{2}\big(2a + (n-1)d\big) = \dfrac{n}{2}(\text{first} + \text{last})$. Find it by writing the sum forwards and backwards.
  • Geometric: $n$th term $ar^{n-1}$; sum $S = \dfrac{a(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}$ for $r \ne 1$. Find it by computing $S - rS$.
  • An infinite geometric series converges exactly when $|r| < 1$, and then $a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \dfrac{a}{1-r}$.
  • Terms tending to $0$ is necessary for convergence but not enough: $1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \cdots$ diverges.
  • $\displaystyle\sum_{k=m}^{n} f(k)$ adds $f(k)$ for $k = m, m+1, \ldots, n$. Sums split over $+$ and constants factor out.
  • A recursion defines each term from earlier ones; arithmetic ($a_n = a_{n-1} + d$) and geometric ($a_n = r\,a_{n-1}$) are the simplest.
  • Each term of an arithmetic sequence is the average of its neighbours; each term of a geometric sequence is their geometric mean.

24.1 Arithmetic Series

Hook

A staircase of blocks has $1$ block in the first column, $2$ in the next, and so on up to $100$. How many blocks is that? Build a second, identical staircase, flip it upside down and set it on top of the first. Every column now holds exactly $101$ blocks, and there are $100$ columns. The two staircases together hold $10{,}100$ blocks, so one staircase holds $5050$. No adding required.

Concept

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers, called terms. When we add the terms we get a series. A sequence is arithmetic when each term is the previous one plus a fixed common difference $d$. With first term $a$:

$$a,\quad a+d,\quad a+2d,\quad \ldots,\qquad n\text{th term} = a + (n-1)d.$$

The $n$th term has $n-1$ steps of size $d$ after the first term, not $n$. That off-by-one is the most common slip in this chapter.

To add the first $n$ terms, write the sum $S$ forwards and again backwards, then add the two lines column by column. Every column adds to the same thing, the first term plus the last term, and there are $n$ columns:

$$2S = n\,(\text{first} + \text{last}) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S = \frac{n}{2}\big(\text{first} + \text{last}\big) = \frac{n}{2}\big(2a + (n-1)d\big).$$

In words: the sum is the number of terms times the average of the first and last terms. A special case worth knowing by heart is $1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$.

Remember the method, not just the formula. Pairing terms from opposite ends works whenever the terms are evenly spaced.

Spot the common difference

Which of these are arithmetic sequences? (a) $7, 11, 15, 19, \ldots$ (b) $2, 4, 8, 16, \ldots$ (c) $20, 17, 14, 11, \ldots$

  1. (a) Differences: $11 - 7 = 4$, $15 - 11 = 4$, $19 - 15 = 4$. Arithmetic with $d = 4$.

    A sequence is arithmetic exactly when consecutive differences are all equal.

  2. (b) Differences: $2, 4, 8$. Not arithmetic.

    The differences grow, so the gap is not fixed. (Each term is double the last, which makes it geometric; see 24.2.)

  3. (c) Differences: $-3, -3, -3$. Arithmetic with $d = -3$.

    The common difference can be negative; the terms then decrease steadily.

(a) and (c) are arithmetic, with $d = 4$ and $d = -3$; (b) is not.

Jump straight to the 40th term

Find the $40$th term of $5, 12, 19, 26, \ldots$

  1. Here $a = 5$ and $d = 12 - 5 = 7$.

    Every arithmetic sequence is pinned down by its first term and common difference.

  2. $40$th term $= a + 39d = 5 + 39 \cdot 7 = 5 + 273 = 278$.

    From term $1$ to term $40$ there are $39$ steps, not $40$.

Sum with first and last known

Evaluate $3 + 7 + 11 + \cdots + 99$.

  1. Count the terms: $99 = 3 + (n-1)\cdot 4$ gives $n - 1 = 24$, so $n = 25$.

    Before using the sum formula we need the number of terms, found from the $n$th-term formula.

  2. $S = \dfrac{25}{2}(3 + 99) = 25 \cdot 51 = 1275$.

    Number of terms times the average of first and last.

The first $n$ positive integers

Find $1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + 200$, and give a formula for $1 + 2 + \cdots + n$.

  1. $200$ terms, first $1$, last $200$: $S = \dfrac{200}{2}(1 + 200) = 100 \cdot 201 = 20100$.

    The pairs $1 + 200$, $2 + 199$, … all equal $201$.

  2. In general: $n$ terms, first $1$, last $n$, so $1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$.

    The same pairing works for any $n$; this formula appears constantly in counting problems.

Seats in a theater

A theater's front row has $18$ seats and each row behind it has $2$ more seats than the row in front. There are $25$ rows. How many seats are there in all?

  1. Last row: $18 + 24 \cdot 2 = 66$ seats.

    Row counts form an arithmetic sequence with $a = 18$, $d = 2$; row $25$ is $24$ steps past row $1$.

  2. Total: $\dfrac{25}{2}(18 + 66) = 25 \cdot 42 = 1050$.

    The average row has $42$ seats, and there are $25$ rows.

$1050$ seats

Recover the sequence from two terms

The $4$th term of an arithmetic sequence is $17$ and the $11$th term is $52$. Find the sum of the first $20$ terms.

  1. From term $4$ to term $11$ is $7$ steps: $7d = 52 - 17 = 35$, so $d = 5$.

    The difference between two terms is (number of steps) $\times\, d$.

  2. $a = 17 - 3d = 17 - 15 = 2$.

    Term $4$ is three steps after term $1$, so walk back three steps.

  3. $S_{20} = \dfrac{20}{2}\big(2 \cdot 2 + 19 \cdot 5\big) = 10 \cdot 99 = 990$.

    With $a$ and $d$ known, the formula $\tfrac{n}{2}(2a + (n-1)d)$ finishes it.

$990$

How many terms to pass 1000?

How many terms of $5 + 9 + 13 + \cdots$ must be added before the total first exceeds $1000$?

  1. $S_n = \dfrac{n}{2}\big(10 + 4(n-1)\big) = \dfrac{n}{2}(4n + 6) = n(2n + 3)$.

    Write the sum as a function of $n$ so we can test values.

  2. We need $n(2n+3) > 1000$. Roughly $2n^2 \approx 1000$, so $n \approx 22$.

    An estimate tells us where to look.

  3. $n = 21$: $21 \cdot 45 = 945$. $n = 22$: $22 \cdot 47 = 1034$.

    Checking both sides of the estimate pins down the first $n$ that works.

$22$ terms

Subtract what you don't want

Find the sum of all integers from $1$ to $100$ that are not multiples of $3$.

  1. All of them: $1 + 2 + \cdots + 100 = \dfrac{100 \cdot 101}{2} = 5050$.

    It is easier to add everything and remove the multiples of $3$ than to add the leftovers directly.

  2. Multiples of $3$: $3 + 6 + \cdots + 99 = 3(1 + 2 + \cdots + 33) = 3 \cdot \dfrac{33 \cdot 34}{2} = 1683$.

    Factoring out $3$ turns it into a sum of the first $33$ integers.

  3. $5050 - 1683 = 3367$.

    What remains is exactly the numbers not divisible by $3$.

$3367$

A condition linking two sums

In an arithmetic sequence with $d \ne 0$, the sum of the first $8$ terms is $3$ times the sum of the first $4$ terms. Find $a/d$.

  1. $S_8 = \dfrac82(2a + 7d) = 4(2a+7d)$ and $S_4 = \dfrac42(2a + 3d) = 2(2a + 3d)$.

    Everything about an arithmetic series is captured by $a$, $d$ and $n$, so express both sums with them.

  2. $4(2a + 7d) = 3 \cdot 2(2a + 3d) \Rightarrow 8a + 28d = 12a + 18d \Rightarrow 10d = 4a$.

    Translate the condition into one linear equation.

  3. $\dfrac{a}{d} = \dfrac{10}{4} = \dfrac52$.

    We can't find $a$ and $d$ separately, but their ratio is fixed.

  4. Check with $a = 5$, $d = 2$: $S_4 = 5+7+9+11 = 32$ and $S_8 = 32 + 13+15+17+19 = 96 = 3 \cdot 32$. ✓

    A concrete example confirms the algebra.

$\dfrac{a}{d} = \dfrac{5}{2}$

Write every sum with $a$, $d$ and $n$; the problem becomes ordinary algebra.

24.2 Geometric Series

Hook

Offer someone a choice: one million dollars today, or one cent today, two cents tomorrow, four the day after, doubling for 30 days. The pennies look silly at first, but by day 30 the running total is over ten million dollars. Quantities that multiply rather than add grow astonishingly fast, and there is a slick trick for totalling them.

Concept

A sequence is geometric when each term is the previous one times a fixed common ratio $r$:

$$a,\quad ar,\quad ar^2,\quad \ldots,\qquad n\text{th term} = ar^{n-1}.$$

To add the first $n$ terms, call the sum $S$ and multiply it by $r$. The list $rS$ is the same list shifted over by one place, so when we subtract, everything cancels except one term at each end:

$$\begin{aligned} S &= a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots + ar^{n-1} \\ rS &= \phantom{a + {}} ar + ar^2 + \cdots + ar^{n-1} + ar^n \\ S - rS &= a - ar^n \end{aligned} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad S = \frac{a(1 - r^n)}{1 - r}\quad (r \neq 1).$$

When $r > 1$ it is often tidier to write $S = \dfrac{a(r^n - 1)}{r - 1}$; it is the same fraction with top and bottom negated. If $r = 1$ every term is $a$ and $S = na$.

The multiply-and-subtract idea is more useful than the formula. It works on any sum where multiplying by something shifts the terms into near-copies of themselves.

The 10th term

Find the $10$th term of $3, 6, 12, 24, \ldots$

  1. $a = 3$ and $r = 6/3 = 2$.

    The ratio of consecutive terms is constant, so divide any term by the one before it.

  2. $10$th term $= ar^9 = 3 \cdot 2^9 = 3 \cdot 512 = 1536$.

    Nine multiplications by $r$ take us from term $1$ to term $10$.

$1536$

A shrinking ratio

Find the $7$th term of $81, 27, 9, \ldots$

  1. $r = \dfrac{27}{81} = \dfrac13$.

    A ratio between $0$ and $1$ makes the terms shrink.

  2. $7$th term $= 81\left(\dfrac13\right)^6 = \dfrac{81}{729} = \dfrac19$.

    $3^6 = 729$ and $81 = 3^4$, so the result is $3^{4-6} = 3^{-2}$.

$\dfrac{1}{9}$

Sum of powers of 3

Evaluate $1 + 3 + 9 + \cdots + 3^7$.

  1. $a = 1$, $r = 3$, and the exponents run $0$ to $7$, so $n = 8$ terms.

    Count terms by their exponents: $0, 1, \ldots, 7$ is eight values.

  2. $S = \dfrac{1\cdot(3^8 - 1)}{3 - 1} = \dfrac{6561 - 1}{2} = 3280$.

    Since $r > 1$, use the form $\dfrac{a(r^n - 1)}{r-1}$ to keep everything positive.

$3280$

A negative ratio

Evaluate $5 - 10 + 20 - 40 + \cdots$ to $8$ terms.

  1. $a = 5$, $r = -2$, $n = 8$.

    Alternating signs mean a negative ratio.

  2. $S = \dfrac{5\big(1 - (-2)^8\big)}{1 - (-2)} = \dfrac{5(1 - 256)}{3} = \dfrac{-1275}{3} = -425$.

    $(-2)^8 = 256$ because the exponent is even. Keep the parentheses around $-2$.

$-425$

The doubling pennies

You receive $1$ cent on day $1$, $2$ cents on day $2$, $4$ cents on day $3$, doubling each day. How much have you received after $30$ days?

  1. Total $= 1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots + 2^{29}$ cents: $a = 1$, $r = 2$, $n = 30$.

    Day $k$ brings $2^{k-1}$ cents, so day $30$ brings $2^{29}$.

  2. $S = \dfrac{2^{30} - 1}{2 - 1} = 2^{30} - 1 = 1{,}073{,}741{,}823$ cents.

    With $r = 2$ the denominator is $1$: the sum of the first powers of $2$ is one less than the next power.

  3. That is about $10.7$ million dollars.

    Divide by $100$ to convert cents to dollars.

$2^{30} - 1 = 1{,}073{,}741{,}823$ cents, about 10.7 million dollars.

Find the ratio first

A geometric sequence has $2$nd term $6$ and $5$th term $162$. Find the sum of its first $6$ terms.

  1. $\dfrac{162}{6} = r^3$, so $r^3 = 27$ and $r = 3$.

    Dividing terms cancels $a$ and leaves a power of $r$: three steps means $r^3$.

  2. $a = 6/3 = 2$.

    Step back once from term $2$.

  3. $S_6 = \dfrac{2(3^6 - 1)}{3 - 1} = 3^6 - 1 = 728$.

    The $2$ on top cancels the $2$ on the bottom.

$728$

Factoring $x^n - 1$

Use the geometric series formula to show that $2^{12} - 1$ is divisible by both $7$ and $15$.

  1. Read the formula backwards with $a = 1$: $1 + x + x^2 + \cdots + x^{k-1} = \dfrac{x^k - 1}{x - 1}$, so $x^k - 1 = (x - 1)(x^{k-1} + \cdots + x + 1)$.

    This turns the sum formula into a factorization.

  2. $2^{12} = 8^4$, so $2^{12} - 1 = (8 - 1)(8^3 + 8^2 + 8 + 1)$, a multiple of $7$.

    Choosing $x = 8$, $k = 4$ exposes the factor $x - 1 = 7$.

  3. $2^{12} = 16^3$, so $2^{12} - 1 = (16 - 1)(16^2 + 16 + 1)$, a multiple of $15$.

    A different grouping, $x = 16$, $k = 3$, exposes $15$.

  4. Check: $2^{12} - 1 = 4095 = 7 \cdot 585 = 15 \cdot 273$. ✓

    Direct computation agrees.

$4095 = 7 \cdot 585 = 15 \cdot 273$, so both divide $2^{12} - 1$.

Multiply and subtract on a new sum

Evaluate $S = 1 + 2\cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 2^2 + 4 \cdot 2^3 + \cdots + 10 \cdot 2^9$.

  1. $2S = 1\cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 2^2 + \cdots + 9 \cdot 2^9 + 10 \cdot 2^{10}$.

    Multiplying by the ratio of the powers shifts each power one place, just as in the geometric case.

  2. Subtract, lining up equal powers: $S - 2S = 1 + (2 - 1)2 + (3-2)2^2 + \cdots + (10 - 9)2^9 - 10 \cdot 2^{10}$.

    Each matched pair of terms leaves exactly one copy of that power of $2$.

  3. So $-S = (1 + 2 + \cdots + 2^9) - 10\cdot 2^{10} = 1023 - 10240 = -9217$.

    What remains is a plain geometric series, worth $2^{10} - 1 = 1023$.

  4. $S = 9217$.

    Multiply both sides by $-1$.

$9217$

Multiply-and-subtract turns "number times power" sums into geometric series.

24.3 Infinite Series

Hook

Walk halfway to a wall. Then half the remaining distance, then half of what's left, forever. You take infinitely many steps, yet you never travel more than the original distance. If the wall was $1$ metre away, your steps $\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac18 + \cdots$ add up to exactly $1$. An infinite sum can have a perfectly finite answer.

Concept

For an infinite series, look at the partial sums $S_1, S_2, S_3, \ldots$, the totals of the first $1, 2, 3, \ldots$ terms. If they settle down toward a fixed number, the series converges and that number is its sum. Otherwise it diverges.

The terms must shrink to $0$ for a series to converge, so $1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots$ and $1 - 2 + 3 - 4 + \cdots$ diverge. But shrinking terms are not enough. The harmonic series $1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac14 + \cdots$ diverges, because its terms can be grouped into blocks each worth at least $\tfrac12$:

$$1 + \tfrac12 + \underbrace{\left(\tfrac13 + \tfrac14\right)}_{\ge \frac12} + \underbrace{\left(\tfrac15 + \cdots + \tfrac18\right)}_{\ge \frac12} + \underbrace{\left(\tfrac19 + \cdots + \tfrac1{16}\right)}_{\ge \frac12} + \cdots$$

For a geometric series, the partial sum is $S_n = \dfrac{a}{1-r}\,(1 - r^n)$. If $|r| < 1$, then $r^n \to 0$ and

$$a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots = \frac{a}{1 - r} \qquad (|r| < 1).$$

If $|r| \ge 1$ (and $a \ne 0$) the terms do not shrink and the series diverges.

Thirds forever

Evaluate $1 + \dfrac13 + \dfrac19 + \dfrac1{27} + \cdots$

  1. $a = 1$, $r = \dfrac13$, and $|r| < 1$ so the series converges.

    Always check $|r| < 1$ before using the infinite-sum formula.

  2. $S = \dfrac{1}{1 - \frac13} = \dfrac{1}{\frac23} = \dfrac32$.

    First term over one minus the ratio.

$\dfrac{3}{2}$

Alternating signs

Evaluate $8 - 4 + 2 - 1 + \cdots$

  1. $a = 8$, $r = -\dfrac12$; $|r| = \dfrac12 < 1$.

    A negative ratio is fine as long as its absolute value is below $1$.

  2. $S = \dfrac{8}{1 - (-\frac12)} = \dfrac{8}{\frac32} = \dfrac{16}{3}$.

    Subtracting a negative ratio makes the denominator larger than $1$.

$\dfrac{16}{3}$

Repeating decimals are geometric series

Write $0.\overline{9} = 0.999\ldots$ and $0.\overline{27} = 0.272727\ldots$ as fractions.

  1. $0.999\ldots = \dfrac{9}{10} + \dfrac{9}{100} + \cdots = \dfrac{9/10}{1 - 1/10} = 1$.

    Each digit is worth one tenth of the one before, so $r = \tfrac1{10}$.

  2. $0.2727\ldots = \dfrac{27}{100} + \dfrac{27}{100^2} + \cdots = \dfrac{27/100}{1 - 1/100} = \dfrac{27}{99} = \dfrac{3}{11}$.

    A two-digit block repeats, so the ratio is $\tfrac{1}{100}$.

$0.\overline{9} = 1$ and $0.\overline{27} = \dfrac{3}{11}$.

A bouncing ball

A ball is dropped from $10$ m. Each bounce takes it back up to $\tfrac35$ of its previous height. What total distance does it travel?

  1. First drop: $10$ m. After that, each rebound height is travelled twice, up and down.

    Splitting the motion this way avoids double-counting the first drop.

  2. Rebound heights: $6 + 3.6 + 2.16 + \cdots = \dfrac{6}{1 - \frac35} = 15$ m.

    A geometric series with $a = 6$ and $r = \tfrac35$.

  3. Total $= 10 + 2 \cdot 15 = 40$ m.

    Each rebound height counts once going up and once coming down.

$40$ m

Solve for the ratio

For what value of $x$ does $1 + 2x + 4x^2 + 8x^3 + \cdots = 3$?

  1. Ratio $r = 2x$, so the sum is $\dfrac{1}{1 - 2x}$ when $|2x| < 1$.

    Each term is the previous one times $2x$.

  2. $\dfrac{1}{1 - 2x} = 3 \Rightarrow 1 - 2x = \dfrac13 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac13$.

    Clear the fraction and solve the linear equation.

  3. Check: $|2x| = \dfrac23 < 1$. ✓

    The formula is only valid when the series converges, so the condition must be confirmed.

$x = \dfrac{1}{3}$

Split into two geometric series

Evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2^n - 1}{4^n}$, that is, $\dfrac{1}{4} + \dfrac{3}{16} + \dfrac{7}{64} + \cdots$

  1. $\dfrac{2^n - 1}{4^n} = \left(\dfrac12\right)^n - \left(\dfrac14\right)^n$.

    Splitting the numerator separates two geometric patterns.

  2. $\dfrac12 + \dfrac14 + \cdots = \dfrac{1/2}{1 - 1/2} = 1$ and $\dfrac14 + \dfrac1{16} + \cdots = \dfrac{1/4}{1 - 1/4} = \dfrac13$.

    Both converge since their ratios are below $1$, so we may sum them separately.

  3. Total $= 1 - \dfrac13 = \dfrac23$.

    Subtract the two convergent sums.

$\dfrac{2}{3}$

The harmonic series really grows

Show that $1 + \dfrac12 + \dfrac13 + \cdots + \dfrac1{16} \ge 3$, and explain why the harmonic series passes any number eventually.

  1. Group: $1 + \dfrac12 + \left(\dfrac13 + \dfrac14\right) + \left(\dfrac15 + \cdots + \dfrac18\right) + \left(\dfrac19 + \cdots + \dfrac1{16}\right)$.

    Each block ends at a power of $2$ and has as many terms as its last denominator's half.

  2. In each block every term is at least the last one, so each block is at least $2 \cdot \tfrac14$, $4 \cdot \tfrac18$, $8 \cdot \tfrac1{16}$, all equal to $\tfrac12$.

    Replacing terms by smaller ones can only lower the total, giving a lower bound.

  3. So the sum is at least $1 + 4 \cdot \tfrac12 = 3$. (Its actual value is about $3.38$.)

    There are four half-sized pieces after the leading $1$.

  4. Going up to $\tfrac1{2^k}$ gives at least $1 + \tfrac{k}{2}$, which exceeds any number once $k$ is large.

    Each doubling of the number of terms adds at least another $\tfrac12$, so the partial sums are unbounded.

The sum is at least $3$; in general the first $2^k$ terms total at least $1 + k/2$, so the series diverges.

Numbers times a shrinking power

Evaluate $S = 1 + \dfrac23 + \dfrac39 + \dfrac4{27} + \cdots$, where the $n$th term is $\dfrac{n}{3^{n-1}}$.

  1. $\dfrac13 S = \dfrac13 + \dfrac29 + \dfrac3{27} + \cdots$

    Multiplying by $\tfrac13$ shifts every power of $3$ along by one place.

  2. $S - \dfrac13 S = 1 + \dfrac13 + \dfrac19 + \dfrac1{27} + \cdots$

    Lining up equal powers, each difference of numerators is $1$, for example $\tfrac29 - \tfrac19 = \tfrac19$.

  3. The right side is $\dfrac{1}{1 - \frac13} = \dfrac32$, so $\dfrac23 S = \dfrac32$ and $S = \dfrac94$.

    A geometric series appears, which we can sum.

$\dfrac{9}{4}$

The same shift-and-subtract trick works for infinite sums, as long as they converge.

24.4 Sigma Notation: $\sum$

Hook

"Add up the squares of the whole numbers from one to fifty" takes a sentence to say, and writing all fifty terms takes even longer. Mathematicians write it in one compact symbol: $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{50} k^2$. Once you can read and write this shorthand, long sums become easy to handle.

Concept

The Greek capital sigma, $\Sigma$ (an S, for "sum"), means "add up":

$$\sum_{k=m}^{n} f(k) = f(m) + f(m+1) + f(m+2) + \cdots + f(n).$$

The letter $k$ is a dummy variable (or index): it takes each whole-number value from the lower limit $m$ to the upper limit $n$, and it vanishes from the final answer. Letters $i$, $j$, $k$ are common, and the choice doesn't matter. The number of terms is $n - m + 1$. An infinite sum uses $\infty$ as the upper limit: $\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} ar^i = a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots$

Two rules follow straight from the ordinary properties of addition and multiplication:

$$\sum (a_k + b_k) = \sum a_k + \sum b_k, \qquad \sum c\,a_k = c \sum a_k.$$

Products have a matching shorthand with capital pi: $\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{4}(x - k) = (x-1)(x-2)(x-3)(x-4)$.

Writing a sum in $\Sigma$ form forces you to find a formula for the general term. If you can't, you may not fully understand the pattern yet.

Expand and add

Evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{4} k^2$.

  1. Substitute $k = 1, 2, 3, 4$: $1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 + 4^2$.

    The index takes each whole value from the bottom limit to the top.

  2. $1 + 4 + 9 + 16 = 30$.

    Then just add.

$30$

Starting at zero

Evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{3} (2j + 1)$.

  1. $j = 0, 1, 2, 3$ give $1, 3, 5, 7$.

    The sum starts at $j = 0$, so there are $3 - 0 + 1 = 4$ terms.

  2. $1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16$.

    These are the first four odd numbers, and their sum is $4^2$.

$16$

Signs that alternate

Write $1 - 2 + 4 - 8 + 16 - 32$ in $\Sigma$ notation and evaluate it.

  1. The terms are $(-2)^0, (-2)^1, \ldots, (-2)^5$, so the sum is $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{5} (-2)^k$.

    Six terms because $k$ runs from $0$ to $5$.

$\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{5}(-2)^k = -21$

Split and factor

Evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20} (4k - 3)$ using the sum rules.

  1. $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20}(4k - 3) = 4\sum_{k=1}^{20} k - \sum_{k=1}^{20} 3$.

    Sums split over addition, and constant factors come out front.

  2. $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20} k = \frac{20 \cdot 21}{2} = 210$ and $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{20} 3 = 20 \cdot 3 = 60$.

    Adding the constant $3$ twenty times gives $60$, not $3$.

  3. $4 \cdot 210 - 60 = 780$.

    Combine the pieces.

$780$

Shift the index

Rewrite $\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^{12} k$ as a sum starting at $1$, and evaluate it.

  1. Let $j = k - 2$. When $k = 3$, $j = 1$; when $k = 12$, $j = 10$. So $\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^{12} k = \sum_{j=1}^{10} (j + 2)$.

    Substituting $k = j + 2$ in the term and in both limits keeps every term the same.

  2. $\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{10} j + \sum_{j=1}^{10} 2 = 55 + 20 = 75$.

    Now the standard formula $\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$ applies.

$\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{10}(j+2) = 75$

A product that telescopes

Evaluate $\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{5}\left(1 + \frac1k\right)$.

  1. $1 + \dfrac1k = \dfrac{k+1}{k}$, so the product is $\dfrac21 \cdot \dfrac32 \cdot \dfrac43 \cdot \dfrac54 \cdot \dfrac65$.

    Rewriting each factor as a single fraction shows the pattern.

  2. Each numerator cancels the next denominator, leaving $\dfrac{6}{1} = 6$.

    This collapse is called telescoping.

$6$

Next in the book: Example 24-6.

A double sum

Evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}\sum_{j=1}^{4} ij$.

  1. Inner sum, $i$ fixed: $\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{4} ij = i(1 + 2 + 3 + 4) = 10i$.

    Work from the inside out; $i$ is a constant while $j$ varies, so it factors out.

  2. Outer sum: $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{4} 10i = 10 \cdot 10 = 100$.

    The same factoring again.

  3. In general, $\displaystyle\sum_{i}\sum_{j} ij = \Big(\sum_i i\Big)\Big(\sum_j j\Big)$.

    Expanding $(1 + 2 + 3 + 4)(1 + 2 + 3 + 4)$ produces every product $ij$ exactly once.

$100$

Next in the book: Exercises 24-12 and 24-13.

A telescoping sum

Evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{99}\frac{1}{k(k+1)}$.

  1. $\dfrac{1}{k(k+1)} = \dfrac1k - \dfrac1{k+1}$.

    Check: $\dfrac1k - \dfrac1{k+1} = \dfrac{(k+1) - k}{k(k+1)}$. Writing terms as differences is what makes telescoping possible.

  2. The sum is $\left(1 - \tfrac12\right) + \left(\tfrac12 - \tfrac13\right) + \cdots + \left(\tfrac1{99} - \tfrac1{100}\right)$.

    Write out the first few and last few terms to see what cancels.

  3. Everything cancels except $1 - \dfrac1{100} = \dfrac{99}{100}$.

    Each $-\tfrac1{k+1}$ cancels the $+\tfrac1{k+1}$ of the next term.

$\dfrac{99}{100}$

If each term is a difference $g(k) - g(k+1)$, the sum collapses to first minus last.

24.5 Sequences

Hook

You can climb a staircase one step or two steps at a time. How many different ways are there to climb $10$ steps? Listing them is hopeless. But your first move is either a single step (leaving $9$) or a double (leaving $8$), so the answer for $10$ is the answer for $9$ plus the answer for $8$. A rule that builds each term from earlier ones can crack problems that a list never could.

Concept

A sequence is just a list of terms, finite or infinite. A compact way to name one is to write its $n$th term in braces: $\{2n\}$ is $2, 4, 6, 8, \ldots$ and $\{(-1)^{n+1}/n\}$ is $1, -\tfrac12, \tfrac13, -\tfrac14, \ldots$, with $n$ starting at $1$.

Some sequences are easier to describe recursively: give the first term or terms, then a rule that produces each term from the ones before it.

$$\text{arithmetic: } a_n = a_{n-1} + d \qquad \text{geometric: } a_n = r\,a_{n-1} \qquad \text{Fibonacci: } F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2}.$$

For the Fibonacci sequence we start with $F_0 = F_1 = 1$ (the book's convention), giving $1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, \ldots$. A recursion is one of infinitely many possible rules; small changes in the rule or the starting values can produce growth, steady steps, or even cycles.

A formula for the $n$th term (an explicit or closed form) lets you jump straight to term $1000$. A recursion makes you walk there, but it is often much easier to find.

From formula to terms

Write the first five terms of $\{n^2 - 1\}$.

  1. $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ give $0, 3, 8, 15, 24$.

    The braces notation means: substitute $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ in turn.

$0, 3, 8, 15, 24$

Next in the book: Exercise 24-15.

From terms to formula

Write in brace notation: (a) $3, 6, 9, 12, \ldots$ (b) $1, -\tfrac12, \tfrac13, -\tfrac14, \ldots$

  1. (a) The $n$th term is $3n$: $\{3n\}$.

    Term $n$ is $n$ times $3$.

  2. (b) Size $\tfrac1n$, sign $+$ when $n$ is odd: $\left\{\dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\right\}$.

    $(-1)^{n+1}$ is $+1$ for odd $n$ and $-1$ for even $n$, a standard way to alternate signs.

$\{3n\}$ and $\left\{\dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\right\}$

Next in the book: Exercise 24-14.

Run a recursion

A sequence has $a_1 = 2$ and $a_n = 3a_{n-1} - 1$ for $n \ge 2$. Find $a_5$.

  1. $a_2 = 3 \cdot 2 - 1 = 5$, $a_3 = 3 \cdot 5 - 1 = 14$.

    A recursion is applied one term at a time, each from the previous one.

  2. $a_4 = 3 \cdot 14 - 1 = 41$, $a_5 = 3 \cdot 41 - 1 = 122$.

    Keep going until the term you want.

$a_5 = 122$

Fibonacci by hand

With $F_0 = F_1 = 1$ and $F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2}$, find $F_{10}$.

  1. $F_2 = 2,\ F_3 = 3,\ F_4 = 5,\ F_5 = 8,\ F_6 = 13$.

    Each term is the sum of the two before it.

  2. $F_7 = 21,\ F_8 = 34,\ F_9 = 55,\ F_{10} = 89$.

    Watch the indexing: with this convention $F_{10}$ is the eleventh term in the list.

$F_{10} = 89$

Next in the book: Exercise 24-16.

Recursive to explicit

A sequence has $a_1 = 7$ and $a_n = a_{n-1} - 4$. Find a formula for $a_n$ and the value of $a_{20}$.

  1. Subtracting $4$ each time makes it arithmetic with $a = 7$, $d = -4$.

    The recursion $a_n = a_{n-1} + d$ is exactly what "arithmetic" means.

  2. $a_n = 7 + (n-1)(-4) = 11 - 4n$, so $a_{20} = 11 - 80 = -69$.

    The explicit form jumps straight to term $20$.

$a_n = 11 - 4n$; $a_{20} = -69$

Next in the book: Exercise 24-17.

Guess and justify a closed form

$a_1 = 1$ and $a_n = a_{n-1} + (2n - 1)$. Find $a_n$.

  1. Compute: $a_1 = 1,\ a_2 = 4,\ a_3 = 9,\ a_4 = 16$. Guess $a_n = n^2$.

    A few terms usually reveal the pattern.

  2. Unrolling the recursion: $a_n = 1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n - 1)$.

    Each step adds the next odd number, so $a_n$ is the sum of the first $n$ odd numbers.

  3. That arithmetic series is $\dfrac{n}{2}\big(1 + (2n - 1)\big) = n^2$. ✓

    The sum formula proves the guess.

$a_n = n^2$

A sequence that cycles

$a_1 = 2$ and $a_n = \dfrac{1}{1 - a_{n-1}}$. Find $a_{100}$.

  1. $a_2 = \dfrac{1}{1 - 2} = -1$, $a_3 = \dfrac{1}{1 - (-1)} = \dfrac12$, $a_4 = \dfrac{1}{1 - \frac12} = 2$.

    When a term repeats, the recursion must repeat everything after it too.

  2. The sequence is $2, -1, \tfrac12, 2, -1, \tfrac12, \ldots$ with period $3$; $a_n = 2$ whenever $n$ leaves remainder $1$ on division by $3$.

    Terms $1, 4, 7, \ldots$ are all $2$.

  3. $100 = 3 \cdot 33 + 1$, so $a_{100} = 2$.

    Reduce the index modulo the period.

$a_{100} = 2$

A Fibonacci identity by telescoping

With $F_0 = F_1 = 1$, show that $F_0 + F_2 + F_4 + \cdots + F_{2n} = F_{2n+1}$.

  1. Test: $F_0 + F_2 = 1 + 2 = 3 = F_3$ and $1 + 2 + 5 = 8 = F_5$.

    Checking small cases builds confidence before proving.

  2. For $k \ge 1$: $F_{2k} = F_{2k+1} - F_{2k-1}$.

    Writing each term as a difference sets up a telescoping sum.

  3. $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} F_{2k} = (F_3 - F_1) + (F_5 - F_3) + \cdots + (F_{2n+1} - F_{2n-1}) = F_{2n+1} - F_1$.

    Every odd-indexed term except the first and last cancels.

  4. Add $F_0$: the total is $F_{2n+1} - F_1 + F_0 = F_{2n+1}$, since $F_0 = F_1$.

    The leftover pieces cancel exactly because both starting values are $1$.

$F_0 + F_2 + \cdots + F_{2n} = F_{2n+1}$ for every $n \ge 0$.

Rearrange the recursion to write a term as a difference, then telescope.

Compare with Example 24-7, which proves a related identity by induction.

Climbing stairs

In how many ways can you climb $10$ stairs taking $1$ or $2$ steps at a time?

  1. Let $c_n$ be the number of ways for $n$ stairs. The first move is $1$ step (then $c_{n-1}$ ways) or $2$ steps (then $c_{n-2}$ ways), so $c_n = c_{n-1} + c_{n-2}$.

    Splitting by the first move covers every climb exactly once.

  2. $c_1 = 1$ and $c_2 = 2$ (either $1 + 1$ or $2$).

    The recursion needs two starting values.

  3. $c_3 = 3,\ c_4 = 5,\ c_5 = 8,\ c_6 = 13,\ c_7 = 21,\ c_8 = 34,\ c_9 = 55,\ c_{10} = 89$.

    This is the Fibonacci sequence again: $c_n = F_n$ with the book's indexing.

$89$ ways

Split by the first move to find a recursion; counting problems often hide Fibonacci numbers.

24.6 Sequences and Means

Hook

Why are they called arithmetic and geometric sequences? Pick any term of $3, 7, 11, 15, 19$, say $11$. Its neighbours $7$ and $15$ average to exactly $11$. Now pick $12$ in $3, 6, 12, 24, 48$: its neighbours multiply to $144$, and $\sqrt{144} = 12$. Each kind of sequence is built from its own kind of average.

Concept

The arithmetic mean of $x$ and $y$ is $\dfrac{x + y}{2}$; the geometric mean of positive $x$ and $y$ is $\sqrt{xy}$. In an arithmetic sequence the neighbours of a term are $t - d$ and $t + d$, and in a geometric one they are $t/r$ and $tr$, so

$$\text{arithmetic: } a_n = \frac{a_{n-1} + a_{n+1}}{2}, \qquad \text{geometric: } a_n^2 = a_{n-1}\,a_{n+1}.$$

This gives the older phrase "insert $k$ means between $A$ and $B$": find $k$ numbers so that $A$, the $k$ numbers, and $B$ form an arithmetic (or geometric) sequence. There are $k + 1$ gaps between $A$ and $B$, so

$$d = \frac{B - A}{k + 1}, \qquad r^{k+1} = \frac{B}{A}.$$

For positive numbers the arithmetic mean is never smaller than the geometric mean, because $\dfrac{x + y}{2} - \sqrt{xy} = \dfrac{(\sqrt x - \sqrt y)^2}{2} \ge 0$, with equality only when $x = y$.

The missing middle term

In an arithmetic sequence the $5$th term is $14$ and the $7$th is $26$. Find the $6$th.

  1. $a_6 = \dfrac{a_5 + a_7}{2} = \dfrac{14 + 26}{2} = 20$.

    Each term of an arithmetic sequence is the average of its neighbours.

$20$

A geometric middle

Find a positive number $g$ so that $4, g, 25$ is a geometric sequence.

  1. $g^2 = 4 \cdot 25 = 100$, so $g = 10$.

    The middle term of a geometric sequence is the geometric mean of its neighbours.

  2. Check: $10/4 = 25/10 = \tfrac52$. ✓

    The ratios agree, so the sequence is geometric.

$g = 10$

Insert arithmetic means

Insert four arithmetic means between $7$ and $22$.

  1. Six numbers in all means $5$ equal gaps: $d = \dfrac{22 - 7}{5} = 3$.

    $k$ inserted terms create $k + 1$ gaps.

  2. $7, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22$.

    Add $3$ repeatedly; landing on $22$ confirms it.

$10, 13, 16, 19$

Insert geometric means

Insert three real geometric means between $2$ and $162$.

  1. Four gaps: $2r^4 = 162$, so $r^4 = 81$ and $r = 3$ or $r = -3$.

    An even power has two real roots, one positive and one negative.

  2. $r = 3$: $2, 6, 18, 54, 162$. $r = -3$: $2, -6, 18, -54, 162$.

    Both lists are geometric and start and end correctly.

Make three expressions arithmetic

Find $x$ so that $x + 1,\ 3x,\ 4x + 2$ is an arithmetic sequence.

  1. The middle term is the average: $2(3x) = (x + 1) + (4x + 2)$.

    Using the mean property gives one equation directly.

  2. $6x = 5x + 3$, so $x = 3$. The terms are $4, 9, 14$. ✓

    Checking shows a common difference of $5$.

$x = 3$

Make three expressions geometric

Find $x$ so that $x,\ x + 6,\ x + 18$ is a geometric sequence.

  1. $(x + 6)^2 = x(x + 18)$.

    The square of the middle term equals the product of its neighbours.

  2. $x^2 + 12x + 36 = x^2 + 18x$, so $6x = 36$ and $x = 6$.

    The $x^2$ terms cancel, leaving a linear equation.

  3. Terms: $6, 12, 24$, with ratio $2$. ✓

    Always confirm the ratio is constant.

$x = 6$

Arithmetic mean versus geometric mean

A rectangle has perimeter $40$. What is the largest possible area?

  1. Sides $x$ and $y$ with $x + y = 20$; area $= xy$.

    Half the perimeter is the sum of two adjacent sides.

  2. $\sqrt{xy} \le \dfrac{x + y}{2} = 10$, so $xy \le 100$.

    The geometric mean never exceeds the arithmetic mean.

  3. Equality holds when $x = y = 10$: a $10 \times 10$ square.

    The two means are equal exactly when the numbers are equal, so $100$ is achieved.

$100$, for a $10 \times 10$ square.

From arithmetic to geometric

Three numbers in arithmetic progression add to $15$. If $1$, $1$ and $4$ are added to them respectively, the results form a geometric progression. Find the original numbers.

  1. Call them $5 - d,\ 5,\ 5 + d$.

    Centring on the middle term makes the sum $3 \cdot (\text{middle}) = 15$, so the middle term is $5$.

  2. New numbers: $6 - d,\ 6,\ 9 + d$. Geometric means $6^2 = (6 - d)(9 + d)$.

    Square of the middle term equals product of the outer terms.

  3. $36 = 54 - 3d - d^2 \Rightarrow d^2 + 3d - 18 = 0 \Rightarrow (d + 6)(d - 3) = 0$.

    Expand and collect into a quadratic.

  4. $d = 3$: $2, 5, 8 \to 3, 6, 12$ (ratio $2$). $d = -6$: $11, 5, -1 \to 12, 6, 3$ (ratio $\tfrac12$).

    Both roots give valid answers, so check each.

$2, 5, 8$ or $11, 5, -1$

Name arithmetic terms symmetrically as $m - d, m, m + d$; then use the mean property.
